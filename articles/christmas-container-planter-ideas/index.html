30 Christmas Container Planter Ideas

Container GardeningUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Christmas planters turn a bare porch into the best part of the house. These 30 ideas cover front-door urns, balcony troughs, window boxes and small indoor pots, with living plants and cut greens side by side — evergreens, poinsettia, hellebore, winter heather, violas, Christmas cactus and rosemary trees, plus birch logs, red berries, pinecones and big bows. Each idea tells you which container survives winter, what potting mix and compost to use, how much sunlight and watering it needs, and how to keep the drainage clear so frost cannot crack the pot. Build a planted pot in October or November, or a cut-green pot in late December, and after Christmas take the ornaments out and keep the greens — the same planter becomes a winter planter until February.

1

Classic Evergreen Urn with Red Berries

Idea 1 of 30

Fill a frost-proof urn with mixed evergreen boughs — fir, spruce and cedar — then tuck in red berries and pinecones for colour. A tall spruce tip in the middle gives height, and the greens hide the soil completely. Water once when you build it, then only while the ground stays unfrozen, and keep the drainage hole clear so water never sits and freezes in the potting mix.

2

Thriller Filler Spiller Christmas Pot

Idea 2 of 30

Use the thriller, filler, spiller formula: a tall dwarf evergreen in the centre, mid-height hellebores or violas around it, and trailing ivy spilling over the rim. The three layers make even a small pot look professionally planted. Choose plants that share the same sunlight and watering needs, and use a light, compost-rich potting mix that drains freely.

3

Dwarf Alberta Spruce Potted Christmas Tree

Idea 3 of 30

A dwarf Alberta spruce in a pot is a living Christmas tree for a porch, balcony or small room. Pick a frost-proof container, keep the root ball moist but never waterlogged, and use warm-white outdoor LED lights — they run cool, so they will not scorch the needles. After Christmas, plant it out or keep it in a sheltered spot until spring.

4

Rosemary Christmas Tree in a Pot

Idea 4 of 30

Trim an upright rosemary plant into a cone shape and you have a Christmas tree that smells wonderful and can be cooked with afterwards. Rosemary needs full sunlight, a pot that drains freely, and very little water — it hates sitting wet. Decorate with tiny LED lights and a ribbon, then keep it by a bright window and move it outside once the frosts pass.

5

Poinsettia in a Frost-Proof Pot Indoors

Idea 5 of 30

Poinsettia is the classic indoor Christmas plant, but it is cold-sensitive and belongs inside in a bright spot, away from draughts. Remove the decorative foil before watering, let excess water drain away, and never leave it standing in water. Keep it out of the cold porch — one frosty night is enough to damage it.

6

Christmas Rose Hellebore in a Sheltered Pot

Idea 6 of 30

The Christmas rose, Helleborus niger, opens flat white flowers from midwinter, and in a mild December the first blooms appear before Christmas. Plant it in a deep pot — 30 to 60 cm — in a sheltered, shady spot, soak the roots first, and water about once a week to begin with. It is one of the few plants that genuinely flowers at Christmas.

7

Winter Heather and Violas Pot

Idea 7 of 30

Winter-flowering heather, Erica carnea, flowers from December into May, and violas flower through most winters in pots. Together they give low, neat colour when most other plants are dormant. Plant them in free-draining potting mix with compost, give them sunlight, and deadhead the violas to keep the flowers coming.

8

Christmas Cactus on a Sunny Windowsill

Idea 8 of 30

Christmas cactus blooms for four to six weeks near the holidays, and it is one of the easiest indoor plants to keep. Give it bright, indirect sunlight, water only when the top of the soil feels dry, and keep it away from radiators. After flowering, let it rest in a cool spot and it will bloom again next December.

9

Red Twig Dogwood and Birch Log Planter

Idea 9 of 30

Push red twig dogwood stems into the middle of a pot for height and fiery red bark that stands out against snow, then add birch logs and evergreen boughs around them. This is one of the most-pinned Christmas planter looks, and the stems last for months. Use a tall, stable container so the arrangement cannot blow over.

10

Winterberry and Pinecone Urn

Idea 10 of 30

Winterberry, cotoneaster and chokeberry all carry glossy red fruit through winter, and they look striking in a dark urn against green evergreens. Add pinecones for texture and a few curly willow stems for height. Cut stems last for weeks outside, and the berries feed birds once the display is finished.

11

Lantern in an Evergreen Planter

Idea 11 of 30

Tuck a lantern into an evergreen planter and the warm light does the pretty work after dark. Fill the pot with pine boughs and pinecones first, set the lantern in the middle, and use a battery or solar candle so there is no trailing wire. It is a ten-minute idea that makes the whole front door feel welcoming.

12

Galvanised Bucket with a Small Tree

Idea 12 of 30

A small tree in a galvanised bucket is the relaxed farmhouse Christmas look: plaid bows, a wooden star and no fuss. Line the bucket with a plastic nursery pot so the tree can drain, add a little potting mix, and keep the bucket out of standing water. The metal will not crack in frost, which makes it a safe winter container.

13

Christmas Window Box with Evergreens

Idea 13 of 30

Fill a window box with fir and spruce boughs, red berries and pinecones, and let a little ivy trail over the front. A window box is the easiest Christmas planter for a flat or a small house — no porch needed. Fix it securely, keep the drainage holes clear, and water lightly so the mix settles around the stems.

14

Woven Basket with a Small Tree

Idea 14 of 30

Put a small potted tree inside a woven basket and the whole display feels soft and handmade. Keep the tree in its nursery pot inside the basket so you can lift it out to water it, and line the basket if it will sit outside in rain. Add a ribbon and a few pinecones, and the basket can be reused for logs after Christmas.

15

Layered Planters on Porch Steps

Idea 15 of 30

Group three planters of different heights on the porch steps — tall at the back, low at the front — and repeat the same evergreens and berries in each one. Repeating the plants ties the group together, and the steps do the work of giving height. Leave the middle of each step clear so nobody trips.

16

Topiary Pair Framing the Front Door

Idea 16 of 30

A matched pair of topiary planters, one on each side of the front door, is the most formal Christmas look and also the simplest. Use boxwood or a small conifer in each pot, add a red bow, and keep both planters the same height. Boxwood keeps its shape all winter, and the pair can stay out long after the decorations come down.

17

White Stars and Bare Branches Pot

Idea 17 of 30

White stars and bare branches give a modern, Scandinavian Christmas planter that does not rely on red at all. Use curly willow or birch twigs in a tall pot, add white stars and a few warm-white lights, and leave it up after Christmas — the stars and branches read as winter, not just as a holiday.

18

Light-Up Branches Beside the Door

Idea 18 of 30

Wrap warm-white LED fairy lights around bare branches in a pot beside the door for a soft glow that costs almost nothing to run. Solar or rechargeable lights are taking over garden lighting, and LEDs run cool, so they will not damage living branches. Check the box for an outdoor or wet-location rating before you buy.

19

Red Berries and Birch Logs in a Crate

Idea 19 of 30

A lined wooden crate filled with evergreen branches, red berry stems and birch logs is a rustic Christmas planter that looks gathered, not bought. Line the crate so the soil stays in, fill the bottom third with upside-down nursery pots to save potting mix, and top with compost-rich mix so the greens sit near the rim.

20

Red Ribbon in Black Urns

Idea 20 of 30

A big red ribbon is one of the clearest trends of the season, and it works best on a plain black urn. The dark pot makes the green boughs and red berries pop, and one oversized bow is enough — you do not need ornaments as well. Tie the bow so it can be untied and reused, and take it off in rain so it does not stain.

21

Birch Logs in a Galvanised Pot

Idea 21 of 30

Birch logs, pinecones, evergreen branches and tiny lights turn a galvanised pot into a little winter scene. Keep the pine branches sparse enough that the white bark stays visible — that is what makes the display work. The pot is frost-proof, the logs last all winter, and the whole thing can stay out until spring.

22

Scandinavian White and Green Planter

Idea 22 of 30

A Scandinavian planter is white, green and calm: a white pot, soft evergreens, a little moss and one white star. Use a rosemary tree or a small spruce as the centre, add white berries or flocked branches for a snowy look, and skip the red entirely. It suits a small porch and a modern front door.

23

Farmhouse Plaid Bow Planter

Idea 23 of 30

A plaid bow, a galvanised pot and mixed evergreens give the farmhouse Christmas look that is pinned again every year. Add pinecones and a wooden star, keep the colours to green, red and natural wood, and let the arrangement look a little loose — farmhouse planters should not look perfect. The same pot works with a plain bow after Christmas.

24

Frost-Proof Fibreglass Urn for Winter

Idea 24 of 30

If your planter must stay outside all winter, choose fibreglass or resin — they do not absorb water, so they will not crack in freeze-thaw cycles. Glazed ceramic is safe only if it is frost-proof rated, and terracotta is not safe outside in winter at all. Lift the urn on pot feet so water can drain, and insulate the sides with bubble wrap in a hard frost.

25

Balcony Railing Trough with Evergreens

Idea 25 of 30

A railing trough is the Christmas planter for a balcony: it sits up and out of the way of your floor pots. Fill a coco-lined trough with cut evergreen boughs, red berries and a few lights, and leave your dormant perennials alone — do not stab their roots. Choose live evergreens rated two zones colder than yours, because pots freeze harder than ground.

26

Kissing Ball Hanging Basket

Idea 26 of 30

A kissing ball — a ball of evergreens, berries and pinecones hung in a wire basket — is a Christmas planter that needs no floor space at all. Hang it above the front door or from a porch beam, keep it light so the hook holds, and mist it now and then so the greens do not dry out. Take it down after Christmas and compost the greens.

27

Dried Orange and Cinnamon Pot

Idea 27 of 30

Dried orange slices, cinnamon sticks and star anise give a Christmas planter a handmade, fragrant finish for almost no cost. Dry the orange slices in a low oven first, then wire them into the evergreens with the cinnamon. They last for weeks outside, they are compostable, and they are the handmade detail Pinterest searches are rewarding this year.

28

Ornamental Kale and Cabbage Pot

Idea 28 of 30

Ornamental kale and cabbage give a Christmas pot bold colour and texture, and they tolerate hard frost. Plant them as the filler around a tall evergreen, in a potting mix with compost, and water only when the soil feels dry — they rot if they sit wet. Their purple, pink and cream leaves look good from December into February.

29

After-Christmas Winter Planter

Idea 29 of 30

Do not throw the planter away on Boxing Day. Take out the ornaments and the red ribbon, keep the evergreens, add birch stems and white berries, and the same pot becomes a winter planter that lasts until February. This is the thriftiest Christmas idea of all — one planter, two seasons, and nothing wasted.

30

Mini Herb Garden Christmas Gift Crate

Idea 30 of 30

A mini herb garden crate — rosemary, thyme and sage in small pots, tied with a ribbon and a handwritten care card — is a Christmas gift that keeps growing. Rosemary likes full sun and free-draining soil, thyme likes it dry, and sage likes a sunny windowsill. Tell the recipient to remove any wrapping before watering, and to let excess water drain away.

Key Takeaways

  • Build every Christmas planter around evergreens — they stay green in the cold, smell like the season, and last for months.
  • Use the thriller, filler, spiller formula: a tall centre, mid-height flowers, and something trailing over the rim.
  • Choose a frost-proof pot — fibreglass or resin — and keep the drainage hole clear so frozen water cannot crack it.
  • Pick plants rated two zones colder than yours, because roots in a pot freeze far harder than roots in the ground.
  • After Christmas, take out the ornaments and keep the greens — the same planter becomes a winter planter until February.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do you put in an outdoor Christmas planter?

Start with a tall centre — a dwarf evergreen, birch poles or red twig dogwood — then pack evergreen boughs around it so no soil shows, and finish with berries, pinecones and one big bow. Mix at least two kinds of evergreen, such as cedar for drape and fir for a stiffer shape, and use a potting mix with compost that drains freely.

Which containers survive winter outside?

Fibreglass and resin survive winter outside because they do not absorb water. Glazed ceramic is safe only if it is frost-proof rated, and terracotta is not safe — it is porous and cracks in freeze-thaw cycles. Whatever the material, keep the drainage hole clear and lift the pot on feet so water can escape.

How do you keep a Christmas planter alive?

Water once when you build it, then water only while the ground stays unfrozen and the soil feels dry. Roots in a pot are far less cold-hardy than roots in the ground, so choose plants rated two zones colder than yours, shelter the pot from wind, and insulate it in a hard frost. Cut evergreen boughs need no watering at all — just keep them out of strong sun.

Can poinsettia go outside in December?

No — poinsettia is cold-sensitive and belongs indoors in a bright spot, away from draughts and radiators. One frosty night outside can damage it. Remove the decorative foil before watering, let excess water drain away, and never leave it standing in water.

When should I build a Christmas planter?

Build a planted Christmas planter in October or November, so living plants have time to settle before December. A cut-green planter — evergreen boughs, berries and pinecones in a pot — can be built in late November or early December, and it will stay fresh for weeks. Plant spring bulbs under the greens if you want the same pot to flower in spring.

How do I water a winter container?

Check the soil with your finger: if the top 3 cm feels dry, water deeply in the morning, and let the excess drain away. Water far less in winter than in summer — most winter containers need water only once a week or less, and none at all once the soil freezes. Evergreens still lose water in winter, so do not let the root ball dry out completely in a dry spell.

What plants bloom by Christmas in pots?

Christmas rose (Helleborus niger), winter heather (Erica carnea), violas, sarcococca or Christmas box, Viburnum tinus, mahonia, Christmas cactus and poinsettia all bloom or colour up in December. Pansies and violas are the most reliable workhorses — they flower through most winters in pots, especially in a sheltered spot.

How do I turn a Christmas planter into a winter planter?

Take out the ornaments and the red ribbon after New Year, keep the evergreens, and add birch stems, white berries and pinecones. The same planter then reads as winter rather than Christmas, and it will last until February. Replace any tired boughs with fresh ones, and the pot can stay out until spring bulbs appear.
