30 February Garden Planning Ideas

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

February is the month the garden year turns: the days are getting longer, the seed catalogues are on the table, and every decision you make now decides what you will harvest in summer. These 30 ideas cover every part of february garden planning — seed orders, sowing calendars, soil tests, pruning deadlines, greenhouse and cold frame prep, and the first early sowings — so that when spring arrives you are ready instead of rushing. Before you start, check your hardiness zone and your last frost date: February is planting time in zones 9 to 11 and in mild parts of zone 8, while in colder zones 3 to 6 it is still a planning month, and the right jobs depend on how cold your garden actually gets.

1

Chase Missing Seed Orders

Idea 1 of 30

Seed orders placed in January should be arriving now, so check every packet against your list and chase anything missing before the sowing season starts. A variety that arrives in March is often too late for February sowings, and popular varieties sell out fast. Email or ring the supplier this week, and if a variety is gone for the year, pick a substitute now rather than in April. Keep the packets somewhere cool and dry until sowing day.

2

Draw a February-to-May Sowing Calendar

Idea 2 of 30

A sowing calendar turns a pile of seed packets into a plan: take a sheet of paper, mark the weeks from February to May, and write each crop under the week you will sow it, counting back from your last frost date. Tomatoes and chillies go earliest in mild areas, hardy peas and broad beans soon after, and tender courgettes last. Pin the calendar where you sow and tick off each sowing as you do it.

3

Shortlist This Year's Vegetable Varieties

Idea 3 of 30

February is the month to choose your varieties, while the catalogues are still full and the choice is widest. For each vegetable, pick one reliable variety and one to try: a tomato that crops early and one that tastes best, a lettuce that stands heat and one that stands cold. Write the shortlist down with the sowing dates, and order only what you will actually grow, because a drawer of unused packets is money wasted.

4

Order Seed Potatoes Before Stocks Run Out

Idea 4 of 30

Seed potatoes sell out fast, and the popular early varieties are often gone by March, so order them in February while the choice is still good. Choose first earlies for the earliest new potatoes, second earlies for summer, and maincrops for storing through winter. Buy certified seed potatoes, never supermarket potatoes, which can carry disease. When they arrive, set them in trays in a bright, cool room to chit.

5

Check Stored Dahlia Tubers for Rot

Idea 5 of 30

Dahlia tubers lifted in autumn spend winter in store, and February is the time to check them before they wake up. Tip them out of their box, squeeze each tuber gently, and throw away any that are soft, shrivelled or mouldy, because one rotting tuber can spoil the whole crate. Dust the healthy ones with dry compost, put them back in a cool, frost-free place, and start them into growth in March.

6

Decide What to Sow in February

Idea 6 of 30

Not everything can be sown in February, and the secret is knowing which crops are ready and which must wait. In mild areas you can sow broad beans, peas, onions and early lettuce under cover, while tomatoes and courgettes wait until March. Write your February sowing list now, with the dates and the place each crop will go, and you will not waste seed on sowings the weather will kill.

7

Clean the Greenhouse Glass for Spring Light

Idea 7 of 30

Winter grime on greenhouse glass can cut the light reaching your plants by a fifth, and February is the time to wash it off before the sowing season. Scrub the glass inside and out with warm soapy water, clear the gutters so rain drains away, and wash the staging too. Clean glass means stronger seedlings, less mould, and a greenhouse that warms up faster on sunny days.

8

Prepare the Cold Frame for Early Crops

Idea 8 of 30

A cold frame is February's best friend, but only if it is ready before you need it. Clear out last year's compost, scrub the glass, and check the lid hinges and the prop so it opens and closes smoothly. Fill it with fresh compost, and put a thermometer inside so you can see how warm it gets. By the time the first seedlings are ready, the frame will be waiting for them.

9

Turn the Compost Heap Before Spring

Idea 9 of 30

A compost heap that has sat all winter needs turning in February, while the garden is still quiet. Fork the heap over, moving the outside material into the middle where it is warmest, and add water if the heap is dry. Turning lets air in and speeds the rotting, so by April you will have dark, crumbly compost for mulching beds. A heap that never gets turned takes twice as long to finish.

10

Finish Winter Pruning Before Buds Swell

Idea 10 of 30

February is the last safe month for most winter pruning, because once the buds swell and the sap rises, cutting causes bleeding and weak growth. Walk the garden with your secateurs and finish the fruit trees, roses and shrubs that are still waiting, pruning on a dry day when the wood is not frozen. Anything left until March may have to wait until next winter, so do not put it off.

11

Plan the Herb Beds for the Year

Idea 11 of 30

Herbs are easy to forget in the rush of spring, so plan them in February while you still have time to think. Decide which herbs you actually cook with, and give each one its place: rosemary and thyme in the sunniest, driest spot, mint in a pot so it cannot spread, and basil near the kitchen door. Order seeds for parsley, coriander and dill now, and the herb bed will be planted before the vegetables take over.

12

Test Your Soil Before Spring Feeding

Idea 12 of 30

Feeding soil you have not tested is guesswork, so February is the time to find out what your soil actually needs. A simple test kit will tell you the pH and the main nutrients, and the result decides whether to add lime, compost or a balanced fertiliser. Most vegetables like soil near neutral, around pH 6.5, while blueberries and heathers need acid soil. Test now, and feed only what the soil is missing.

13

Check Water Butts and Hoses for Frost Damage

Idea 13 of 30

A hard winter cracks water butts, splits hoses and breaks taps, and February is the time to find the damage before you need water in spring. Check each butt for cracks, run the tap to see if it still turns, and unroll every hose to look for splits. Replace washers and broken fittings now, and fit a lid on each butt to keep out leaves and mosquitoes. A butt repaired in February saves a flood in April.

14

Sharpen Secateurs and Spades for Spring

Idea 14 of 30

Blunt tools tear stems and tire your arms, and February is the quiet month to sharpen everything before spring. Clean the blades with wire wool, sharpen secateurs with a whetstone, and run a file along the edge of spades and hoes. Oil the moving parts, tighten loose handles, and replace any tool that is bent or cracked. Sharp tools make cleaner cuts, and clean cuts heal faster and let in less disease.

15

Order Summer Bulbs for Planting After Frost

Idea 15 of 30

Dahlias, gladioli, lilies and begonias are planted after the last frost, but they are ordered in February, while the catalogues still have the best varieties. Choose colours that suit your borders, and order the bulbs now so they arrive in time for spring planting. Store them cool and dry when they come, and start dahlias into growth indoors in March so they flower weeks earlier than bulbs planted straight out.

16

Plan a Cut-Flower Patch for Summer

Idea 16 of 30

A cut-flower patch gives you armfuls of flowers all summer, and February is the time to plan it. Pick a sunny bed, and choose flowers that keep producing when you cut them: zinnias, cosmos, sweet peas, cornflowers and dahlias are all good. Plan the rows so you can reach every plant, order the seed now, and sow the hardy ones first. A patch the size of a door can fill a vase every week from June to October.

17

Order Bare-Root Fruit Before the Season Ends

Idea 17 of 30

Bare-root fruit trees and bushes are only sold while they are dormant, and the season ends in late February or March, so order now or wait another year. Choose varieties that suit your garden: a dessert apple for eating, a plum for cooking, and currants or gooseberries for the shady corners. Order from a nursery that sells certified stock, and have the planting holes ready before the trees arrive.

18

Inspect Fences, Paths and Beds After Winter

Idea 18 of 30

Winter wind, rain and frost damage fences, paths and raised beds, and February is the time to walk the garden and list the repairs. Look for loose fence panels, wobbly posts, cracked paving and beds whose boards have rotted or bowed. Fix the worst problems now, while plants are dormant and the beds are empty, because a repair in March means working around new growth. A sound fence also keeps out rabbits and deer.

19

Plan the Lawn's First Spring Care

Idea 19 of 30

Lawns wake up in March, and February is the time to plan their first care. Decide when you will make the first cut — usually when the grass is growing and the ground is dry — and set the mower blades high for the first few cuts. Plan to scarify and feed later in spring, not now, and mark any bare patches for overseeding in April. A lawn planned in February greens up faster than one left to chance.

20

Plan a Wildlife Corner for Spring

Idea 20 of 30

A wildlife corner costs nothing and brings the garden to life: a log pile for beetles, a patch of nettles for butterflies, and native flowers for bees. February is the time to choose the spot — a quiet corner out of the way — and to plan what goes there. Stack logs now, while the garden is quiet, and sow native wildflower seed in spring. Frogs, hedgehogs and bees will find it within weeks.

21

Plan Spring Containers for the Patio

Idea 21 of 30

Patio containers are the first colour of spring, and February is the time to plan them. Decide which pots will hold tulips and daffodils, which will hold violas and primroses, and which will wait for summer bedding. Clean the pots, check the drainage holes, and buy fresh compost now so you are not scrambling in March. A patio planned in February looks good from the first warm day.

22

Set a February Garden Budget

Idea 22 of 30

Gardening gets expensive when every catalogue arrives at once, so February is the time to set a budget before you order anything. List what you need — seeds, compost, bulbs, tools and repairs — and put a price beside each one, then decide what can wait until next year. A budget stops you buying three kinds of tomato when one would do, and it leaves money for the plants you really want.

23

Plan Outdoor Sowings by Soil Temperature

Idea 23 of 30

Seeds sown into cold soil sit and rot, so February sowings should wait for the soil to warm, not for a date on the calendar. Push a soil thermometer 10 centimetres into the bed in the morning: most vegetables germinate once the soil reaches 7 to 10 degrees, and peas and broad beans cope with less. Check the temperature for a week, and sow on the day the soil is warm enough, not before.

24

Mark Your Last Frost Date on the Calendar

Idea 24 of 30

Every sowing decision in spring hangs on your last frost date, so February is the time to find it and mark it where you will see it. Look it up for your area — your hardiness zone and local weather records will give you an average date — and write it on the calendar in red. Then count back from it for each crop: tomatoes six weeks before, courgettes four, and hardy peas as soon as the soil warms.

25

Plan Pea and Bean Supports Now

Idea 25 of 30

Peas and beans need supports from the day they go in, and February is the time to plan them while the beds are empty. Decide between hazel pea sticks, bamboo canes in a wigwam, or a net strung between posts, and order the canes now before the garden centres sell out. Cut and store the supports in a dry place, and put them up on planting day so the young plants can climb straight away.

26

Plan the First Salad Bed for March

Idea 26 of 30

The first salad of the year comes from a bed planned in February and sown in March, so choose the spot now. Pick the sunniest bed with the best drainage, and plan rows of lettuce, radish, spring onions and rocket, with a second sowing two weeks later. Order the seed this month, and have the bed weeded and raked before March, so sowing takes ten minutes on the first dry day.

27

Review Last Year's Garden Notes

Idea 27 of 30

The best teacher in gardening is your own last year, and February is the time to read what you wrote. Look back at your notes and ask what worked: which tomato cropped best, which bed stayed wet, which variety the slugs ate first. Then change the plan: drop the failures, repeat the successes, and try one new thing. A gardener who learns from last year grows a better garden every year.

28

Book a Compost and Manure Delivery for March

Idea 28 of 30

Beds need feeding before spring sowing, and bulk compost and manure are cheapest when ordered early. February is the time to work out how much you need — a barrowload per bed is a good rule — and to book a delivery for March, before the spring rush. Order well-rotted manure, never fresh, which burns young roots. A delivery booked now arrives when you need it, not three weeks later.

29

Plan Greenhouse Staging for Spring Seedlings

Idea 29 of 30

A greenhouse fills fast in spring, and February is the time to plan where everything will go. Work out how many trays you will have, and whether you need another shelf or a second staging level. Put the tallest plants at the back, the shortest at the front, and leave a path wide enough for a watering can. A greenhouse planned now holds twice as many plants as one filled at random.

30

Set Your First Harvest Dates

Idea 30 of 30

A garden planned backwards from the harvest is a garden that feeds you, so February is the time to set your first harvest dates. Work out when you want the first new potatoes, the first peas and the first salad, and count back to the sowing dates that make them happen. Write the harvest dates on the calendar next to the sowing dates, and the whole year becomes a plan instead of a hope.

Key Takeaways

  • February planning decides the whole year — order seeds and potatoes while the choice is widest.
  • Count every sowing back from your last frost date, and write the dates on a calendar.
  • Finish pruning, cleaning, sharpening and repairs in February, before spring sowing starts.
  • Test the soil before you feed it, and sow outdoors only when the soil is warm enough.
  • Plan the garden on paper first — beds, supports, staging and harvest dates — then plant.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start planning the February garden?

Start in late January, before the sowing season begins, while the garden is still quiet and the catalogues are full. February planning means ordering seeds and potatoes, drawing your sowing calendar, and checking tools and stored tubers — jobs that are hard to do once spring arrives. A garden planned in February is planted on time in March.

What can I plant in February?

In mild areas, zones 8 to 11, February is planting time for broad beans, peas, onions and early lettuce under cover, and for bare-root fruit while it is still dormant. In colder zones 3 to 6, February is for planning, not outdoor planting, because the ground is still frozen. Check your hardiness zone first.

Is February too early to sow seeds outdoors?

For most seeds, yes: February soil is too cold for germination, and seeds sown into cold, wet soil rot instead of sprouting. Hardy crops like broad beans and peas can go out under cover in mild areas, once the soil reaches 7 degrees. For everything else, wait until March, or sow indoors and plant out after your last frost date.

What should I order in February?

Order seed potatoes, summer bulbs and bare-root fruit in February, while the choice is still wide and the best varieties are in stock. Order seeds for March and April sowings, and check that your January orders have arrived. Buy compost and manure now too, before the spring rush pushes prices up. A February order arrives when you need it.

When should I finish winter pruning?

Finish winter pruning in February, before the buds swell and the sap starts to rise, because cuts made after that bleed and heal slowly. Prune fruit trees, roses and shrubs on a dry day when the wood is not frozen, and stop once the buds show green. Anything still unpruned in March should wait until next winter.

How do I plan a sowing calendar?

Take a sheet of paper, mark the weeks from February to May, and write each crop under its sowing week, counting back from your last frost date. Work backwards: tomatoes six weeks before the last frost, courgettes four, and hardy peas as soon as the soil warms. Pin it where you sow, and tick off each sowing.

What garden jobs should I finish in February?

Finish winter pruning, greenhouse cleaning, tool sharpening and compost turning in February, while the garden is still quiet and the beds are empty. Check stored dahlia tubers for rot, repair fences and water butts, and order seed potatoes before stocks run out. Jobs finished in February do not compete with sowing in March.

How do I use my last frost date in February planning?

Look up the last frost date for the area, and write it on the calendar — every spring sowing hangs on it. Count back from it for each crop: tomatoes six weeks before, courgettes four, and hardy peas as soon as the soil warms. In mild zones it falls in February, while in cold zones it is April or May.
