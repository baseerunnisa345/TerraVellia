28 Ways to Dry Herbs for Winter

Herb GardensUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Drying herbs is how summer flavour survives the winter: a jar of home-dried rosemary, thyme or mint turns a plain stew into a garden supper in January. These 28 ideas cover every part of drying herbs for winter — hanging bundles, mesh racks, dehydrators, low ovens and microwaves, the herbs that dry best and the ones that blacken, and storage that keeps flavour for months. The main harvest is over by December, but in mild hardiness zones 8 to 11 you can still cut herbs from the garden, while in cold zones 3 to 6 the garden crop is done and fresh shop bunches are the way to fill your jars — so check your hardiness zone and your last frost date before you start.

1

Hang Small Bundles Upside Down

Idea 1 of 28

Hanging is the classic way to dry herbs, and it works because gravity pulls the oils down into the leaves while air moves freely around the stems. Tie small bundles of five or six stems with string, hang them upside down from a hook or a line, and leave them in a warm, dark, airy room. Small bundles dry faster than fat ones, and they do not trap damp in the middle, which is what causes mould.

2

Air-Dry Herbs on a Mesh Rack

Idea 2 of 28

A mesh rack dries leaves and small sprigs that are too fiddly to tie into bundles. Spread the leaves in a single thin layer on a cake rack, a window screen or a purpose-made herb drying rack, and set it somewhere warm with moving air. Turn the leaves once a day so they dry evenly, and keep the layer thin — piled leaves go mouldy in the middle before the edges dry.

3

Dry Herbs in a Paper Bag

Idea 3 of 28

A paper bag is the trick for herbs that drop their seeds as they dry, like dill, coriander and fennel. Put the bundle head-down in a paper bag, tie the bag loosely around the stems, and hang the whole thing up. The bag catches every seed, keeps dust off the leaves, and still lets air through. Punch a few holes in the bag for ventilation, and never use plastic — it traps damp and rots the herbs.

4

A Drying Rack in a Warm Airing Cupboard

Idea 4 of 28

An airing cupboard is a ready-made drying room: warm, dark and dry, with a little air moving past the shelves. Hang bundles from the rails or spread leaves on a rack, and leave the door ajar a crack so damp air escapes. The steady warmth dries herbs in a week or so, and the darkness keeps their colour bright. Keep the rack away from damp towels, which slow the drying.

5

Dry Herbs in a Dark, Ventilated Room

Idea 5 of 28

Light is the enemy of dried herbs: it bleaches the colour and steals the flavour oils, so always dry in the dark or in dim light. A spare room, a garage or a shed works if air can move through it — open a window a crack or run a small fan on low. Warmth helps, but air movement matters more. Never dry herbs in a damp cellar or a steamy bathroom, where mould wins.

6

Dry Herbs in a Low Oven

Idea 6 of 28

An oven dries herbs in under an hour, but it is the easiest method to get wrong, because too much heat cooks the flavour away. Set the oven to its lowest temperature, usually 50 degrees or less, spread the leaves on a baking tray, and prop the door open a crack so damp escapes. Check every ten minutes, and take the herbs out the moment the leaves feel crisp.

7

Microwave-Dry Small Batches in Minutes

Idea 7 of 28

A microwave is the fastest way to dry a small handful of herbs, and it works surprisingly well for parsley, mint and basil. Lay a few leaves between two sheets of kitchen paper, microwave for 30 seconds, check, and repeat in short bursts until the leaves are crisp. Work in tiny batches and stop the moment the leaves feel dry — an extra ten seconds can scorch them brown.

8

Dry Herbs in a Makeshift Solar Dryer

Idea 8 of 28

A simple solar dryer uses the winter sun without bleaching the herbs: a shallow wooden box with a mesh shelf and a clear lid, set in the sunniest spot you have. The sun warms the air inside while the lid keeps dust and flies off, and the mesh lets damp escape. Put the dryer where it catches the midday sun, shade the herbs themselves with a cloth, and bring the box in at night.

9

Dry Basil Without Black Leaves

Idea 9 of 28

Basil is the herb most likely to turn black as it dries, because its soft, wet leaves bruise and oxidise easily. Pick the leaves in the morning when they are dry, handle them gently, and dry them fast — a dehydrator or a microwave beats slow air drying for basil. Spread the leaves in a single layer, keep them out of the light, and accept a little darkening: the flavour is still there.

10

Dry Mint for Winter Teas

Idea 10 of 28

Mint dries beautifully and keeps its scent for months, which makes it the easiest herb for winter teas. Cut the stems in the morning, tie them in small bundles, and hang them in a dark, airy room until the leaves feel papery. Strip the leaves into a jar, keep it in the dark, and crumble a pinch into hot water all winter. One summer plant dried this way fills a jar that lasts until spring.

11

Dry Rosemary on the Stem

Idea 11 of 28

Rosemary is one of the best herbs for drying, because its woody stems and needle leaves hold their oils for months. Hang small sprigs upside down in a dark room for a week or two, then strip the needles off the stems by running your fingers down them. Store the needles whole in a jar, and crumble them into roasts and stews. Rosemary dried this way keeps its flavour for a full year.

12

Dry Thyme and Strip the Leaves

Idea 12 of 28

Thyme dries fast because its tiny leaves hold little water, and it keeps its flavour better than almost any herb. Hang small sprigs upside down for a week, then strip the leaves by pulling the stem between your fingers over a bowl. The leaves fall off cleanly when they are dry. Store them whole in a dark jar, and crumble them into soups, stews and roast vegetables all winter.

13

Dry Oregano for Pizza Season

Idea 13 of 28

Oregano is at its most flavourful when it is dried, which is why dried oregano outsells fresh in most kitchens. Cut the stems just before the flowers open, hang the bundles upside down in the dark, and strip the leaves when they crumble. Store them whole and crumble them over pizza, pasta and roast vegetables. A single plant dried in summer seasons a winter's worth of suppers.

14

Dry Sage Leaves Whole

Idea 14 of 28

Sage dries well because its thick, grey-green leaves hold little water and lots of oil. Pick the leaves in the morning, lay them in a single layer on a rack, and dry them in a dark, airy room until they feel papery. Keep them whole in a jar — whole leaves keep their flavour far longer than crumbled ones. Crumble a leaf or two into stuffing, roast pork and winter squash dishes.

15

Dry Parsley Flat, Not in Bundles

Idea 15 of 28

Parsley goes mouldy in bundles because its soft, wet leaves trap damp in the middle, so dry it flat instead. Lay the leaves in a single layer on a mesh rack in a dark, airy room, and turn them once a day. They take a week or so to dry fully. Parsley loses some flavour when dried, so use it generously in soups and stews, where it still adds a fresh, green note.

16

Dry Dill Before It Goes to Seed

Idea 16 of 28

Dill has two harvests: the feathery leaves for drying, and the seeds for pickling. Cut the leaves before the plant flowers, when the flavour is strongest, and dry them flat on a rack or in a paper bag. The leaves dry in a few days and crumble easily. Keep them in a dark jar and use them in fish dishes, potato salads and pickles all winter.

17

Dry Chives Snipped and Scattered

Idea 17 of 28

Chives dry fast because the hollow stems hold little water, but they lose their onion bite quicker than most herbs, so dry them gently. Snip the stems into short pieces, scatter them in a single layer on a rack, and dry them in the dark until they feel papery. Store them in a small dark jar and use them within a few months, sprinkled over soups, baked potatoes and scrambled eggs.

18

Dry Lavender for Scent and Tea

Idea 18 of 28

Lavender is dried for scent as much as for flavour, and the buds keep both for a year or more. Cut the stems just as the buds open, tie them in small bundles, and hang them upside down in a dark room. When the buds feel dry, strip them into a jar. Use them in tea, in baking, in sachets for drawers, or scattered over roast lamb.

19

Dry Bay Leaves for the Stew Pot

Idea 19 of 28

Bay is the easiest herb to dry: the tough, leathery leaves dry slowly and keep their flavour for a year or more. Pick the leaves in the morning, lay them in a single layer on a rack, and leave them in a dark, airy room until they feel stiff and papery. Store them whole in a jar, and drop one leaf into stews, soups and rice dishes — one leaf is enough for a whole pot.

20

Dry Lemon Balm for Calming Teas

Idea 20 of 28

Lemon balm dries well and keeps its lemon scent for months, which makes it a good winter tea herb. Cut the stems in the morning, hang small bundles upside down in the dark, and strip the leaves when they feel papery. Store them in a dark jar, and steep a spoonful in hot water for a calming evening tea. The flavour fades after six months, so use it up by spring.

21

Dry Chamomile Flowers for Tea

Idea 21 of 28

Chamomile is dried for its flowers, not its leaves, and the tiny daisy heads keep their apple scent for a year. Pick the flowers on a dry morning, when they are fully open, and spread them in a single layer on a rack in a dark, airy room. They dry in a few days. Store them in a dark jar, and steep a spoonful in hot water for a gentle bedtime tea.

22

Dry Tarragon Before the First Frost

Idea 22 of 28

Tarragon is tender, so the first frost kills it outright — cut it and dry it before the cold arrives. Pick the leaves in the morning, hang small bundles upside down in a dark room, and strip the leaves when they crumble. French tarragon keeps its aniseed flavour far better than the Russian kind. Store the leaves whole in a dark jar, and use them in chicken dishes, sauces and omelettes.

23

Store Dried Herbs Whole, Crumble Later

Idea 23 of 28

Whole dried leaves keep their flavour far longer than crumbled ones, because less surface is exposed to air. Strip the leaves off the stems, store them whole in a jar, and crumble them only when you cook — rub a pinch between your palms over the pot. The difference is real: herbs crumbled in summer taste flat by Christmas, while herbs stored whole still taste of the garden.

24

Label Every Jar with Herb and Date

Idea 24 of 28

Every dried herb looks the same in a jar six months later, so label everything the day you pack it: the herb name, the variety if you know it, and the month you dried it. A strip of masking tape and a pen is all you need. Dried herbs keep six to twelve months, and the date tells you which jar to use first — and which one is past its best.

25

Keep Dried Herbs in the Dark

Idea 25 of 28

Light is the fastest way to ruin dried herbs: it bleaches the colour and breaks down the flavour oils, so a jar on a sunny shelf is empty flavour in a month. Store dried herbs in dark glass jars, in a cupboard, or in a drawer — anywhere the light cannot reach them. A cool, dark cupboard keeps herbs at their best for six to twelve months.

26

How Long Dried Herbs Really Last

Idea 26 of 28

Dried herbs do not go off, but they do go flat: the flavour fades a little every month. Most herbs are at their best for six months and still good at twelve, though basil and chives fade fastest and rosemary, thyme and bay last longest. Rub a pinch between your fingers and smell it — if the scent is faint, the herb is done, and it is time to use twice as much or throw it out.

27

Make a Winter Herb Salt

Idea 27 of 28

Herb salt is the best way to use up dried herbs that are losing their edge: the salt preserves what flavour is left and seasons as it goes. Mix one part crumbled dried herbs — rosemary, thyme and sage work well — with four parts flaky sea salt, and store the mix in a small jar. Sprinkle it over roast vegetables, chicken and potatoes all winter.

28

Blend a Dried Herb Mix for Roasts

Idea 28 of 28

A dried herb mix turns a plain roast into a proper winter supper, and it uses the herbs you dried in summer. Mix equal parts crumbled thyme, rosemary, oregano and sage, and store the blend in a dark jar. Rub a spoonful into chicken, pork or roast vegetables with olive oil before cooking. One jar of the mix seasons a winter's worth of roasts.

Key Takeaways

  • Dry low-moisture herbs whole, in the dark, with air moving — heat and light steal flavour.
  • Basil, mint and parsley need fast, gentle drying, or they blacken or go mouldy.
  • Store herbs whole in dark jars and crumble them only when you cook.
  • Dried herbs keep six to twelve months — label every jar with the herb and the date.
  • December drying works in mild zones from the garden, and everywhere from fresh shop bunches.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which herbs dry best?

Low-moisture herbs dry best: rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage, bay and lavender all dry and keep their flavour for a year. Soft, wet herbs like basil, mint, parsley and chives need faster drying, a dehydrator or microwave, or they go brown or mouldy. Dill and lemon balm sit in the middle, and dry well in a dark, airy room.

How long does air drying take?

Most herbs air-dry in seven to fourteen days, depending on the herb and the weather. Thin leaves like thyme and oregano dry in a week; thick leaves like sage and bay take longer. The herb is ready when the leaves crumble between your fingers and the stems snap. If leaves bend instead of crumbling, give them more time.

Why do my dried herbs go brown or black?

Herbs go brown or black when they dry too slowly, in light, or too hot. Basil is the worst offender: its soft leaves bruise and oxidise, so dry it fast, in the dark, and handle it gently. Damp is the other cause: herbs dried in a humid room go mouldy. Dry in a dark, airy place and spread leaves thinly.

Can I dry herbs in the oven?

Yes, but use the lowest setting, usually 50 degrees, and prop the door open so damp escapes. Spread the leaves in a single layer on a baking tray and check every ten minutes. Most herbs are done in 30 minutes to an hour. Take them out the moment the leaves feel crisp: extra time can cook the flavour away.

Should I dry herbs whole or chopped?

Dry them whole and chop or crumble them later. Whole leaves keep their flavour far longer, because less surface is exposed to air and light. Strip the leaves off the stems, store them whole in a dark jar, and crumble a pinch over the pot when you cook. Herbs crumbled before storage taste flat within a few months.

How do I know when herbs are dry enough?

Leaves should crumble easily between your fingers, and stems should snap rather than bend. If the leaves feel soft or leathery, they still hold water, and they will go mouldy in the jar. Test leaves from the middle of the bundle, where drying is slowest. When in doubt, dry for another day: over-dried herbs are fine, under-dried ones rot.

How long do dried herbs keep?

Most dried herbs are at their best for six months and still usable at twelve, though the flavour fades a little every month. Rosemary, thyme, oregano and bay last longest; basil, mint and chives fade fastest. Store them whole, in the dark, in airtight jars, and label jars with herb and date so you use the oldest first.

Can I dry herbs I bought from the shop?

Yes — fresh bunches from the shop dry just as well as garden herbs, and in December they are often the best option, when the garden harvest is over. Check the bunches are fresh and dry, not damp or wilting, and spread the leaves out so air can move. The same rules apply: dark, airy, and not too hot.
