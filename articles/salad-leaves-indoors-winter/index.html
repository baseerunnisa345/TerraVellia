30 Salad Leaves to Grow Indoors This Winter

Vegetable GardeningUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Fresh salad in the middle of winter sounds impossible until you try it on a windowsill: a tray of lettuce, a pot of rocket and a row of spinach can keep cutting for weeks, even while the garden outside is frozen. These 30 ideas cover every part of grow salad leaves indoors winter — which leaves to grow, how to sow them, where to put the trays, how much light and water they need, and how to cut them so they grow back. Because the leaves grow indoors, your hardiness zone and your last frost date do not decide the harvest — a bright windowsill, a little warmth and regular picking do.

1

A Sunny Windowsill Salad Station

Idea 1 of 30

Salad leaves need light more than heat, and a sunny windowsill is the best place in the house to grow them in winter. Choose the brightest sill you have, ideally south-facing, and set the trays close to the glass. Turn them every few days so the leaves grow straight, and keep them off the radiator, which dries compost fast. Four hours of sun a day keeps lettuce, rocket and spinach cropping.

2

Loose-Leaf Lettuce in a Wide Tray

Idea 2 of 30

Loose-leaf lettuce is made for indoor winter growing because you pick the leaves, not the whole head. Sow the seed thinly in a wide tray of compost, cover it lightly, and keep it damp until the seedlings appear. Thin the plants to a hand's width apart so each one has room to grow, and start picking the outer leaves once they are big enough to eat. One tray sown in December can feed you into February.

3

Rocket That Loves a Cool Sill

Idea 3 of 30

Rocket is one of the easiest salad leaves to grow indoors because it likes the cool conditions of a winter windowsill. Sow it in a pot of compost, keep the soil damp but never wet, and it will be ready to pick in about three weeks. Cut the outer leaves and leave the centre to grow, and the plant will keep producing for months. Rocket that grows too warm bolts fast, so a cool sill suits it best.

4

Spinach Picked Leaf by Leaf

Idea 4 of 30

Spinach grown indoors gives you leaves all winter if you pick it the right way. Sow the seed in a deep tray, thin the seedlings to a few centimetres apart, and water from below so the leaves stay dry. Pick the biggest outer leaves first, leaving the small centre leaves to grow on, and the plant will keep producing. A pot of spinach sown in November can still be cropping in March.

5

Mizuna for Mild Mustard Leaves

Idea 5 of 30

Mizuna is a Japanese leaf with a mild mustard flavour, and it grows fast on a winter windowsill. Sow it thinly in a tray, keep the compost damp, and the feathery leaves will be ready in about four weeks. Cut the outer leaves and the plant will grow back, giving you two or three pickings from one sowing. Mizuna copes with cool rooms better than lettuce, so it suits a north-facing sill too.

6

Tatsoi Rosettes in a Shallow Pot

Idea 6 of 30

Tatsoi grows as a neat rosette of dark green, spoon-shaped leaves, and it is one of the hardiest salad leaves you can grow indoors. Sow it in a shallow pot, thin the seedlings to one per pot, and keep the compost damp. The leaves are mild and buttery, good raw in salads or wilted in a pan, and the plant will regrow after cutting. Tatsoi is happy in a cool room, so it rarely bolts in winter.

7

Lamb's Lettuce for the Darkest Weeks

Idea 7 of 30

Lamb's lettuce, also called corn salad or mache, is the salad leaf for the darkest weeks of winter because it grows when almost nothing else will. Sow it in a tray of compost in autumn, keep it cool and damp, and the small, nutty leaves will be ready in six to eight weeks. Pick the whole rosette or take the outer leaves, and sow a second tray a month later. It is slow, but it never fails.

8

Mustard Leaves with a Gentle Bite

Idea 8 of 30

Mustard leaves add a warm bite to winter salads, and they are ready to pick just three weeks after sowing. Sow them thinly in a tray, keep the compost damp, and cut the leaves young, when they are mild and tender. Older leaves turn hot and tough, so pick little and often. A small tray of mustard is enough for a household, because a little goes a long way in a salad bowl.

9

Red Chard Stems for Winter Salads

Idea 9 of 30

Rainbow chard grown indoors gives you two crops in one: the glossy leaves for salads and the bright stems for cooking. Sow it in a deep pot, thin to one plant per pot, and pick the outer leaves while the centre keeps growing. Young chard leaves are tender enough to eat raw, and the red stems look beautiful in a winter salad. One pot of chard can crop for three months if you keep picking.

10

Baby Kale Leaves, Not Full Plants

Idea 10 of 30

Kale grown for baby leaves is a different crop from the big plants in the garden, and it suits a windowsill perfectly. Sow the seed thickly in a tray, and cut the leaves when they are the size of your hand, before they turn tough. The flavour is mild and sweet, nothing like the bitterness of old kale, and the plants regrow after cutting. Baby kale is one of the most nutritious leaves you can grow indoors.

11

Cut-and-Come-Again Lettuce Mix

Idea 11 of 30

A cut-and-come-again lettuce mix is the simplest way to keep salad on the table all winter. Sow a mix of loose-leaf varieties in a wide tray, and when the leaves reach picking size, cut them two centimetres above the compost with scissors. The plants regrow from the cut stems, and you can take three or four pickings from one sowing. Sow a fresh tray every fortnight and you will never run out.

12

A Fresh Salad Bowl Every Fortnight

Idea 12 of 30

The secret to winter salads is not one big sowing but many small ones. Sow a tray every fortnight, and by the time the first tray is finished, the next one is ready to pick. Keep a simple routine: sow on the first and the fifteenth of the month, label each tray with the date, and water the newest tray first. Four small trays in rotation give you salad every week from December to March.

13

Grow Lights Hung Low Over Salad Trays

Idea 13 of 30

In the darkest weeks of winter, a grow light can double the speed of your salad leaves. Hang a small LED grow light about 15 centimetres above the trays, and switch it on for twelve hours a day, from morning to evening. Keep the light close, because a light hung too high does nothing, and raise it as the leaves grow. Salad under lights grows stocky and green instead of pale and leggy.

14

Shallow Trays, Not Deep Pots

Idea 14 of 30

Salad leaves have shallow roots, so a tray five centimetres deep is all they need, and deep pots only waste compost. Use seed trays, mushroom punnets or even a foil tray with holes punched in the bottom, and fill them with fine, damp compost. Shallow trays warm up fast on a sunny sill, which helps the seed germinate, and they are easy to move when you need the space. Just remember they dry out quickly, so check the water daily.

15

Fine Compost for Tender Salad Roots

Idea 15 of 30

Salad leaves grow fast and tender, and they need fine, soft compost to do it. Use a good seed compost, not garden soil, and sieve it first if it is lumpy, because big lumps stop tiny seeds from germinating. Fill the tray, level the surface, and water it before you sow, so the seed sits on damp compost. Fine compost holds water without drowning the roots, and that is what salad leaves need.

16

Water Little and Often

Idea 16 of 30

The biggest mistake with indoor salad is watering too much at once, which rots the roots and brings on damping off. Water little and often instead: a small amount every day or two, just enough to keep the compost damp like a wrung-out sponge. Water in the morning, so the leaves dry before night, and water the compost, not the leaves. A tray that is too wet grows mould, while a tray that is too dry stops growing.

17

Pick the Outer Leaves First

Idea 17 of 30

The way you pick decides how long a salad plant keeps cropping. Always take the outer leaves first, leaving the small centre leaves to grow, and never strip a plant bare. Use scissors or pinch the leaf off at the base, and pick in the morning, when the leaves are crisp and full of water. A plant picked this way will give you leaves for six weeks or more, while a plant stripped bare gives you one meal.

18

Five Leaves in One Salad Tray

Idea 18 of 30

One tray can hold a whole salad if you sow five leaves together. Mix lettuce, rocket, spinach, mizuna and mustard in one tray, sowing each in its own strip so you can see what is what. The leaves grow at different speeds, so you pick the fast ones first and the slow ones later, and every picking is a ready-made salad. A mixed tray is also prettier than five separate pots, and it takes up less space on the sill.

19

Endive and Chicory for Bitter Winter Leaves

Idea 19 of 30

Endive and chicory are the bitter leaves that make a winter salad interesting, and they grow well indoors. Sow them in a tray in autumn, thin the seedlings, and pick the leaves young, when the bitterness is gentle. A few bitter leaves mixed with sweet lettuce and mild spinach balance a salad, and they are good with cheese and nuts too. Endive and chicory are slow to grow, so sow them early and be patient.

20

Sorrel for a Lemon Note in Salads

Idea 20 of 30

Sorrel is a perennial leaf with a sharp lemon flavour, and a pot on the windowsill will supply you all winter. Sow it once and it will keep producing leaves for years, growing back after every picking. Add just a few leaves to a salad, because the flavour is strong, and mix them with mild lettuce so they do not take over. Sorrel is also good in soups, and it is one of the first leaves to appear in spring.

21

Land Cress for Cold-Weather Salads

Idea 21 of 30

Land cress is a hardy little leaf with the flavour of watercress, and it grows happily on a cold windowsill. Sow it in a tray of damp compost, keep it cool, and the peppery leaves will be ready in about five weeks. Pick the outer leaves and the plant will regrow, giving you several pickings through winter. Land cress is one of the few salad leaves that actually prefers the cold, so it is perfect for a north-facing sill.

22

Claytonia, the Miner's Lettuce

Idea 22 of 30

Claytonia, known as miner's lettuce, was eaten by gold miners for its vitamin C, and it is a lovely winter salad leaf. Sow it in a tray in autumn, keep the compost damp, and the round, fleshy leaves will be ready in six weeks. The flavour is mild and fresh, and the leaves are good mixed with rocket and mustard. Claytonia likes cool conditions and shade, so it is happy on a windowsill that gets little direct sun.

23

Parsley and Chervil as Salad Leaves

Idea 23 of 30

Parsley and chervil are usually called herbs, but their leaves belong in the salad bowl too. Sow parsley in a pot and chervil in a tray, and pick the leaves young, when they are soft and full of flavour. A handful of chopped parsley lifts a winter salad, and chervil adds a gentle aniseed note that suits fish and eggs. Both grow slowly in winter, so sow them early and keep picking the outer leaves.

24

Salad Burnet for Cucumber-Flavoured Leaves

Idea 24 of 30

Salad burnet is an old-fashioned leaf that tastes faintly of cucumber, and it is a delight in winter salads. Sow it in a pot of compost, keep it damp, and pick the young leaves, which are tender and fresh. The flavour is gentle, so use a good handful in the bowl, mixed with lettuce and rocket. Salad burnet is a perennial, so one pot will last for years, and it is one of the first leaves to grow in spring.

25

Beet Leaves for Colour in the Bowl

Idea 25 of 30

Beetroot grown for its leaves, not its roots, gives you deep red and green leaves that colour a winter salad. Sow the seed in a tray, thin the seedlings, and pick the young leaves while they are tender, before they turn tough and earthy. The flavour is mild and sweet, and the red stems look beautiful against green lettuce. Grow beet leaves in a sunny spot, and keep the compost damp so the leaves stay soft.

26

A Salad Tray Beside the Kitchen Sink

Idea 26 of 30

The best place for a salad tray is beside the kitchen sink, where you will see it every day and remember to water it. A tray in the kitchen gets picked more often than one in a spare room, and the leaves are at hand. Keep it on a saucer for drips, and turn it every few days so the leaves grow evenly. A salad tray you can reach while you cook is a salad tray that gets eaten.

27

Nasturtium Leaves with a Peppery Bite

Idea 27 of 30

Nasturtium leaves are peppery, pretty and edible, and a pot on a sunny sill will give you leaves all winter. Sow the seed in a pot of compost, and let the plant trail or climb a small cane. Pick the young round leaves, which are mild, and leave the older ones, which are hotter. Nasturtium flowers are edible too, and they brighten a winter salad like nothing else.

28

No Slugs Indoors, Just Clean Leaves

Idea 28 of 30

One of the best things about growing salad indoors is that there are no slugs, no snails and almost no pests. The leaves stay clean, with no holes and no slime, and you can eat them straight from the tray after a quick rinse. Keep the trays clean, remove dead leaves promptly, and do not bring outdoor plants inside, because they can carry aphids. A clean windowsill is the easiest pest control there is.

29

Morning Harvests for the Crispest Leaves

Idea 29 of 30

Salad leaves are at their crispest in the morning, when they are full of water from the night. Pick them early, before the sun warms the room, and put them straight into cold water for a few minutes. Dry them gently in a tea towel or a salad spinner, and they will stay crisp for hours. Leaves picked in the evening are softer and wilt faster, so morning is always the best time to harvest.

30

Three Trays for a Winter Salad Bar

Idea 30 of 30

Three trays are all you need for a winter salad bar: one of lettuce, one of rocket and mustard, and one of spinach and chard. Sow them a week apart, so they are ready in turn, and keep a fourth tray sown as a backup. Pick from each tray in turn, and the salad bar never runs out. Three trays take up less than a metre of windowsill, and they will feed a household all winter.

Key Takeaways

  • Salad leaves need light more than heat — a bright windowsill beats a warm room.
  • Pick the outer leaves first, and the plants will keep cropping for weeks.
  • Sow a fresh tray every fortnight, so one tray is always ready to pick.
  • Water little and often, in the morning, and keep the compost damp, never wet.
  • Mix five leaves in one tray for a ready-made salad from a single picking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I grow salad leaves indoors in winter?

Yes, salad leaves are one of the easiest crops to grow indoors in winter, because they need light more than heat. A bright windowsill, a tray of compost and regular watering are enough for lettuce, rocket, spinach and mizuna. They grow slowly in the darkest weeks, so start them in autumn. A sunny sill can feed a household.

Which salad leaves grow best indoors in winter?

Lettuce, rocket, spinach, mizuna, tatsoi, lamb's lettuce and mustard leaves all grow well indoors in winter. They are fast, they cope with cool rooms, and they regrow after cutting. Avoid heat-loving leaves like basil in midwinter, and choose loose-leaf varieties rather than hearting types, which need more space and light than a windowsill can give.

How much light do indoor salad leaves need?

Salad leaves need as much light as you can give them in winter, ideally four to six hours of sun a day on a south-facing windowsill. In the darkest weeks, a small LED grow light hung above the trays for twelve hours a day keeps them growing. Leaves that do not get enough light grow pale, leggy and weak.

How often should I water indoor salad leaves?

Water little and often, keeping the compost damp like a wrung-out sponge, never wet and never dry. In winter, a small amount every day or two is usually enough, and you should water in the morning so the leaves dry before night. Shallow trays dry out fast, so check them daily, and always water the compost, not the leaves.

How do I cut salad leaves so they grow back?

Cut the outer leaves first, leaving the small centre leaves to grow, and cut about two centimetres above the compost with scissors. Never strip a plant bare, or it will die instead of regrowing. Pick in the morning, when the leaves are crisp, and a tray cut this way will give you three or four pickings before it needs replacing.

Why are my indoor salad leaves pale and leggy?

Pale, leggy leaves almost always mean too little light, which is common in midwinter. Move the trays to your brightest windowsill, turn them every few days, and hang a grow light above them if you can. Leaves that are already leggy will not recover, so sow a fresh tray in better light and keep the light close to the leaves.

Can I grow salad leaves without grow lights?

Yes, you can grow salad leaves without grow lights if you have a bright, south-facing windowsill. Growth will be slower in December and January, so sow in autumn, choose cool-loving leaves like lamb's lettuce and mizuna, and be patient. A grow light helps, but it is not essential, and many gardeners grow winter salad on windowsills alone.

How do I keep indoor salad leaves free of pests?

Indoor salad rarely gets pests, because slugs and snails cannot reach a windowsill. Keep the trays clean, remove dead leaves promptly, and do not bring outdoor plants inside, because they can carry aphids. If aphids appear, wash the leaves with water or pick them off by hand. Clean compost and good air flow prevent most problems before they start.
