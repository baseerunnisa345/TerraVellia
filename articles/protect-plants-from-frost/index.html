23 Ways to Protect Plants From Frost

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Frost is the quiet killer of the winter garden: one clear, still night can blacken tender leaves, split pots and end a plant that took all summer to grow. The good news is that most frost damage is preventable, and the tools are simple — horticultural fleece, mulch, cloches, cold frames and a warm wall. These 23 ideas cover every way of protecting plants from frost, from draping fleece over a single plant to lining a greenhouse with bubble wrap, with the plants that need help most — citrus, dahlias, tree ferns, roses and tender pots — and the materials that work best. Before you start, check your hardiness zone and your last frost date: winter looks very different in zone 8 than in zone 5, and the right protection depends on how cold your garden actually gets.

1

Horticultural Fleece Draped Loosely

Idea 1 of 23

Horticultural fleece is the first line of defence: drape it loosely over tender plants in the late afternoon, weigh the edges down with bricks or pegs, and let it trap the warmth the soil gathered during the day. Fleece breathes, so damp air escapes instead of freezing on the leaves, and it lets in light, so you can leave it on for several cold days. Never pull it tight — frozen fleece touching a leaf can burn it.

2

Cloches Over Individual Plants

Idea 2 of 23

A cloche is a small cover for a single plant, and it is the simplest frost protection there is. Glass bell cloches, cut-off plastic bottles and purpose-made plastic cloches all work the same way: they hold a pocket of warm air around the plant and keep frost off the leaves. Set them over young lettuce, cuttings and tender seedlings before dusk, lift them off on mild days so the plant gets air, and weigh them down in wind.

3

Deep Mulch Around the Crown

Idea 3 of 23

A deep mulch blanket keeps the roots and the crown — the growing point at the base — alive even when the top growth dies back. Pile 10 to 15 cm of straw, bark, leaf mould or compost around the base of tender perennials, roses and newly planted shrubs after the first light frost. Keep the mulch a few centimetres from the stem so damp does not rot it, and pull it back in spring when growth restarts.

4

Move Pots Against a Warm Wall

Idea 4 of 23

A south- or west-facing brick wall stores the day's heat and gives it back all night, so it is the warmest spot in a cold garden. Move tender pots against it before a frost, and the wall can keep the air around them a degree or two warmer — often enough to save a plant. Walls also shelter pots from the coldest winds, which strip heat faster than still air.

5

Bring Tender Pots Indoors Overnight

Idea 5 of 23

The surest protection is the simplest: carry tender pots into a porch, garage or unheated room before nightfall and put them back out in the morning. Citrus, pelargoniums, tender succulents and young plants all survive a cold night this way. Keep them in the brightest spot you have indoors, water sparingly while they are inside, and never leave them beside a hot radiator.

6

Water the Soil Before a Frost

Idea 6 of 23

Moist soil holds heat far better than dry soil, so water the ground around tender plants in the late afternoon before a frosty night. The water releases its warmth slowly as the temperature falls, keeping the roots a degree or two above freezing. Water the soil, not the leaves — wet leaves freeze faster — and never water if the soil is already frozen or waterlogged.

7

Cover with Old Blankets and Sheets

Idea 7 of 23

You do not need to buy anything to protect plants: old blankets, sheets and towels work as well as fleece for one cold night. Drape them over tender plants before dusk, peg or weigh the edges down, and remove them in the morning so the plants get light and air. Avoid plastic sheeting on its own — it traps damp, freezes to the leaves and can do more harm than good.

8

Straw Bale Windbreak Around Beds

Idea 8 of 23

Wind makes frost far worse, because it strips the thin layer of warm air that sits around a plant. Stack straw bales on the windward side of a tender bed to make a windbreak that slows the air and traps warmth. The bales also insulate the soil at the bed's edge, and in spring you can break them up and use the straw as mulch.

9

Cold Frame with a Glass Lid

Idea 9 of 23

A cold frame is a low, glass-lidded box that turns a patch of ground into a sheltered microclimate, several degrees warmer than the open garden. It is ideal for hardening off young plants, overwintering cuttings and growing winter salads. Open the lid on mild days so plants get air and do not overheat in sun, close it before dusk, and throw a piece of old carpet over the glass on the coldest nights.

10

Hessian Wrap for Tree Ferns

Idea 10 of 23

Tree ferns need their crown protected: frost kills the growing point at the top and the whole plant dies. Pack straw into the crown in autumn, then wrap the trunk and crown in hessian sacking, tying it loosely so air can move. The hessian keeps the worst cold and wind off while letting the plant breathe, and you can unwrap it in spring once the last frost has passed.

11

Raise Pots on Feet

Idea 11 of 23

Pots sitting flat on cold ground or a patio draw cold up from below, and water pooling underneath freezes the roots from the bottom. Raise every pot on terracotta feet, bricks or a wooden batten so air can flow underneath and water drains away. It is a five-minute job that saves pots from cracking and roots from freezing, and it costs almost nothing.

12

Fleece Tunnel on Hoops Over Rows

Idea 12 of 23

For a whole row of vegetables, a fleece tunnel beats draping sheets: push hoops of wire or plastic pipe into the ground every metre, pull horticultural fleece over them, and bury the edges in soil. The tunnel holds warm air around the crop, keeps frost off the leaves, and still lets rain through. Lift one side on mild days to ventilate, and weigh it down again before night.

13

Line the Greenhouse with Bubble Wrap

Idea 13 of 23

A layer of bubble wrap inside the greenhouse is the cheapest insulation there is: it traps air against the glass, cuts heat loss, and can keep the inside several degrees warmer on a frosty night. Pin large-bubble wrap to the inside of the glass in late autumn, leaving a gap at the vents for air, and take it down in spring so the plants get full light again.

14

String Lights Under the Cover

Idea 14 of 23

A string of old-fashioned incandescent fairy lights under a fleece cover gives off a gentle warmth — just enough to lift the air under the cover above freezing on a cold night. Lay the lights around the base of the plants, never touching the leaves, and switch them on before dusk. LED lights look pretty but give off almost no heat, so they will not help.

15

Lift Dahlias and Store the Tubers

Idea 15 of 23

Dahlias are not frost hardy, and a single hard frost turns their tubers to mush in the ground. After the first light frost blackens the foliage, cut the stems down, lift the tubers carefully with a fork, and dry them for a few days. Pack them in dry compost or vermiculite in a frost-free shed, check them monthly for rot, and replant after the last frost in spring.

16

Group Pots Together for Warmth

Idea 16 of 23

Pots standing alone lose heat from every side, but pots grouped tightly together shelter each other: the ones in the middle stay noticeably warmer, and the group breaks the wind. Push your tender pots into a tight cluster in the most sheltered corner of the garden, with the hardiest plants on the outside as a windbreak, and cover the whole group with one sheet of fleece.

17

Shelter Pots Under a Porch Roof

Idea 17 of 23

A porch roof, carport or cold frame keeps the worst of the frost off: frost forms on clear, still nights when heat escapes to the sky, and a roof above the plants stops that heat loss. Move tender pots under any roof you have for the winter — they will stay a few degrees warmer than plants in the open, and the roof also keeps winter rain off the compost.

18

Hill Soil Around Tender Stems

Idea 18 of 23

Hilling — drawing soil up around the stems of tender plants — buries the lower buds below the frost line, so even if the top dies back, the plant regrows from the base. It works for young dahlias, tender perennials and new shrubs. Hill the soil up in late autumn when the first frosts threaten, and level it back down in spring when growth restarts.

19

Fleece Jackets for Standard Trees

Idea 19 of 23

Standard trees — lollipop-shaped bay, olive and citrus — are vulnerable because their crown sits high in the coldest air and their trunk is exposed. Wrap the crown in a fleece jacket, tie it at the trunk, and mound compost around the base. The jacket keeps the leaves from freezing on a still night, and you can take it off in the morning so the tree gets light.

20

Cover Before Dusk, Uncover After Thaw

Idea 20 of 23

Timing matters as much as the cover itself: put covers on before dusk, while the soil is still warm from the day, and take them off in the morning only after the frost has thawed. Covering too late traps cold air, not warm air, and leaving covers on for days starves the plants of light and invites mould. On a run of frosty nights, lift the covers each morning and replace them each evening.

21

Vent Covers on Sunny Days

Idea 21 of 23

Sun on a covered plant can cook it: the temperature under fleece or plastic on a bright winter day can rise far above the air outside, then plunge again at night. Lift the covers on sunny days, even in winter, so the plants get fresh air and the temperature under the cover stays even. Ventilate cloches and cold frames too — a closed cloche in winter sun can reach 30 degrees.

22

Leave Frost-Damaged Growth Until Spring

Idea 22 of 23

When frost blackens a plant, the instinct is to cut the damage off — but do not. The dead top growth is itself a blanket: it shelters the buds and stems below from the next frost. Leave everything alone until spring, then prune back to green, living wood once the last frost has passed. Cutting too early exposes fresh cuts to the next cold night.

23

Check the Forecast Every Evening

Idea 23 of 23

The best frost protection is a habit: check the forecast every evening from late autumn to spring, and act before the temperature falls. A clear sky, still air and a dry day are the classic warning signs of a frosty night, even when the forecast says otherwise. Keep fleece, pegs and covers by the back door so you can protect a plant in five minutes.

Key Takeaways

  • Cover before dusk so the day's warmth stays trapped under the cover.
  • Fleece beats plastic — it breathes, so leaves do not sweat and freeze.
  • Mulch the roots, protect the crown, and move pots to shelter.
  • Know your hardiness zone and last frost date before planting tender things out.
  • Leave frost-damaged growth alone until spring, then prune back to green wood.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I cover plants for frost?

Cover plants before dusk, while the soil is warm from the day, so the cover traps warmth rather than cold air. Check the forecast each evening from late autumn to spring, and cover whenever frost is expected or the sky is clear and still. Remove covers in the morning after the frost has thawed, so plants get light and air.

What is the best material to cover plants from frost?

Horticultural fleece is the best all-round choice: it lets light and air through, holds warmth, and does not freeze to the leaves. Old blankets and sheets work for a single night, and cloches suit individual plants. Avoid plain plastic sheeting — it traps damp, freezes to the foliage and can scorch leaves in winter sun.

Can I use plastic sheeting to protect plants from frost?

You can, but only with care: plastic sheeting must never touch the leaves, because damp freezes to it and burns the foliage. Drape it over hoops or a frame so there is an air gap, and remove it every morning. Fleece is safer and easier — it breathes, lets light through and can stay on for several cold days.

How cold is too cold for tender plants?

Most tender plants suffer below about 5 degrees, and a hard frost at minus 2 degrees or lower can kill them outright. Half-hardy plants cope with a light frost but not a freeze. Check your hardiness zone and the plant label: if it says tender or frost-free, protect it whenever the forecast drops near freezing.

Should I water plants before a frost?

Yes — water the soil, not the leaves, in the late afternoon before a frosty night. Moist soil holds heat far better than dry soil and releases it slowly as the temperature falls, keeping roots a degree or two warmer. Never water frozen or waterlogged soil, and never wet the foliage, which freezes faster when damp.

When should I uncover plants in the morning?

Uncover plants only after the frost has thawed and the sun has warmed the air, usually mid-morning. Uncovering too early exposes cold, wet leaves to the next chill, while leaving covers on all day starves plants of light and can cook them in winter sun. On a run of frosty nights, lift covers each morning and replace them before dusk.

Will mulch alone protect plants from frost?

Mulch protects the roots and the crown, which is enough to save a plant when the top growth dies back — but it does not protect tender leaves and stems. For tender plants, combine mulch with a fleece cover or a move to shelter. Use 10 to 15 cm of straw or bark, kept a few centimetres from the stem.

What should I do with frost-damaged plants?

Leave them alone until spring. The dead top growth shelters the buds and stems below from the next frost, so cutting it off exposes fresh cuts to more cold. Once the last frost has passed, prune back to green, living wood, and the plant will usually regrow from the base or from buds you could not see.
