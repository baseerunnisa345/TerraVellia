30 Winter Greenhouse Gardening Ideas

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

An unheated greenhouse buys you a whole season: tender plants that would die outside survive under glass, and winter salads keep growing while the garden sleeps. These 30 ideas cover every part of winter greenhouse gardening — insulating with bubble wrap, heating safely, ventilating on sunny days, watering little, and choosing what to grow — for greenhouses with no heat at all and for ones with a little help. In cold hardiness zones 3 to 6 an unheated greenhouse needs hardy plants only, or a heater on the coldest nights, while in mild zones 8 to 11 it stays frost-free most of the winter. Either way, the work you do now decides what is alive when the last frost passes and spring begins.

1

How an Unheated Greenhouse Works in Winter

Idea 1 of 30

An unheated greenhouse does not stop frost — it softens it. The glass traps the day's heat and blocks the wind, so the air inside stays a few degrees warmer than the garden, and tender plants that would die outside survive. But on a clear, still night the inside can still freeze, so know your limits: an unheated greenhouse buys roughly one hardiness zone of protection, and in cold zones it suits hardy salads and overwintering plants, not tender crops.

2

Bubble Wrap on the Inside of the Glass

Idea 2 of 30

A layer of large-bubble wrap pinned to the inside of the glass is the cheapest greenhouse insulation there is. It traps air against the panes, cuts heat loss, and can keep the inside several degrees warmer on a frosty night. Fix it in late autumn with clips or tape, leave a gap at the vents and door so air can move, and take it down in spring — it costs you light, and plants need every ray by March.

3

An Electric Fan Heater on a Thermostat

Idea 3 of 30

An electric fan heater on a thermostat is the simplest way to keep a greenhouse frost-free: set it to 5 degrees and it only runs on the coldest nights, warming the air and moving it around so no corner freezes. Choose a heater made for greenhouses, keep it off the floor and away from water, and never run an extension lead through a wet garden. It costs little to run if it only switches on below freezing.

4

A Paraffin Heater for Power Cuts

Idea 4 of 30

A paraffin heater needs no electricity, so it keeps working when a winter storm cuts the power — exactly when a heated greenhouse needs it most. It gives off steady warmth and a little moisture, which suits most winter plants. But it burns oxygen and gives off fumes, so ventilate every day, keep it away from plants and fleece, and never leave a lit heater unattended in a greenhouse full of dry leaves.

5

Soil-Warming Cables Under the Bench

Idea 5 of 30

Soil-warming cables heat the roots, not the air, which is the efficient way to keep plants growing in a cold greenhouse. Lay the cable in sand under a bench, set the thermostat to about 18 degrees, and stand trays and pots on the warm bench: seedlings germinate and cuttings root while the air above stays cool. It uses far less electricity than heating the whole greenhouse, and the plants are stronger for it.

6

Wash the Glass for Every Ray of Light

Idea 6 of 30

Winter light is weak and short, and dirty glass can steal a third of it. Wash the inside and outside of every pane in late autumn with warm soapy water, clearing algae, dust and the summer shading you no longer need. Clean glass lets in noticeably more light, which is what winter salads and overwintering plants need most. While you are at it, check for cracked panes and loose clips before the first storm.

7

Ventilate on Sunny Winter Days

Idea 7 of 30

A closed greenhouse in winter sun can hit 25 degrees by midday, then plunge below freezing at night — and that swing is what kills plants and breeds grey mould. Open a roof vent and the door on sunny days, even in January, to let damp air out and keep the temperature even. Close everything before dusk so the day's warmth stays trapped overnight, and never ventilate on a frosty, windy day.

8

Damping Down the Paths for Humidity

Idea 8 of 30

Damping down — wetting the paths and staging with a watering can — raises the humidity inside a greenhouse, which stops tender leaves drying out in heated air. Do it in the morning, so the floor dries before nightfall, and never damp down on a cold, damp day when the air is already wet. In an unheated greenhouse you rarely need it; in a heated one, it is the difference between crisp leaves and scorched ones.

9

Water Little, and Only in the Morning

Idea 9 of 30

Winter plants in a greenhouse need far less water than summer ones: the air is cool, growth is slow, and wet compost in the cold rots roots. Water little, in the morning only, so the leaves and the surface dry before nightfall, and always let the pot drain. Check with your finger before you water — if the top two centimetres feel damp, leave it. Overwatering is the commonest way to kill a greenhouse plant in winter.

10

Winter Salad Leaves in Greenhouse Beds

Idea 10 of 30

A greenhouse bed keeps winter salads growing when the garden is bare: sow lettuce, rocket, spinach, mizuna and lamb's lettuce in autumn, and pick the outer leaves all winter. The glass keeps the frost off and the rain away, so the leaves stay clean and crisp. Sow little and often, keep the soil just damp, and cover the bed with fleece on the coldest nights for an extra few degrees.

11

Herbs That Keep Growing Under Glass

Idea 11 of 30

Parsley, chives, mint, thyme and winter savory all keep growing in a greenhouse through winter, giving you fresh herbs when the garden has none. Grow them in pots on the staging, where they are easy to reach and easy to move, and pick little and often so the plants stay bushy. Keep them on the dry side — wet herbs in a cold greenhouse rot — and cut mint back hard in late winter before it takes over.

12

Overwinter Tender Plants on the Bench

Idea 12 of 30

Pelargoniums, fuchsias, dahlias in pots and tender cuttings all overwinter happily on a greenhouse bench, safe from frost but cool enough to rest. Cut them back by a third, keep them just damp through winter, and give them the brightest spot you have. A cool rest is what they need — a warm greenhouse forces soft growth that frost kills — so aim for frost-free, not warm, and they will burst back into growth in spring.

13

Staging and Shelving That Earns Its Space

Idea 13 of 30

Good staging doubles the growing space in a greenhouse: a slatted bench keeps air moving under the pots, and a shelf above holds trays of seedlings while taller plants grow below. Keep the bench clear of clutter, leave gaps for air, and put the plants that need the most light at the top. In winter, raise the staging off the cold floor with bricks or blocks, so the pots never sit in the coldest air.

14

A Potting Bench That Works All Winter

Idea 14 of 30

A potting bench inside the greenhouse means you can sow, pot on and take cuttings out of the wind all winter. Keep a bag of compost, clean pots and a hand fork there, and a bucket of water so you are not carrying cans through the rain. A dry, sheltered bench also keeps seed packets and labels from going damp, and it turns a cold greenhouse into a workshop you actually use.

15

Grey Mould: Spot It Early, Act Fast

Idea 15 of 30

Grey mould is the winter greenhouse disease: a soft grey fuzz on dead leaves and stems that spreads fast in damp, still air. Check your plants weekly, pick off dead leaves at once, and throw them in the bin — never on the compost heap. Ventilate on sunny days, space plants so air moves between them, and water in the morning. A plant caught early usually recovers; a plant ignored can lose the whole bench.

16

Aphids in Winter: Check Under the Leaves

Idea 16 of 30

Aphids do not stop in winter — they hide in a heated greenhouse, breeding on the soft new growth. Check under the leaves of your overwintering plants every week, and squash small colonies by hand or wash them off with soapy water. In an unheated greenhouse a cold snap usually finishes them, but a heated one needs watching all winter. Never use a strong spray on a plant you plan to eat.

17

Red Spider Mite in a Dry Greenhouse

Idea 17 of 30

Red spider mite loves a hot, dry greenhouse, and a heated one in winter suits it perfectly. The signs are fine yellow speckling on the leaves and tiny webs underneath. Damp the paths down in the morning, mist the leaves of non-edible plants, and keep the air moving — mites hate damp air. If you find them, wash the plant well and isolate it from its neighbours before they spread.

18

Raise Pots Off the Cold Floor

Idea 18 of 30

A greenhouse floor is the coldest place in the building: cold air sinks, and a concrete floor sucks heat out of any pot standing on it. Raise pots on bricks, slats or a bench, so air flows underneath and the roots stay a few degrees warmer. It also stops pots sitting in puddles after watering, which rots roots in winter. A pot on the floor in January is a pot at risk.

19

Fleece and Horticultural Wrap Inside the Greenhouse

Idea 19 of 30

One layer of fleece inside the greenhouse is worth several degrees on a frosty night, and it costs almost nothing. Drape horticultural fleece over the bench before dusk, tuck it around the pots, and lift it in the morning so the plants get light. It works as a second skin inside the glass, protecting tender plants while the greenhouse itself stays just frost-free. Keep the fleece dry — wet fleece freezes to the leaves.

20

Grow Lights for Short Winter Days

Idea 20 of 30

In December the days are so short that even a bright greenhouse cannot give seedlings enough light, and they grow leggy and weak. A grow light hung low over the bench, on for twelve to fourteen hours a day, keeps seedlings short and strong. LED lights are cheap to run and give off little heat, so hang them close to the plants. Turn them off at night — plants need darkness as much as light.

21

Greenhouse-Sown Trays Under Cover

Idea 21 of 30

A greenhouse is a fine place to sow seeds in late winter: trays under cover get more light than a windowsill and stay frost-free. Sow in fine seed compost, cover the trays with a clear lid or fleece, and stand them on the warmest bench. Check daily — the lid keeps the compost damp, so water rarely — and prick seedlings out into individual pots before they get leggy. It is the greenhouse way to start the spring early.

22

Finished Compost in Greenhouse Pots

Idea 22 of 30

A handful of finished compost in a greenhouse pot is the gentlest feed there is: it adds nutrients, holds moisture, and brings in the microbes that keep the compost healthy. Mix it into the top of pots holding overwintering plants, or use it to top-dress salad beds between sowings. Never use unfinished compost in a pot — it steals nitrogen and can burn roots — and never use garden soil, which is too heavy and too cold.

23

A Month-by-Month Winter Greenhouse Calendar

Idea 23 of 30

A winter greenhouse calendar keeps the work in order: in December, insulate, clean the glass and check the heater; in January, sow the first salads, check for mould and ventilate on sunny days; in February, sow tomatoes and peppers under cover, prick out seedlings and plan the spring. A simple list on the greenhouse door stops the big jobs being forgotten, and it reminds you what to do on a dark afternoon when the garden is asleep.

24

Do Not Seal the Greenhouse Shut

Idea 24 of 30

Sealing a greenhouse shut all winter feels sensible, but it is a mistake: still, damp air breeds grey mould and pests, and plants suffocate without fresh air. Open a vent or the door on every mild, dry day, even for an hour, and let the air change. A greenhouse needs to breathe, even in January — the plants inside are alive, and they need moving air as much as they need light and water.

25

Do Not Overwater in Winter

Idea 25 of 30

Overwatering is the commonest winter greenhouse mistake, and it kills more plants than frost. In cold, slow growth, a pot that stays wet for weeks rots the roots, and the plant collapses without warning. Water little, let the pot drain, and check the compost with your finger before you water again. If in doubt, wait a day — a dry plant revives with a drink, but a rotted plant never comes back.

26

Do Not Heat More Than You Need

Idea 26 of 30

Heating a greenhouse to summer temperatures all winter wastes money and grows soft, leggy plants that collapse in spring. Most winter plants only need frost-free air — about 5 degrees — and a little warmth at night. Set the thermostat low, heat only the bench or the plants that need it, and let the rest rest cold. A cool greenhouse grows tougher plants, and the heating bill stays small.

27

Snow on the Roof: Clear It or Leave It?

Idea 27 of 30

Snow on a greenhouse roof blocks the light, and a heavy fall can strain the glass — but clearing it is not always the answer. Light snow melts off a heated greenhouse on its own, and snow on an unheated roof insulates the glass against a hard frost. Clear it when it is deep or wet and heavy, using a soft brush from the ground, and never climb on the roof or strike the glass with a hard tool.

28

A Thermometer That Tells the Truth

Idea 28 of 30

A max-min thermometer is the most useful tool in a winter greenhouse: it records the coldest night and the hottest day, so you know whether your heater is working and whether the vents need opening. Hang it at plant height, out of direct sun, and check it every morning. If the night low keeps touching zero, add insulation or heat; if the day high keeps soaring, ventilate more.

29

Citrus and Tender Fruit Under Glass

Idea 29 of 30

A citrus tree in a pot is the classic greenhouse winter guest: it hates frost but loves the cool, bright air under glass. Keep it frost-free, water sparingly, and give it the sunniest spot in the greenhouse. Feed it weakly in late winter as growth restarts, and watch for scale insects on the leaves. In spring, move it outside after the last frost — a citrus that winters well under glass fruits far better the next year.

30

Use the Greenhouse as a Spring Cold Frame

Idea 30 of 30

In spring, the greenhouse doubles as a cold frame: open the vents wide, stand trays of seedlings on the floor, and let them feel the day's air while the glass still shelters them at night. Over two weeks, move them closer to the door, then outside for the day and back in at night. It is the gentlest way to harden plants off, and it uses the building you already have.

Key Takeaways

  • An unheated greenhouse buys about one hardiness zone — know what it can and cannot protect.
  • Bubble wrap on the inside glass and fleece over the bench are the cheapest winter warmth.
  • Ventilate on sunny days and water little, in the morning — damp, still air breeds grey mould.
  • Heat only to frost-free, about 5 degrees, and heat the bench or the roots, not the whole building.
  • Winter salads, herbs and overwintering tender plants are the crops an unheated greenhouse does best.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to heat my greenhouse in winter?

Not always. An unheated greenhouse suits hardy salads, herbs and overwintering tender plants, and it buys roughly one hardiness zone of protection. Heat it only if you grow tender crops or keep citrus and tropical plants, and then only to about 5 degrees. A small fan heater on a thermostat, used on the coldest nights, is usually enough.

How do I insulate a greenhouse for winter?

Pin bubble wrap to the inside of the glass in autumn, leaving gaps at the vents and door for air. It can keep the inside several degrees warmer on a frosty night. Add fleece over the bench on cold nights, and raise pots off the cold floor. Take the bubble wrap down in spring, because it costs the plants light.

What can I grow in an unheated greenhouse in winter?

Winter salads are the best crop: lettuce, rocket, spinach, mizuna, lamb's lettuce and winter herbs all grow slowly but steadily under glass. You can also overwinter tender plants like pelargoniums, fuchsias and dahlias in pots, and sow the first seeds in late winter. Tender summer crops need heat, so save those for spring.

How often should I water a greenhouse in winter?

Far less than in summer. Water little, in the morning only, and only when the top two centimetres of compost feel dry. Cold, slow-growing plants in damp compost rot quickly, so let pots drain and never leave them standing in water. Check with your finger before every watering, and if in doubt, wait a day.

Why is there condensation inside my greenhouse?

Condensation forms when warm, damp air meets cold glass, and it is worst on still, cold nights after watering. Ventilate on sunny days, water in the morning, and space plants so air moves between them. Wipe the glass if drips fall on the plants, because wet leaves invite grey mould. A little condensation is normal in winter.

How do I stop grey mould in a greenhouse?

Grey mould loves damp, still air, so ventilate on every mild day, space plants for airflow, and water in the morning. Pick off dead leaves at once and bin them, never compost them. Keep the greenhouse clean, and do not overcrowd the bench. A plant caught early usually recovers; left alone, mould can take the whole bench.

When should I start sowing seeds in the greenhouse?

Start in late winter, from February in most regions, when the light is returning. Sow hardy salads and broad beans first, then tomatoes, peppers and chillies under cover, on a warm bench or with soil-warming cables. Check your last frost date: seedlings started too early grow leggy in the weak light and are hard to keep alive.

Can I use my greenhouse as a cold frame in spring?

Yes — it is one of its best uses. Open the vents wide, stand seedling trays on the floor, and let them feel the day's air while the glass shelters them at night. Over two weeks, move them nearer the door, then outside by day and back in at night. It hardens plants off, using the building you already have.
