30 Ways to Freeze Fresh Herbs

Herb GardensUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Freezing is the fastest way to save summer flavour for winter: a tray of basil cubes or a bag of frozen rosemary turns a plain January stew into a garden supper. These 30 ideas cover every part of freezing fresh herbs — water and olive oil cubes, herb butter, flat bags, vacuum sealing, blanching, flash-freezing whole leaves, the herbs that freeze best and the ones that go limp, and storage that keeps flavour for months. The main harvest is over by December, but in mild hardiness zones 8 to 11 you can still cut herbs from the garden, while in cold zones 3 to 6 the garden crop is done and fresh shop bunches are the way to fill your freezer — so check your hardiness zone and your last frost date before you start.

1

Freeze Herbs in Water Ice Cubes

Idea 1 of 30

Water cubes are the simplest way to freeze herbs, and they work for almost every herb you cook with. Chop the leaves, pack them loosely into an ice cube tray, cover with water, and freeze. Pop the cubes into a bag once they are solid, and drop one straight into soups, stews and sauces all winter. Water cubes suit parsley, mint, dill and coriander best, and they keep for six months.

2

Olive Oil Cubes for Basil and Parsley

Idea 2 of 30

Olive oil cubes are the answer for herbs that blacken in water, and basil is the classic case. Chop the leaves, pack them into an ice cube tray, and cover with olive oil instead of water. The oil seals out air, so the basil stays green, and the cube melts straight into a pan for pasta, pizza and roast vegetables. Parsley freezes just as well this way.

3

Herb Butter Cubes for Roasts

Idea 3 of 30

Herb butter is a roast dinner waiting to happen: mash soft butter with chopped rosemary, thyme, sage or garlic, spoon it into an ice cube tray, and freeze. A cube melted over roast chicken, steak or winter vegetables gives you instant flavour, and a knob stirred into mashed potato or pasta does the same. Wrap the cubes well so they do not pick up freezer smells.

4

Flat Freezer Bags, Labelled and Stacked

Idea 4 of 30

Flat bags are the neatest way to store chopped herbs, and they thaw faster than any other method. Chop the herbs, spoon them into a freezer bag, squeeze out the air, and lay the bag flat to freeze. Once solid, the bags stack like books and take up almost no room. Label each bag with the herb and the date, and break off a corner whenever you need a spoonful.

5

Vacuum-Sealed Bags for Long Storage

Idea 5 of 30

A vacuum sealer takes the air out of the bag, and air is what causes freezer burn and flavour loss. Seal whole sprigs or chopped herbs in vacuum bags, and they keep their colour and flavour for up to a year — far longer than ordinary bags. It is the best method if you freeze a big harvest at once. Mark each bag with the herb, the date and the weight.

6

Blanch First for Bright Green Herbs

Idea 6 of 30

Blanching keeps green herbs bright: drop parsley, basil, dill or chives into boiling water for ten to twenty seconds, then straight into iced water to stop the cooking. The quick scald fixes the green colour and kills the enzymes that turn herbs brown in the freezer. Pat the leaves dry, pack them into trays or bags, and freeze the same day.

7

Freeze Raw for Woody Herbs

Idea 7 of 30

Woody herbs need no blanching at all, because their tough leaves hold up to freezing on their own. Strip rosemary, thyme, sage and bay from their stems or freeze them as whole sprigs, raw, in a labelled bag. They thaw soft and dark, but the flavour is all there. Use them in roasts, stews and stocks, where texture does not matter and the oils do the work.

8

Flash-Freeze Whole Leaves on a Tray

Idea 8 of 30

Flash-freezing stops leaves clumping into one solid lump, which is the curse of freezing herbs loose in a bag. Spread whole leaves or sprigs in a single layer on a baking tray, freeze for an hour until they are hard, then tip them into a bag. The leaves stay separate, so you can shake out exactly what you need, a few leaves at a time, all winter.

9

Chop and Freeze in Teaspoon Portions

Idea 9 of 30

Teaspoon portions take the measuring out of winter cooking: chop the herbs, spoon them into teaspoon-sized mounds on a tray or into a mini muffin tin, and freeze until solid. Bag the frozen portions, and each one is a ready measure for a pan of sauce or a pot of soup. It works best for parsley, coriander, dill and chives, the herbs you use by the spoonful.

10

Herb Paste Cubes for Quick Cooking

Idea 10 of 30

Herb paste is a flavour bomb for busy weeknights: blitz parsley, coriander, basil or mint with a little oil and water into a thick paste, spoon it into an ice cube tray, and freeze. A cube stirred into a curry, a stew or a pan of vegetables melts in seconds and tastes freshly made. Label the tray, because every green paste looks the same once it is frozen.

11

Freeze Herbs in Stock for Winter Soups

Idea 11 of 30

Stock cubes with herbs built in are a winter soup in one step: chop parsley, thyme, sage or bay, spoon the herbs into an ice cube tray, and cover with cooled stock instead of water. Freeze, bag, and drop a cube or two into a pot whenever you make soup, stew or risotto. The stock carries the herbs through the dish, and nothing is wasted.

12

Freeze Herbs in Wine for Pan Sauces

Idea 12 of 30

Wine cubes turn a plain pan sauce into something worth serving: pack thyme, rosemary or sage into an ice cube tray and cover with red or white wine. Freeze, bag, and drop a cube into the pan when you deglaze after frying meat or roasting vegetables. The wine adds depth, the herbs add scent, and the cube melts in a minute. Use cheap wine — the cooking does the rest.

13

Freeze Basil Before It Blackens

Idea 13 of 30

Basil is the herb most likely to turn black in the freezer, because its soft, wet leaves bruise and oxidise easily. Freeze it fast and keep air away from it: blanch it briefly, or pack it in olive oil cubes, or flash-freeze whole leaves on a tray. Never freeze basil loose in a bag, where it crushes and darkens. Even blackened basil still tastes of basil in a cooked sauce.

14

Freeze Mint for Teas and Desserts

Idea 14 of 30

Mint freezes beautifully, and it keeps its scent for months, which makes it the easiest herb for winter teas and puddings. Freeze whole leaves in water cubes for drinks, or chop the leaves and freeze them in oil for cooking. Drop a water cube into hot water for instant mint tea, or into a jug of iced water in summer. One summer plant frozen this way fills a bag that lasts until spring.

15

Freeze Rosemary in Whole Sprigs

Idea 15 of 30

Rosemary is one of the best herbs for freezing, because its woody sprigs and needle leaves hold their oils for months. Freeze whole sprigs raw in a labelled bag, and strip the needles off after thawing by running your fingers down the stem. Drop a sprig whole into a roast or a stew, and fish it out before serving. Rosemary frozen this way keeps its flavour for a full year.

16

Freeze Thyme Leaves Stripped from Stems

Idea 16 of 30

Thyme freezes fast because its tiny leaves hold little water, and it keeps its flavour better than almost any herb. Strip the leaves from the stems before freezing — they come off cleanly over a bowl — and freeze them loose in a bag, so you can shake out a pinch at a time. Thyme frozen this way goes straight into soups, stews, roasts and stuffing all winter.

17

Freeze Oregano for Pizza and Pasta

Idea 17 of 30

Oregano is at its most flavourful when it is frozen, because the cold locks in the oils that drying can dull. Chop the leaves or freeze them whole in a bag, and crumble them over pizza, pasta and roast vegetables straight from the freezer. A single plant frozen in summer seasons a winter's worth of suppers, and the flavour stays strong for six months or more.

18

Freeze Sage Leaves for Stuffing

Idea 18 of 30

Sage freezes well because its thick, grey-green leaves hold little water and lots of oil. Pick the leaves in the morning, freeze them whole in a bag or in oil cubes, and use them in stuffing, roast pork and winter squash dishes. Whole leaves keep their flavour far longer than chopped ones. Crumble a leaf or two into the pan, and one leaf seasons a whole dish.

19

Freeze Parsley Chopped for Soups

Idea 19 of 30

Parsley freezes well chopped, and it is the herb you will reach for most in winter cooking. Blanch it briefly to keep the colour bright, chop it fine, and freeze it in water cubes or flat bags. Drop a cube into soups, stews and sauces, or stir a spoonful into mashed potato. Parsley loses its fresh bite when frozen, so use it generously in cooked dishes.

20

Freeze Dill for Fish Dishes

Idea 20 of 30

Dill has two harvests: the feathery leaves for freezing, and the seeds for pickling. Cut the leaves before the plant flowers, when the flavour is strongest, and freeze them chopped in water cubes or flat bags. The leaves freeze in a few hours and keep for six months. Use them in fish dishes, potato salads, pickles and creamy sauces all winter.

21

Freeze Chives in Small Snipped Batches

Idea 21 of 30

Chives freeze fast because the hollow stems hold little water, but they lose their onion bite quicker than most herbs, so freeze them in small batches and use them up within a few months. Snip the stems into short pieces, freeze them in teaspoon portions or small bags, and sprinkle them over soups, baked potatoes and scrambled eggs straight from the freezer.

22

Freeze Coriander for Curries and Salsas

Idea 22 of 30

Coriander is the herb that divides kitchens, but if you love it, freezing is the way to keep it through winter. Chop the leaves and freeze them in water cubes or as a paste with a little oil, and stir a cube into curries, soups and salsas. The flavour survives freezing far better than drying, and a cube melts into a hot dish in seconds.

23

Label Every Bag with Herb and Date

Idea 23 of 30

Every frozen herb looks the same in a bag six months later, so label everything the day you pack it: the herb name, the method if it matters, and the month you froze it. A strip of masking tape and a pen is all you need. Frozen herbs keep three to six months at their best, and the date tells you which bag to use first — and which one is past its best.

24

How Long Frozen Herbs Really Last

Idea 24 of 30

Frozen herbs do not go off, but they do go flat: the flavour fades a little every month. Most herbs are at their best for three to six months and still usable at twelve, though basil, mint and chives fade fastest and rosemary, thyme and bay last longest. Taste a pinch after thawing — if the flavour is faint, the herb is done, and it is time to use twice as much or throw it out.

25

Stop Freezer Burn with Double Wrapping

Idea 25 of 30

Freezer burn is dried-out, flavourless patches where air has reached the herbs, and it ruins a bag in weeks. Stop it by wrapping twice: pack the herbs in a small bag, squeeze the air out, then put that bag inside a second bag, or wrap portions in cling film before bagging. Keep the freezer at minus eighteen, and do not open bags and refreeze them.

26

Thaw Frozen Herbs Before Using

Idea 26 of 30

Most frozen herbs need no thawing at all — they go straight into hot food, where they melt in seconds. Thaw them only for cold dishes like dips, dressings and salsas, and then thaw a portion in a small bowl in the fridge, never on the worktop. Never refreeze herbs once they have thawed, and use thawed herbs the same day, because they go limp fast.

27

What Never to Freeze

Idea 27 of 30

Some herbs are not worth freezing, and knowing which saves you a bag of mush. Do not freeze herbs you want to eat fresh — a frozen basil leaf will never garnish a salad — and do not freeze delicate herbs like salad burnet or young salad leaves, which turn to slime. Never freeze herbs that have already thawed, and never freeze a herb in water if you plan to fry it.

28

Drop Frozen Cubes Straight into the Pot

Idea 28 of 30

The golden rule of frozen herbs is simple: do not thaw them, just drop them in. A water, oil or stock cube melts into a simmering pot in a minute, and the herbs cook in the steam without going limp. This is why cubes beat loose bags for soups, stews and sauces. Keep the cubes near the top of the freezer, so you can grab one with one hand while you stir.

29

Frozen Herbs for Winter Roasts and Stews

Idea 29 of 30

Frozen herbs shine in long, slow cooking, where their flavour has time to melt into the dish. Drop frozen rosemary and thyme into a roast, frozen sage into stuffing, and frozen parsley and bay into a stew, and the whole pot tastes of the garden. Roasts and stews are forgiving: the herbs go soft, but the oils do the work, and nobody misses the fresh texture.

30

Freezing vs Drying: Which Herb, Which Method

Idea 30 of 30

Freezing and drying suit different herbs, and picking the right method is half the job. Freeze soft, wet herbs — basil, parsley, mint, dill and coriander — where freezing keeps the fresh flavour that drying would dull. Dry woody herbs — rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage and bay — where drying concentrates the oils. When in doubt, freeze a little of each and taste them in January.

Key Takeaways

  • Freeze tender herbs in oil or water cubes; woody herbs freeze well raw or whole.
  • Blanching keeps green herbs bright — skip it for rosemary, thyme, sage and bay.
  • Label every bag with herb and date, and use frozen herbs within six months.
  • Drop frozen herbs straight into hot cooking — thawing makes them limp.
  • December freezing works in mild zones from the garden, and everywhere from fresh shop bunches.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which herbs freeze best?

Woody herbs freeze best: rosemary, thyme, sage and bay hold their flavour for a year, frozen raw as whole sprigs or leaves. Soft herbs like basil, mint, parsley, dill and coriander freeze well too, but only in oil or water cubes, or as a paste. Chives freeze, but lose their bite fastest, so use them within a few months.

Do I need to blanch herbs before freezing?

Blanch tender green herbs for ten to twenty seconds to keep their colour bright: parsley, basil, dill and chives all benefit. Woody herbs need no blanching at all — rosemary, thyme, sage and bay freeze raw, as whole sprigs or stripped leaves. If you skip blanching, freeze fast and keep air out, or the herbs darken.

How long do frozen herbs keep?

Most frozen herbs are at their best for three to six months and still usable at twelve, though the flavour fades a little every month. Rosemary, thyme and bay last longest; basil, mint and chives fade fastest. Freeze in small portions, label every bag with herb and date, and use the oldest bag first.

Can I freeze herbs in water or oil?

Yes, both work, and the choice depends on the dish. Water cubes suit soups, stews and teas, and work for parsley, mint, dill and coriander. Oil cubes suit frying, roasting and pasta, and are best for basil and parsley, which blacken in water. Butter cubes are the third option, for roasts and vegetables.

Why did my frozen basil go black?

Basil blackens because its soft leaves bruise and oxidise, and slow freezing makes it worse. Freeze basil fast, keep air away, and use oil cubes or a brief blanch instead of water. Pack leaves gently, never crush them into a bag, and freeze the same day you pick them. Blackened basil still tastes fine in a cooked sauce.

Can I freeze herbs I bought from the shop?

Yes — fresh bunches from the shop freeze just as well as garden herbs, and in December they are often the best option, when the garden harvest is over. Check the bunches are fresh and dry, not damp or wilting, and freeze them the same day. The same rules apply: freeze fast, keep air out, and label everything.

Should I thaw frozen herbs before cooking?

No — drop frozen herbs straight into hot food, where they melt in seconds and keep their flavour. Thaw them only for cold dishes like dips, dressings and salsas, and thaw in the fridge, never on the worktop. Never refreeze herbs once they have thawed, and use thawed herbs the same day, because they go limp fast.

Can frozen herbs replace fresh herbs?

In cooking, yes — frozen herbs work in soups, stews, sauces, roasts and curries, where they melt in and the flavour carries the dish. They cannot replace fresh herbs for garnish or salads, where frozen herbs go limp and dark. Use frozen herbs for heat, fresh herbs for the plate, and dried herbs for woody herbs like rosemary and thyme.
