30 January Garden Planning Ideas

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

January looks like the quietest month in the garden, and it is the most important. These 30 ideas cover the whole new-year job list: what to sow indoors, what to prune, how to plan crop rotation and succession sowing, and how to start seeds for spring — with the hardiness zone and last frost date checked first, because the right job in January depends on where you garden. You will find seed starting for chillies, peppers and aubergines, microgreens on a windowsill, bare-root fruit, compost and soil care, watering evergreens in dry spells, and a garden journal and sowing calendar to carry the plan through the year. Do the planning now, and the harvest in summer will follow it.

1

Sketch Your 2027 Garden Plan on Paper

Idea 1 of 30

January is the best month to plan, because the garden is quiet and you can see its bones. Draw each bed on paper, mark the sunny and shady spots, and note what grew where last year. A plan made now stops you buying the wrong seeds in spring, and it is the first step in crop rotation — never grow the same family in the same bed two years running.

2

Order Seeds from Catalogues Early

Idea 2 of 30

January is seed catalogue month. Pour over the catalogues, make a list, and order early — popular varieties sell out. Check the packet dates, and compost any seed that is too old to sow. Order a few new varieties to try, but keep most of your list to plants you know grow well in your soil and sunlight.

3

Inventory Seeds and Compost Old Ones

Idea 3 of 30

Tip every seed packet out, check the dates, and sort them by sowing month. Most vegetable seed stays viable for two to three years if it is kept cool and dry, but old seed germinates poorly. Compost the packets that are out of date, and write a short shopping list for the gaps — it is cheaper than buying the same seed twice.

4

Start Chillies and Peppers Indoors

Idea 4 of 30

Chillies and peppers need a long growing season, so start them indoors in January in warmth — around 20 to 25 degrees — with good light. Sow in seed trays or small pots, cover with a clear lid until they germinate, and give them a sunny windowsill or a grow light. They cannot go outside until after the last frost, so do not rush them.

5

Start Aubergines Indoors in Warmth

Idea 5 of 30

Aubergines also need a long, warm season, so sow them indoors in January or February in a heated propagator or a warm room. They germinate slowly in the cold, so keep the tray covered and warm, and pot seedlings on as soon as they have two true leaves. In mild climates they can go out in spring; in cold climates they need a greenhouse.

6

Sow Tomatoes for the Greenhouse

Idea 6 of 30

Sow cordon tomatoes indoors in January only if you have a heated greenhouse to grow them on in — otherwise wait until February or March. Sow in a warm, bright spot, and pot them on into larger pots as they grow. Tomatoes need warmth, sunlight and regular watering, and they cannot tolerate frost at any stage.

7

Sow Leeks for Spring Planting

Idea 7 of 30

Sow leeks in January in trays indoors, and they will be ready to plant out in spring. Leeks need a long season and a deep, compost-rich soil to grow tall and white. Sow thinly, keep the tray moist, and plant the seedlings out when they are pencil-thick, dropping them into deep holes and watering them in.

8

Grow Microgreens on a Windowsill

Idea 8 of 30

Microgreens are the fastest January crop — mustard, radish, methi and sunflower sprouts are ready in 7 to 10 days on a sunny windowsill. Sow thickly in a shallow tray of potting mix, keep it moist, and cut the shoots with scissors when they are a few centimetres tall. They are the easiest way to grow fresh food in the coldest month.

9

Sow Spinach and Lettuce Indoors

Idea 9 of 30

In mild climates, sow spinach, lettuce and Asian greens indoors in January, and they will give you salads weeks before the outdoor season starts. Use a free-draining potting mix with compost, give them as much sunlight as you can, and water gently. In cold climates, sow them under cover and plant out only after the last frost.

10

Chit Potatoes in a Bright Cool Room

Idea 10 of 30

Chitting — sprouting seed potatoes before planting — starts in January. Stand the tubers in egg boxes, eyes up, in a bright, cool, frost-free room, and short, sturdy green shoots will appear in a few weeks. Chitted potatoes grow faster and crop earlier than unchitted ones, and the shoots should be short and green, never long and white.

11

Prune Apple and Pear Trees While Dormant

Idea 11 of 30

Prune free-standing apple and pear trees while they are dormant, between November and early March. Cut out dead, damaged and crossing branches first, then open the centre so light and air reach the fruit. Always cut to an outward-facing bud, clean your secateurs between trees, and never prune in frost or when the wood is frozen.

12

Plant Bare-Root Fruit Trees and Bushes

Idea 12 of 30

January is a good month to plant bare-root fruit trees and bushes, as long as the soil is not frozen or waterlogged. Soak the roots for an hour first, dig a hole wide enough to spread them out, and plant at the same depth as the nursery mark. Water well, mulch with compost, and stake the tree for its first year.

13

Plan Crop Rotation for the New Year

Idea 13 of 30

Crop rotation is the single best thing you can plan in January: divide your beds into four groups — roots, brassicas, legumes and potatoes or squash — and move each group to a new bed every year. Rotation keeps the soil healthy, confuses pests, and stops diseases building up. Write the plan down, or you will forget it by spring.

14

Plan Succession Sowing for All Season

Idea 14 of 30

Succession sowing means sowing a little, often, instead of everything at once. Plan to sow lettuce, radishes and beans every two weeks from spring to autumn, so you always have something fresh to pick. As soon as one crop finishes, sow the next in its place — one bed can give three harvests a year if you plan it now.

15

Clean Pots, Tools and the Greenhouse

Idea 15 of 30

January is the month to clean everything before the season starts. Scrub pots in hot soapy water, oil wooden tool handles, sharpen secateurs, and wash greenhouse glass inside and out so more sunlight reaches your seedlings. Clean tools spread fewer diseases, and clean glass can add hours of light a day.

16

Top-Dress Beds with Compost

Idea 16 of 30

Top-dress your beds with a 5 cm layer of mature compost in January, and the worms will pull it down into the soil by spring. Compost feeds the soil, not just the plants — it holds water, improves drainage and feeds the microbes that make nutrients available. Never dig frozen soil, and never use fresh manure on a bed you will plant soon.

17

Check Winter Protection and Mulch

Idea 17 of 30

Walk the garden in January and check every plant that needs protection. Top up mulch around tender plants, check fleece and cloches after wind and rain, and knock heavy snow off evergreens and polytunnels so branches do not snap. In a dry spell, water evergreens — they still lose water in winter, even when they look dormant.

18

Water Evergreens in Dry Spells

Idea 18 of 30

Evergreens, conifers and rhododendrons keep transpiring all winter, so in a dry January they can die of thirst while the rest of the garden sleeps. Water them on a mild day when the soil is not frozen, and mulch around the roots to hold the moisture in. Container evergreens dry out fastest, so check their pots first.

19

Harvest Leeks, Kale and Winter Greens

Idea 19 of 30

January is a harvesting month, not just a planning month. Pick leeks as you need them, cut kale leaves from the outside in, and harvest winter spinach, chard and Asian greens before a hard frost. In mild climates, sweet peas, calendula and pansies keep blooming, and roses can be pruned towards the end of the month.

20

Sow Sweet Peas Indoors Under Cover

Idea 20 of 30

Sow sweet peas indoors in January in root trainers or deep pots, and they will flower weeks earlier than spring-sown plants. Soak the seed overnight first, sow one seed per pot, and keep them in a cool, bright place — sweet peas like it cool, not warm. Pinch out the tips when they reach 10 cm, and they will bush out.

21

Sow Pansies and Violas for Spring Colour

Idea 21 of 30

Pansies and violas sown in January give colour in spring, and they are some of the hardiest winter flowers. Sow in trays indoors, keep them cool and bright, and plant them out when the soil can be worked. They flower through most winters in pots, and deadheading keeps them flowering for months.

22

Prune Roses in Mild Climates

Idea 22 of 30

In mild climates, prune roses towards the end of January for a strong flush of flowers in February and spring. In cold climates, wait until the last frosts have passed. Cut out dead wood first, then cut back to an outward-facing bud, and clear fallen leaves from the base — they can carry disease into the new season.

23

Start a Garden Journal and Sowing Calendar

Idea 23 of 30

Start a garden journal in January and you will thank yourself in July. Write down what you sow, when you sow it, where you plant it and how it grows, with the weather and the first and last frost dates. A sowing calendar made now — month by month, with hardiness zones and succession dates — is the cheapest tool a gardener owns.

24

Check Hardiness Zone and Last Frost Date

Idea 24 of 30

Before you sow anything in January, check two things: your hardiness zone and your last frost date. The hardiness zone tells you which plants survive your winter, and the last frost date tells you when tender plants can go outside. Both vary by place, so look them up for your own garden — a date that works in one town can be a month wrong in another.

25

Plan Companion Planting Pairs

Idea 25 of 30

Plan your companion planting pairs in January, while you still have time to buy the right seed. Tomatoes with basil, carrots with onions, beans before squash, and marigolds along every edge to confuse pests are proven combinations. Keep it simple — three or four pairs per bed — and skip the folklore. Good companions mean fewer chemicals and healthier soil.

26

Build or Repair a Raised Bed

Idea 26 of 30

January is the month to build or repair a raised bed, while it is empty. Replace rotten boards — cedar lasts eight to ten years — check the corners, and refill with a loose, compost-rich mix. A bed 1.2 metres wide is the right width: you can reach the middle from either side without stepping on the soil and compacting it.

27

Plan a Pollinator Border for Spring

Idea 27 of 30

Plan a pollinator border now, so you can order the seed in time for spring. A 30 cm border of borage, calendula, nasturtiums and lavender around your vegetable beds brings bees every day, and more pollinators mean better fruit set on beans, squash and cucumbers. Leave a few flowers to seed, and the border returns by itself next year.

28

Sow Herbs Indoors: Parsley, Chives, Coriander

Idea 28 of 30

Sow parsley, chives and coriander indoors in January, and you will have fresh herbs weeks before the outdoor season. Parsley germinates slowly — soak the seed overnight first — chives are almost foolproof, and coriander likes it cool. Keep harvesting mint, thyme and oregano outside, and protect basil from cold by bringing its pot indoors at night.

29

Plan Watering and Drip Irrigation

Idea 29 of 30

Plan your watering now, while the beds are empty. A drip line along each row waters slowly at the roots, uses far less water than a hose, keeps leaves dry so disease stays low, and works while you are away. Sketch where the lines will run, check your water butt and rain barrel, and order the fittings before spring.

30

Set Three Garden Goals for the New Year

Idea 30 of 30

Set three garden goals for the new year, and write them down. One goal for food — a vegetable you have never grown. One goal for wildlife — a pollinator border, a pond or a bird box. One goal for yourself — a seat in the sun, a cutting bed, or ten minutes in the garden every day. Three small goals, kept, beat a long list that is forgotten by March.

Key Takeaways

  • January is a planning month: the plan you make now decides the harvest you get in summer.
  • Start only long-season seeds indoors in January — chillies, peppers, aubergines, leeks and onions — and wait for the rest.
  • Check your hardiness zone and last frost date before you sow or plant anything out.
  • Rotate your crops every year: roots, brassicas, legumes and potatoes each move to a new bed.
  • Grow microgreens on a windowsill — they are ready in 7 to 10 days, and they are the fastest food January gives you.

Frequently Asked Questions

What can I plant in January?

It depends on your climate. In mild climates you can sow spinach, lettuce, radish, peas and broad beans outdoors, and start tomatoes, peppers and aubergines indoors. In cold climates, January is mostly for indoor sowing — microgreens, leeks, onions and sweet peas — and for planning. Check your hardiness zone and last frost date first, and never plant out tender plants before the frosts pass.

What seeds should I start indoors in January?

Start seeds that need a long season: chillies, peppers, aubergines, leeks, onions, celeriac and sweet peas. In warm zones you can also start tomatoes for a greenhouse crop. Sow in warmth — around 20 to 25 degrees — with good light, and use a clear lid until they germinate. Most other vegetables are better sown in February or March.

Is January too late or too early to prune fruit trees?

January is the right time to prune free-standing apple and pear trees — the dormant window runs from November to early March. Do not prune stone fruit such as plums and cherries in winter, and never prune when the wood is frozen. Cut out dead, damaged and crossing branches first, and always cut to an outward-facing bud.

How do I plan crop rotation?

Divide your beds into four groups — roots, brassicas, legumes and potatoes or squash — and move each group to a new bed every year, so no group returns to the same bed for four years. Rotation keeps the soil healthy, confuses pests and stops diseases building up. Write the plan down in January, or you will forget it by spring.

What is the last frost date and why does it matter?

The last frost date is the average date of the last frost in spring in your area, and it decides when tender plants can go outside. Tomatoes, beans, squash and peppers die in a frost, so they must stay indoors until after that date. Look the date up for your own town — it can be a month different in the next valley, and a cold spring can push it later.

Can I grow vegetables indoors in January?

Yes — microgreens are the easiest January crop, ready in 7 to 10 days on a sunny windowsill. You can also grow lettuce, spinach, herbs and Asian greens indoors, and start peppers, chillies and tomatoes in trays. Give them as much sunlight as you can, or use a grow light, and water gently — indoor seedlings rot if they sit wet.

What garden jobs should I do in January?

Plan the year, order seeds, inventory old seed, clean pots, tools and the greenhouse, top-dress beds with compost, check winter protection and mulch, prune apple and pear trees, plant bare-root fruit, and harvest leeks, kale and winter greens. January is a quiet month outdoors, but it is the busiest month for planning — the work you do now decides the harvest you get in summer.

When should I order seeds?

Order in January, as soon as the catalogues arrive. Popular varieties sell out, and ordering early gives you time to plan your sowing calendar before the season starts. Check the packet dates on the seed you already have first, and compost any that is too old — ordering the same seed twice is the most common January waste.
