30 Raised Bed Vegetable Garden Ideas

Raised BedsUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Raised beds turn even the smallest garden into a productive vegetable patch. With looser, compost-rich soil, better drainage and less bending, a raised bed lets you grow more food in less space — and it looks good doing it. These 30 ideas cover layouts, materials, planting plans and small-space tricks, from soil depth and spacing to sunlight, watering and companion planting, so whether you have a sunny backyard or a single courtyard corner, you will find a bed that fits.

1

Keyhole Raised Bed for Easy Composting

Idea 1 of 30

A keyhole bed is a round raised bed with a notch cut out so you can reach the centre, where a wire compost basket sits. Kitchen scraps go in the basket, water washes nitrogen and nutrients into the soil, and worms do the rest. Build it about 1.8 metres across and 60 cm high, and plant leafy greens and herbs around the edge where they are easy to pick.

2

Square-Foot Layout for Maximum Harvest

Idea 2 of 30

Divide a 1.2 by 1.2 metre bed into sixteen 30 cm squares with thin battens, then plant each square by the spacing on the seed packet: one tomato, four lettuces, nine spinach plants or sixteen carrots per square. The grid stops you overcrowding, makes watering and weeding simple, and turns a small bed into a surprisingly large harvest — sow a few squares every two weeks for a steady succession of salads.

3

Companion Planting That Actually Works

Idea 3 of 30

Pair plants that help each other: tomatoes with basil, carrots with onions, and marigolds along every edge to confuse pests. Beans fix nitrogen in the soil, so plant them before heavy feeders like squash. Keep the combinations simple — three or four proven pairs per bed — and skip the folklore. Good companions mean fewer chemicals, healthier plants and better soil health.

4

Cedar Raised Bed That Lasts for Years

Idea 4 of 30

Cedar is the classic raised-bed timber because it resists rot without chemical treatment. Use 5 cm thick boards, 30 cm high, screwed into corner posts, and line the inside with landscape fabric to keep soil in and slugs out. A 2.4 by 1.2 metre cedar bed should last eight to ten years. Oil the outside once a year if you want it to keep its warm colour.

5

Herb Spiral in a Raised Bed Corner

Idea 5 of 30

Tuck a small herb spiral into one corner of a raised bed and you get five microclimates in one square metre. Sun-loving perennial rosemary and thyme go on top where it is dry, basil and parsley sit in the middle, and mint — always in its own pot, sunk into the soil — goes at the damp base. It saves space, looks beautiful, and keeps dinner herbs within reach.

6

Deep-Soil Bed for Root Vegetables

Idea 6 of 30

Carrots, parsnips and beets need at least 45 cm of loose, stone-free soil with a pH of 6.0 to 7.0 to grow straight. Build a deep bed, fill the bottom half with coarse compost and the top half with a sandy loam mix, and never walk on it — compaction is what makes roots fork. Thin seedlings early so each root has room, and water deeply rather than often.

7

Salad Bar Bed by the Kitchen Door

Idea 7 of 30

Put a small salad bed right outside the kitchen door and you will use it every day. Fill it with cut-and-come-again lettuce, radishes, spring onions and herbs, and sow a new row every two weeks from spring to autumn. Pick leaves in the morning, let the plants regrow, and you will have fresh salad for months from a bed barely a metre long.

8

Vertical Trellis on the North Side

Idea 8 of 30

Fix a trellis along the north edge of a raised bed so climbing plants never shade the rest. Beans, peas, cucumbers and small squash all climb happily, and growing up instead of out frees the bed for low crops underneath. Use sturdy 2 metre posts and wire or netting, and tie stems loosely as they grow so wind does not snap them.

9

Strawberry Tower in a Raised Bed

Idea 9 of 30

Strawberries love raised beds because the fruit hangs clear of damp soil and slugs. Plant runners around the edges — each runner is a free new plant — so berries cascade over the sides, and mulch with straw to keep fruit clean. Net the bed when fruit ripens, or the birds will eat your harvest first. One well-kept bed gives bowls of berries for three or four years.

10

Root Vegetable Bed with Loose Soil

Idea 10 of 30

Root vegetables fail in heavy soil, so dedicate one bed to a loose mix: equal parts topsoil, compost and coarse sand, forked through and never compacted. Sow carrots, turnips and beetroot in rows 25 cm apart, thin ruthlessly, and water evenly — dry spells followed by heavy watering split roots. Harvest young for the sweetest flavour.

11

Pollinator Border Around Your Beds

Idea 11 of 30

Ring your vegetable beds with a 30 cm border of borage, calendula, nasturtiums and lavender, and bees will visit daily. More pollinators mean better fruit set on beans, squash and cucumbers, and the flowers double as pest confusers and edible garnishes. Leave a few flowers to seed so the border returns by itself next year.

12

Shade-Tolerant Bed for North Walls

Idea 12 of 30

A bed against a north wall still grows food if you choose the right crops. Lettuce, spinach, chard, kale and Asian greens all cope with three to four hours of sun — classic partial-shade crops — and they bolt far slower in cool shade. Keep the soil rich and moist, harvest outer leaves often, and you will pick salads all summer from a spot most gardeners write off.

13

Tomato Cage Bed with Strong Supports

Idea 13 of 30

Give tomatoes their own bed with serious supports from day one. Sink tall cages or stakes 30 cm into the soil before planting, space plants 60 cm apart, and tie stems every 20 cm as they grow. Mulch thickly to keep soil moisture even — steady moisture and calcium uptake is what stops blossom-end rot — and remove lower leaves for airflow once fruit sets.

14

Waist-High Bed for Easy Gardening

Idea 14 of 30

A waist-high bed on legs means no bending and no kneeling, which suits bad backs and wheelchair users. Build it 80 cm high with a solid base and drainage holes, fill the bottom with light material like straw bales or empty pots, and keep the soil depth to 30 cm. Grow herbs, salads and strawberries — deep-rooted crops belong in ground-level beds.

15

Corner L-Shaped Bed for Small Yards

Idea 15 of 30

An L-shaped bed turns a wasted corner into the most productive spot in the yard. Build two 60 cm wide arms along the fences, leaving the middle open so you can reach every plant from the path. Tall crops go at the back against the fence, low salads at the front, and the corner itself suits a single tomato or a small herb clump.

16

Rain Garden Bed That Drinks Runoff

Idea 16 of 30

Place a low raised bed where roof runoff flows and it becomes a rain garden that waters itself. Line the base with gravel, fill with free-draining soil, and plant thirsty crops like celery, chard and watercress at the wet end. A simple overflow notch stops flooding in heavy rain, and you will barely need to water this bed all summer.

17

Compost-Fed Bed for Hungry Plants

Idea 17 of 30

Feed one bed heavily with home compost, rich in nitrogen and organic matter, and reserve it for hungry crops: squash, pumpkins, sweetcorn and brassicas. Dig in a 10 cm layer of mature compost each spring, then mulch again in midsummer to keep moisture in. These plants repay the feeding with size — a single compost-fed squash plant can fill a whole bed by August.

18

Kids' Garden Bed They Can Reach

Idea 18 of 30

Give children their own low bed — 1.2 metres square and only 20 cm high — and let them choose what grows. Fast, fun crops work best: sunflowers, peas, radishes, strawberries and pumpkins. Let them water with a small can, pick their own harvest, and eat it straight from the plant. A child who grows a carrot will eat a carrot.

19

Cut-Flower Bed for Weekly Bouquets

Idea 19 of 30

Dedicate one raised bed to cut flowers and you will have bouquets from June to October. Sow zinnias, cosmos, sweet peas and sunflowers in rows, and pick stems every few days — cutting and deadheading actually makes them flower more. Grow tall varieties at the back and short ones at the front, and plant in successions so there is always something new in bloom.

20

Winter Greens Bed with a Cold Frame

Idea 20 of 30

Top a raised bed with a simple cold frame — a hinged glass or polycarbonate lid — and you can pick salad leaves all winter. Sow winter lettuce, spinach, rocket and mizuna in late summer — check your hardiness zone and first frost date to time it right — vent the lid on sunny days, and close it before frost. The raised bed warms faster than open ground, so growth starts weeks earlier in spring too.

21

Bean Arch Bed with a Living Roof

Idea 21 of 30

Bend two trellis panels into an arch over a raised bed and plant runner beans at both ends. By midsummer the arch is a green tunnel of flowers and dangling pods, and the shade underneath suits lettuce and herbs. Pick beans every two days to keep them coming, and leave a few pods to dry for next year's seed.

22

Mediterranean Herb Bed in Full Sun

Idea 22 of 30

Mediterranean herbs want sun, poor soil and very little water, so give them their own sunny bed with a gritty, free-draining mix. Plant drought-tolerant rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage and lavender, mulch with gravel rather than compost, and water only in long dry spells. The flavour is strongest when the plants are slightly stressed, so resist the urge to feed them.

23

Berry Patch Bed with Netting

Idea 23 of 30

Raspberries and currants thrive in raised beds with acidic, compost-rich soil (pH 5.5 to 6.5). Plant canes 45 cm apart against wires, mulch thickly, and fit a simple netting frame before the fruit ripens — birds can strip a bush in a morning. Prune summer raspberries after fruiting and autumn ones in late winter, and the patch will feed you for a decade.

24

Rain Barrel Bed with Drip Lines

Idea 24 of 30

Put a rain barrel beside a raised bed, run a gravity drip line along each row, and the bed waters itself slowly at the roots. Drip watering uses far less water than a hose, keeps leaves dry so disease stays low, and works while you are away. Add a simple timer if your barrel is mains-fed, and mulch the surface to hold the moisture in.

25

Three Sisters Bed, the Old Way

Idea 25 of 30

The Three Sisters — corn, beans and squash — have been grown together for centuries, and they still work. Corn gives beans a pole to climb, beans feed the soil with nitrogen, and squash leaves shade the ground and keep weeds down. Plant corn first, beans when the corn is knee-high, and squash a week later. It is a complete meal from one bed.

26

Succession Planting Bed for All Season

Idea 26 of 30

Never let a bed sit empty. As soon as one crop finishes, sow the next: spring lettuce gives way to summer beans, which give way to autumn kale. Sow small batches every two weeks instead of everything at once, and keep a notebook of what followed what — rotating crop families each year keeps the soil healthy and pests confused. Succession planting turns one bed into three harvests a year.

27

Gravel Path Bed for Tidy Edges

Idea 27 of 30

Set raised beds 60 cm apart and fill the gaps with gravel over weed fabric, and the whole vegetable garden stays tidy and mud-free. Gravel drains fast, suppresses weeds, and makes the beds look like a designed garden rather than a work area. Edge the paths with timber or stone so gravel stays put, and rake it level once a season.

28

Pallet Collar Bed on a Budget

Idea 28 of 30

Pallet collars — the hinged wooden frames used for shipping — make instant raised beds for very little money. Stack one or two collars, line with fabric, fill with soil, and you are planting within the hour. Check the stamp on the wood first: use only heat-treated (HT) pallets, never chemically treated (MB) ones, and replace collars as they weather.

29

Wildflower Edge for Bees and Beauty

Idea 29 of 30

Sow a wildflower strip along the sunny edge of your raised beds and the whole garden comes alive. Poppies, cornflowers, cosmos and calendula feed bees and hoverflies, which then pollinate your vegetables and eat your aphids. Sow in spring on bare soil, let the flowers seed themselves, and mow the strip once in autumn.

30

Harvest Basket Bed by the Gate

Idea 30 of 30

Put your most-picked bed by the garden gate — herbs, salads and cherry tomatoes — and hang a basket on the gatepost. Every trip in or out becomes a harvest, and visitors leave with something fresh. Keep the basket stocked with scissors and a small trowel so picking is always easy, and the bed by the gate becomes the heart of the garden.

Key Takeaways

  • Build beds 1.2 metres wide so you can reach the middle from either side without stepping on the soil.
  • Fill beds with a loose, compost-rich mix — most vegetables prefer a soil pH of 6.0 to 7.0 — and never walk on them; compaction is the enemy of good roots.
  • Grow up as well as out: trellises, arches and cages double what a small bed can produce.
  • Feed the soil, not just the plants — a thick compost mulch each season does most of the work for you.
  • Plant something for pollinators in every bed, and your vegetables will set more fruit with less effort.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep should a raised bed be for vegetables?

Most vegetables do well in 30 cm of soil, which suits a single-board bed. Root vegetables like carrots and parsnips need at least 45 cm of loose, stone-free soil to grow straight, so build a deeper bed for them. If your bed sits on concrete or paving, add 10 cm of coarse gravel at the base for drainage before filling it with soil.

What is the best soil mix for a raised bed?

A good starting mix is roughly 60 percent quality topsoil, 30 percent mature compost and 10 percent coarse material such as perlite, vermiculite or sharp sand for drainage — aim for a soil pH of 6.0 to 7.0, which suits most vegetables. Avoid using only bagged compost, which dries out fast and shrinks. Top up with 5 cm of fresh compost every spring, because the soil level drops as organic matter breaks down.

How often should I water a raised bed?

Raised beds dry out faster than ground-level soil, especially in sun and wind. In summer, check the soil daily and water deeply when the top 3 cm feels dry — a deep soak two or three times a week, or a drip irrigation line at the roots, beats a light sprinkle every day. Mulch with straw or compost to hold moisture in, and water in the morning so leaves dry before night.

Do raised beds need lining?

Lining the sides with landscape fabric keeps soil from washing out through gaps and slows timber rot, but never line the bottom with plastic — roots and worms need to reach the ground below, and water must drain away. If you are worried about weeds from below, use a layer of cardboard, which smothers weeds and rots down within a season.

What vegetables grow best in raised beds?

Almost everything grows well in raised beds, but they shine for salads, herbs, tomatoes, beans, carrots and strawberries. Warm, well-drained soil suits heat lovers like peppers and aubergines, while the loose mix is perfect for root crops. Avoid very large, thirsty plants like pumpkins in small beds unless you let them trail outside. Sow salads little and often for steady succession harvests.

How do I keep slugs out of a raised bed?

Slugs love the damp shelter of raised beds, so use several defences at once: copper tape around the outside, a dry mulch of straw, beer traps sunk at soil level, and regular evening checks with a torch. Keep the area around the bed clear of hiding places, water in the morning, and encourage birds and frogs, which eat slugs for you. A few sacrificial nasturtiums nearby also draw slugs away from your crops.

Can I put a raised bed on grass or concrete?

Yes, both work. On grass, lay cardboard over the lawn to smother it, then build the bed on top — the grass rots down into the soil. On concrete or paving, use a bed at least 30 cm deep, add drainage holes or a gravel layer, and water more often, because the bed cannot draw moisture from the ground below.

When should I start planting a raised bed?

Start cool-season crops like lettuce, peas and radishes in early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked — check your hardiness zone and last frost date first. Wait until after the last frost for tomatoes, beans, squash and peppers, which need warm soil. In mild climates you can sow salads and hardy greens almost all year, and a cold frame on top extends the season by several weeks.
