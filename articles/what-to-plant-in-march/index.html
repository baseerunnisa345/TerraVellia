What to Plant in March: 29 Ideas

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

March is the month the garden wakes up, and knowing what to plant in March is the difference between a summer of plenty and a spring of empty beds. These 29 ideas cover every kind of March planting — vegetables to sow direct, tender crops to start indoors, herbs, flowers, summer bulbs and fruit — with soil warming, frost protection and what to hold back until the cold has passed. Your hardiness zone and your last frost date decide the exact timing: in mild zones 8 to 11 you can sow outside for most of March, while in cold zones 3 to 5 the soil is still frozen and most sowing stays indoors until the last frost passes.

1

Sow Peas Direct in March Soil

Idea 1 of 29

Peas are one of the first vegetables you can sow outside in March, because they germinate in cool soil and shrug off a light frost. Sow the seeds two inches deep in a shallow drill, in double rows so the plants support each other, and protect them from mice with a cloche or wire mesh until they sprout. Choose a smooth-seeded variety for early sowing — wrinkled peas rot in cold, wet soil.

2

Sow Broad Beans Outdoors in March

Idea 2 of 29

Broad beans are the hardiest bean you can sow in March, and they grow fast once the soil warms. Sow the seeds two inches deep and six inches apart, in double rows a foot apart, so the plants hold each other up as they grow. Pinch out the growing tips in June to stop blackfly settling on the soft shoots. Broad beans fix nitrogen in the soil, so the bed they leave behind is richer for the crop that follows.

3

Sow Carrots in March Rows

Idea 3 of 29

Carrots sown in March are ready by early summer, and they taste sweeter than shop carrots because they grow slowly in cool soil. Sow the seed thinly in a shallow drill, in fine, stone-free soil, and cover with a thin layer of compost rather than heavy earth. Thin the seedlings to two inches apart once they are big enough to hold, and keep the bed watered so the roots grow straight and sweet.

4

Sow Parsnips Before the Soil Warms

Idea 4 of 29

Parsnips need a long season, so March is the time to sow them — they take three weeks to germinate and all summer to grow. Sow the seed thinly in a deep, stone-free bed, because a stone in the way forks the root. Mark the row with a few radish seeds, which sprout fast and show you where the slow parsnips are. Harvest after the first frost, when the cold has sweetened the roots.

5

Sow Lettuce Little and Often in March

Idea 5 of 29

March is the start of the salad season, and the trick is to sow a little lettuce every two weeks rather than a whole bed at once. Sow a short row under a cloche or in a cold frame, thin the seedlings to six inches apart, and pick the outer leaves as the plants grow. A March sowing gives you cut-and-come-again leaves by May, and a fresh row keeps the supply going into summer.

6

Sow Spinach in March for Spring Leaves

Idea 6 of 29

Spinach loves the cool days of March, when the soil is damp and the sun is gentle — it bolts fast once the heat arrives, so sow it now. Sow the seed an inch deep in rich, moist soil, and thin the seedlings to four inches apart. Pick the outer leaves young and the plant keeps growing for weeks. A March sowing gives you spring leaves for salads and cooking before the summer heat ends the crop.

7

Sow Radishes for the Fastest March Crop

Idea 7 of 29

Radishes are the fastest crop you can sow in March — some varieties are ready to pull in three weeks, which makes them the perfect first harvest of the year. Sow the seed half an inch deep in a sunny spot, thin to an inch apart, and water well, because dry soil makes radishes hot and woody. Sow a short row every fortnight, and mark the row with a stick so you remember where the quick crop is.

8

Plant Onion Sets in March Beds

Idea 8 of 29

Onion sets are the easiest way to grow onions, and March is the classic month to plant them. Push each set into well-drained soil so just the tip shows, spacing them four inches apart in rows a foot apart. Birds love to pull them out, so cover the bed with fleece or netting until the roots take hold. A March planting gives you onions to lift and dry in late summer.

9

Plant Seed Potatoes in March

Idea 9 of 29

March is potato-planting month for most gardens, once the soil has warmed a little and the worst frosts have passed. Plant chitted seed potatoes — those with short, dark shoots — in a trench six inches deep, eyes up, a foot apart, and cover with soil. Earth up the shoots as they grow, and you will be digging new potatoes by June. Early varieties are the best bet for a March planting.

10

Start Tomatoes Indoors in March

Idea 10 of 29

Tomatoes started indoors in March grow into strong plants ready for the greenhouse in May and the garden after the last frost. Sow the seed thinly in trays of fine compost, keep them at eighteen degrees or more, and prick the seedlings out into individual pots once they have two true leaves. Give them the brightest windowsill you have, and turn the pots daily so the stems grow straight and sturdy.

11

Start Sweet Peppers Indoors in March

Idea 11 of 29

Sweet peppers need a long, warm season, so March is the latest you should start them indoors. Sow the seed in trays on a heated propagator or the warmest windowsill in the house, because peppers will not germinate below eighteen degrees. Prick them out into individual pots and keep them warm until May, when they can go into the greenhouse. In a cool summer they crop best under glass.

12

Start Aubergines Indoors in March

Idea 12 of 29

Aubergines are the most heat-hungry vegetable you can start in March, and they repay the effort with glossy fruit by late summer. Sow the seed in a heated propagator at twenty degrees or more, and keep the seedlings warm and bright — a cold windowsill will stall them for weeks. Pot them on into large pots and grow them in the greenhouse, where the heat and shelter they need are easiest to give.

13

Start Courgettes Indoors at Month's End

Idea 13 of 29

Courgettes grow so fast that starting them too early in March leaves you with leggy plants and nowhere to put them. Wait until the last week of the month, sow one seed per pot of compost, and keep them warm until they sprout. Plant them out after the last frost, and one or two plants will feed a family all summer — three plants is a courgette glut waiting to happen.

14

Sow Basil Indoors on a Warm Windowsill

Idea 14 of 29

Basil is a tender herb that hates the cold, so in March it belongs on a warm windowsill, not in the garden. Sow the seed on the surface of fine compost, press it down lightly, and keep the pot at twenty degrees until it sprouts. Pinch out the growing tips once the plants have four pairs of leaves, and they will bush out into a plant that feeds your summer salads and pasta.

15

Sow Parsley in March for Summer Picking

Idea 15 of 29

Parsley is slow to germinate — it can take a month — so sow it in March to have plants ready for summer picking. Soak the seed overnight to speed things up, then sow it thinly in a pot or a sheltered bed. Keep the soil damp, and be patient: the first leaves appear when you have almost given up. Flat-leaf parsley has the best flavour, and one sowing gives you leaves from June to the first frost.

16

Sow Chives in March for a Perennial Patch

Idea 16 of 29

Chives are a perennial herb, so a March sowing gives you a patch that comes back every spring for years. Sow the seed in a pot or a sunny corner, thin the seedlings to a few inches apart, and snip the hollow stems for salads, soups and baked potatoes. Let a few flower heads go to seed, and the patch will sow itself. Chives are one of the first herbs to wake up in spring.

17

Sow Dill in March for Pickles and Fish

Idea 17 of 29

Dill sown in March gives you feathery leaves for fish dishes by early summer, and seed heads for pickling by autumn. Sow the seed where it is to grow, because dill hates being moved, and thin the seedlings to a foot apart so the plants have room to grow tall. Dill self-seeds freely, so let one plant go to seed and you will have dill in the same spot next year without sowing at all.

18

Sow Sweet Peas Outdoors in March

Idea 18 of 29

Sweet peas sown outdoors in March grow into the scented climbers that fill the garden with colour and perfume all summer. Sow the seed an inch deep at the base of a wigwam or a trellis, in rich soil, and water well. Pinch out the growing tips when the plants are six inches tall, and they will branch out and flower for months. Pick the flowers often — the more you cut, the more they bloom.

19

Start Sunflowers Indoors in March

Idea 19 of 29

Sunflowers started indoors in March grow into tall, cheerful plants that flower by midsummer, and they are the easiest flower for a child to grow. Sow one seed per pot of compost, keep it warm until it sprouts, and plant the seedling out after the last frost. Give each plant a cane for support and a sunny spot, and it will tower over the garden by August.

20

Sow Nasturtiums in March for Summer Colour

Idea 20 of 29

Nasturtiums are the easiest annual you can sow in March — they grow in poor soil, flower all summer, and the leaves and flowers are edible. Sow the seed an inch deep where it is to flower, in a sunny spot, and thin the seedlings to a foot apart. They tumble over walls and pots, and they lure aphids away from your vegetables, which makes them a useful companion as well as a pretty one.

21

Sow Cosmos Indoors in March

Idea 21 of 29

Cosmos started indoors in March gives you feathery plants covered in daisy flowers from July until the first frost. Sow the seed thinly in trays of compost, prick the seedlings out into individual pots, and plant them out after the last frost. Cosmos likes poor soil — rich soil grows leaves instead of flowers — and the more you cut the blooms, the more the plants produce.

22

Plant Dahlias After the Last Frost

Idea 22 of 29

Dahlias are the stars of the late-summer garden, and March is the month to start their tubers indoors if your last frost is late. Pot the tubers in compost, keep them warm until shoots appear, and plant them out only after the last frost — a single cold night kills them. In mild zones you can plant the tubers straight into the garden at the end of March. Feed them well, and they will flower from July to October.

23

Plant Gladioli in March for Summer Spikes

Idea 23 of 29

Gladioli planted in March give you tall spikes of colour in July and August, and they are one of the easiest summer bulbs to grow. Plant the corms four inches deep and six inches apart, in a sunny, well-drained bed, pointy end up. Plant a few every fortnight until May, and the flowers will follow one after another all summer. Stake tall varieties before the wind bends them.

24

Plant Lilies in March for Summer Scent

Idea 24 of 29

Lilies planted in March fill the garden with scent in July, and they are easier to grow than their glamour suggests. Plant the bulbs six inches deep in a sunny spot with good drainage, in groups of three or five, and mulch the bed to keep the roots cool. Oriental lilies have the strongest perfume, while Asiatic lilies are the hardiest. A March planting gives you flowers in the first summer.

25

Plant Strawberries in March Beds

Idea 25 of 29

Strawberries planted in March settle in before summer and fruit the same year, which makes them the quickest fruit you can grow. Set the plants a foot apart in rich, well-drained soil, with the crown — the growing point — just above the surface, and water them in well. Mulch with straw to keep the fruit clean, and pick the berries as they ripen in June and July.

26

Plant Raspberries Before Buds Break

Idea 26 of 29

March is the last good month to plant raspberry canes, while they are still dormant and the buds have not yet broken. Set the canes eighteen inches apart along a wire support, cut them down to a foot after planting, and water them in. Summer-fruiting varieties crop on last year's wood, while autumn-fruiting types fruit on this year's growth. A March planting gives you berries by the following summer.

27

Warm the Soil Before You Sow

Idea 27 of 29

March soil is often too cold for seeds to germinate, and a seed sown in cold soil rots before it sprouts. Warm the bed first: cover it with black plastic or a cloche for a week or two, and the soil underneath will warm by several degrees. Sow into the warm soil, then replace the cover until the seedlings appear. A soil thermometer tells you the truth — most vegetables need the soil at seven degrees or more.

28

Keep Fleece Handy for March Frosts

Idea 28 of 29

March frosts are the gardener's trap: the days feel like spring, and then a clear night drops below freezing and kills the new growth. Keep a roll of horticultural fleece by the back door, and cover tender seedlings and new shoots whenever the forecast warns of frost. Drape it loosely so the plants can breathe, weigh the edges down with bricks, and take it off in the morning so the sun can reach the leaves.

29

Do Not Plant Tender Crops Out Yet

Idea 29 of 29

The hardest March lesson is patience: tomatoes, courgettes, beans, sweetcorn and basil will all die in a frost, and a plant put out too early grows slower than one kept warm until May. Hold tender crops indoors or in the greenhouse until after your last frost date, and harden them off gradually — a few hours outside each day — before they move out for good. A plant set out a week late always beats a plant killed by frost.

Key Takeaways

  • Sow peas, beans, carrots and lettuce direct in March — cool-season crops grow best in cool soil.
  • Start tomatoes, peppers and aubergines indoors in March; plant them out only after the last frost.
  • March is the month for summer bulbs, strawberries and raspberries — and for sweet peas outdoors.
  • Warm the soil before you sow, and keep fleece handy: March frosts kill tender growth overnight.
  • Your hardiness zone decides the timing — mild zones sow outside all March, cold zones sow indoors.

Frequently Asked Questions

What vegetables can I plant in March?

March is the best month for cool-season vegetables: sow peas, broad beans, carrots, parsnips, lettuce, spinach and radishes direct, and plant onion sets and seed potatoes. Start tomatoes, peppers, aubergines and courgettes indoors. In mild zones you can sow almost everything outside, while in cold zones most sowing stays indoors until the last frost.

Is March too early to plant tomatoes outside?

Yes — tomatoes are tender and a single frost kills them, so in March they belong indoors on a warm windowsill or in a heated propagator. Start the seed indoors in March, grow the plants on in pots, harden them off gradually, and plant them outside only after your last frost date, which falls in late April or May.

What flowers can I sow in March?

Sow sweet peas outdoors in March, and start sunflowers, cosmos, nasturtiums and marigolds indoors for planting out after the last frost. Hardy annuals like cornflowers, calendula and poppies can be sown direct, while tender flowers stay inside until May. Pick the flowers often once they bloom, and most annuals flower for months.

Can I plant potatoes in March?

Yes — March is the classic month to plant seed potatoes in most gardens. Plant chitted potatoes in a trench six inches deep, a foot apart, once the soil has warmed and the worst frosts have passed. Earth up the shoots as they grow, and early varieties are ready to dig by June.

What should I not plant in March?

Hold back tender crops until after the last frost: tomatoes, courgettes, cucumbers, sweetcorn, beans and basil all die in a frost, so keep them indoors or in the greenhouse through March. Warm-season flowers like zinnias and dahlias also wait until May. A plant set out a week late always beats one killed by frost.

How do I protect March seedlings from frost?

Cover seedlings with horticultural fleece, a cloche or an old sheet whenever the forecast warns of frost, and take the cover off in the morning. Water the soil before a frost, because damp soil holds heat better than dry soil. Keep pots against a warm wall overnight, and bring tender seedlings indoors if a hard frost is forecast.

When is the last frost date in my area?

Your last frost date depends on your hardiness zone and your local climate: in mild zones it can fall in February or March, while in cold zones it may not pass until May. Check your zone on a hardiness map or ask a local nursery, and mark the date on your calendar.

Can I plant summer bulbs in March?

Yes — March is the month for summer bulbs like dahlias, gladioli, lilies and begonias, which flower from July onwards. Plant them after the last frost, or start them indoors and plant out in May. In mild zones you can plant straight into the garden at the end of March.
