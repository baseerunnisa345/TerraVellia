26 Seeds to Start Indoors This Winter

Vegetable GardeningUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Starting seeds indoors in winter is the cheapest way to fill a garden: a packet of seeds, a tray of compost and a bright windowsill can give you dozens of plants for the price of one bought plant. These 26 ideas cover every part of seed starting indoors winter — tomatoes, chillies, aubergines, sweet peas, leeks and flowers — with the seed trays, grow lights and humidity domes that make germination reliable, and the potting on, feeding and hardening off that turn seedlings into strong plants. Before you start, check your hardiness zone and your last frost date: sow 6 to 10 weeks before that date, because seedlings started too early grow leggy indoors, and the right sowing date depends on how cold your garden actually gets.

1

A Sunny Windowsill Seed Station

Idea 1 of 26

A sunny windowsill is the simplest seed station there is, and it costs nothing. Clear the sill, wipe the glass inside and out, and set your trays where they get the most light — a south-facing window is best, east-facing is good, and a north-facing sill will need a grow light. Keep the trays away from cold draughts and hot radiators, turn them every day so seedlings grow straight, and you can raise dozens of plants on one windowsill.

2

Seed Trays with a Clear Lid

Idea 2 of 26

A seed tray with a clear lid is a tiny greenhouse: it holds moisture in, keeps the compost damp, and gives seeds the warm, humid air they need to germinate. Sow into damp compost, press the lid on, and put the tray somewhere bright but out of direct sun. Lift the lid daily to let fresh air in, and take it off once the seedlings are up, so they do not go soft and leggy.

3

A Heated Propagator for Warmth-Lovers

Idea 3 of 26

A heated propagator is a seed tray with a gentle heat source underneath, and it is the best way to germinate warmth-lovers like tomatoes, chillies and aubergines in a cold house. The steady bottom heat, around 20 degrees, wakes seeds that would sit sulking in a cool room. Sow into the propagator, close the lid, and move the seedlings out into the light the moment they appear, before they stretch.

4

Humidity Dome for Steady Germination

Idea 4 of 26

A humidity dome is a clear plastic cover that sits over a seed tray and traps warm, moist air around the seeds. It stops the compost drying out between waterings, which is the commonest reason seeds fail indoors. Keep the dome on until the first seedlings appear, then lift it a little each day to let the air in, and remove it completely once most of the seeds are up.

5

Grow Lights Hung Low Over Seedlings

Idea 5 of 26

Seedlings on a windowsill in January get far too little light, and they stretch towards the window until they flop. Hang a grow light just 10 centimetres above the seedlings and they stay short, stocky and green. LED grow lights are cheap to run and stay cool, so they can sit close without scorching leaves. Raise the light as the seedlings grow, keeping the gap small.

6

Fine Seed Compost, Not Garden Soil

Idea 6 of 26

Seeds need fine, free-draining compost, not garden soil: garden soil compacts in a tray, holds too much water, and can carry damping-off disease. Buy seed compost, or sieve multipurpose compost to remove lumps and twigs, and fill the tray loosely. Never press the compost down hard — roots need air as much as water. Good compost is the cheapest insurance for a tray of seedlings.

7

Sieve Compost for Tiny Seeds

Idea 7 of 26

Tiny seeds like petunias, lobelia and celery need a fine, level surface, because a seed buried under a lump of compost may never reach the light. Push your compost through a kitchen sieve into the tray, level it with a piece of wood, and sow on the smooth surface. Cover the smallest seeds with a dusting of sieved compost or vermiculite, and they will germinate evenly across the tray.

8

Pre-Water Trays Before Sowing

Idea 8 of 26

Water the compost before you sow, not after: wet compost is easy to water evenly, and watering after sowing can wash tiny seeds into clumps or bury them too deep. Fill the tray, water it well and let it drain, then sow on the damp surface. The compost will stay moist under the lid for days, and the seeds will not dry out before they germinate.

9

Sow Thinly in Rows

Idea 9 of 26

Sow thinly, in rows, and your seedlings will thank you: crowded seeds compete for light and air, grow leggy, and are hard to prick out without damage. Sow a pinch of seed along a shallow drill, leave a finger's width between rows, and thin the seedlings to the strongest once they have their first true leaves. A tray sown thinly gives you more usable plants than a tray sown thickly.

10

Vermiculite Over Fine Seeds

Idea 10 of 26

Vermiculite is a light, mineral grit that makes the perfect cover for fine seeds: it holds moisture, lets light through, and stops the compost surface crusting over. Sow the seeds on damp compost, then sprinkle a thin layer of vermiculite over the top instead of compost. The seeds stay moist and get the light they need, and the pale surface shows you at a glance where the seeds are.

11

Soak Large Seeds Overnight

Idea 11 of 26

Large, hard-coated seeds like sweet peas, beans and nasturtiums germinate faster if you soak them overnight in lukewarm water before sowing. The water softens the seed coat and wakes the seed inside, so it sprouts days earlier. Soak for no more than 12 hours, sow into damp compost the next morning, and discard any seeds that have gone soft and mushy — they will only rot in the tray.

12

Label Trays the Day You Sow

Idea 12 of 26

A tray of bare compost in February looks exactly like every other tray, so label everything the day you sow. Write the plant name, the variety and the sowing date on a wooden label or a strip of tape, and push it into the tray where you can read it. A marker pen on the tray itself fades in the sun. Good labels save you from planting out a mystery seedling in spring.

13

Stop Damping Off with Air and Clean Trays

Idea 13 of 26

Damping off is the seedling killer: stems go thin and black at the base and the seedling flops over, and it is caused by damp, still air and dirty trays. Stop it before it starts: wash trays in hot soapy water before sowing, use fresh seed compost, water carefully, and ventilate the propagator daily. Thin seedlings so air moves between them, and never let trays sit in standing water.

14

Tomatoes Started Indoors in Late Winter

Idea 14 of 26

Tomatoes are the classic indoor-sown crop: start them in late winter, six to eight weeks before your last frost date, in a warm spot with a grow light. Sow thinly in a tray, prick the seedlings out into individual pots once they have two true leaves, and grow them on cool and bright. Plant them out only after the last frost, and they will crop weeks earlier than plants sown outdoors.

15

Chillies and Peppers on the Warmest Sill

Idea 15 of 26

Chillies and peppers need the longest season of any vegetable, so start them first — ten to twelve weeks before your last frost date — on the warmest windowsill in the house, or on a heat mat. They germinate slowly, sometimes taking three weeks, so be patient and keep the compost warm and just moist. Pot them on into bigger pots as they grow, and they will fruit all summer.

16

Aubergines in the Warmest Spot in the House

Idea 16 of 26

Aubergines are the most heat-hungry vegetable you can sow indoors, and they will not germinate in a cool room. Give them the warmest spot in the house — on a heat mat, in a heated propagator, or above a radiator — and keep them near 25 degrees until they sprout. Sow in January or February, grow them on warm and bright, and plant them out only when nights are reliably warm.

17

Sweet Peas in Root Trainers

Idea 17 of 26

Sweet peas hate having their roots disturbed, which is why root trainers suit them so well: the long, narrow cells let the roots grow straight down, and the seedlings slide out whole at planting time. Sow one seed per cell in late winter, keep them cool and bright so they grow stocky, and pinch out the growing tip when the plants are ten centimetres tall. They will flower all summer.

18

Leeks in Deep Modules

Idea 18 of 26

Leeks sown indoors in winter give you the longest growing season, and deep modules suit their long roots. Sow the seeds thinly in modules in January or February, and grow them on cool and bright — leeks like it cool, and warm rooms make them soft. When the seedlings are as thick as a pencil, plant them out into deep holes in the bed, and they will swell into fat leeks by autumn.

19

Basil from Seed on a Warm Sill

Idea 19 of 26

Basil is a warmth-lover that sulks in a cold room, so sow it on the warmest windowsill you have, or on a heat mat, in late winter. Sow the tiny seeds on the surface of fine compost and barely cover them — they need light to germinate. Keep them warm and moist, and pinch out the growing tips once the plants are ten centimetres tall, so they branch into bushy plants.

20

Zinnias and Cosmos for Summer Colour

Idea 20 of 26

Zinnias and cosmos are half-hardy annuals that flower all summer, and they are easy to start indoors in late winter. Sow them in modules six weeks before your last frost date, and grow them on bright and cool so they stay stocky. Plant them out after the last frost, and they will flower from June until the first frosts, filling beds and pots with colour for months.

21

Thin Seedlings to the Strongest

Idea 21 of 26

Thinning feels ruthless, but it is the kindest thing you can do for a tray of seedlings: crowded plants compete for light, grow leggy, and fall prey to damping off. Once the seedlings have their first true leaves, snip out the weakest with scissors, leaving the strongest at the spacing the packet suggests. The plants that are left grow into strong, healthy specimens instead of a crowd of weaklings.

22

Prick Out Seedlings into Individual Pots

Idea 22 of 26

Pricking out is the moment a seedling moves from the crowded seed tray into its own pot, and it is best done when the seedlings have two true leaves. Hold each seedling by a leaf, never the stem, tease it out with a dibber, and plant it into damp compost up to its first leaves. Water it in gently, keep it out of strong sun for a day, and it will root quickly into its new pot.

23

Pot On Before Roots Circle

Idea 23 of 26

A seedling left too long in a small pot runs out of food and its roots circle the pot, and it may never recover. Pot on — move the plant into a pot one size bigger — as soon as roots appear at the drainage holes. Water the plant first, tip it out gently, and set it into fresh compost at the same depth. A plant potted on in time grows away fast.

24

Feed Seedlings Weakly After Pricking Out

Idea 24 of 26

Seedlings in fresh compost need no food for their first weeks, because the compost holds enough to get them started. Once they are pricked out and growing, feed them weakly — a quarter-strength liquid feed once a week — so they grow steadily without going soft and leggy. Full-strength feed burns young roots, and overfed seedlings are the first to fall to damping off and pests.

25

Succession Sow Every Two Weeks

Idea 25 of 26

One sowing gives you one flush of plants; a sowing every two weeks gives you a steady supply all season. Sow a small tray of lettuce, salad leaves or herbs every fortnight from late winter onwards, and you will always have young plants ready to go out. Label each sowing with its date, and stop sowing tender crops six weeks before your last frost date, so the last batch is not caught by the cold.

26

Harden Off Indoor Seedlings Gradually

Idea 26 of 26

Seedlings raised indoors are soft, because they have never felt wind or cold, so harden them off before they go in the ground. For a week before planting out, put them outside in a sheltered spot during the day and bring them in at night, then leave them out overnight for the last two days. A seedling hardened off gradually barely checks its growth when you plant it out.

Key Takeaways

  • Start seeds 6 to 10 weeks before your last frost date, and count back from it.
  • Bright light and cool air keep seedlings short and stocky — use grow lights in midwinter.
  • Fine compost, careful watering and clean trays stop damping off before it starts.
  • Prick out at two true leaves, pot on before roots circle, and feed weakly.
  • Harden seedlings off for a week, then plant out after the last frost.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start seeds indoors in winter?

Start most seeds six to ten weeks before your last frost date — tomatoes, chillies and aubergines in January or February, hardy flowers and leeks a little earlier. Starting too soon gives you leggy seedlings that outgrow their pots before the weather is fit. Check your hardiness zone and count back from your last frost date for each crop.

Do I need grow lights to start seeds indoors?

In midwinter, yes for most seeds: a windowsill gives far too little light, and seedlings stretch and flop. A simple LED grow light hung 10 centimetres above the trays, giving 14 to 16 hours of light a day, keeps seedlings short and stocky. Herbs cope on a bright sill, but tomatoes and chillies really need the extra light.

What compost should I use for seed starting?

Use fine seed compost, or sieve multipurpose compost to remove lumps and twigs, and never use garden soil in trays — it compacts, holds too much water and can carry damping-off disease. Fill trays loosely, water before sowing, and do not press the compost down hard, because roots need air as much as moisture.

How do I stop seedlings going leggy?

Leggy seedlings mean too little light or too much warmth: move them to the brightest spot you have, hang a grow light close above them, and grow them on cool once they have germinated. Sow thinly, thin to the strongest, and turn trays daily. A small fan blowing gently across the trays also keeps stems short and strong.

Why do my seedlings keep dying at the base?

Seedlings that go thin and black at the base and flop over have damping off, a fungal disease of damp, still air. Wash trays before sowing, use fresh seed compost, water carefully, and ventilate the propagator daily. Thin seedlings so air moves between them, and never let trays sit in standing water.

When should I prick out seedlings?

Prick seedlings out when they have two true leaves — the first leaves after the seed leaves — and before they get crowded. Hold each seedling by a leaf, never the stem, and plant it into damp compost in its own pot. Water it in gently, keep it out of strong sun for a day, and it will root quickly.

How often should I water indoor seedlings?

Water little and often, from a fine spray or a watering can with a fine rose: check the compost with your finger and water only when the top feels dry. Overwatering rots seeds and causes damping off faster than drought, so err on the dry side.

When can I plant indoor seedlings outside?

Plant seedlings out after your last frost date, once they have been hardened off for a week — outside by day, in at night, then out overnight for the last two days. Plant out on a cloudy day or in the evening, water them in well, and protect them from slugs. Tender crops like tomatoes wait until nights are warm.
