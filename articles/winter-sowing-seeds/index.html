30 Winter Sowing Seed Ideas

Vegetable GardeningUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Winter sowing seeds in milk jugs and recycled containers is the laziest clever way to start a garden: you sow in December or January, leave the jugs outside all winter, and the seeds germinate when spring arrives. These 30 ideas cover every way of winter sowing seeds — milk jug greenhouses, drainage and ventilation, cold stratification, hardy annuals, vegetables and herbs, and perennials that need chilling — with the compost, watering and transplanting rules that make them work. Before you start, check your hardiness zone and your last frost date: in cold zones, sow in January or February when the cold is reliable, and in mild zones you can start in December, but the right seeds depend on how cold your garden actually gets.

1

Milk Jug Mini Greenhouses

Idea 1 of 30

The classic winter sowing container is a milk jug with the top cut almost all the way around, leaving a small hinge. Fill it with damp compost, sow the seeds, tape the cut shut, and leave the cap off so rain can get in. The jug acts as a mini greenhouse: it warms up in the sun, holds moisture, and protects seedlings from wind and birds. By spring, strong seedlings are ready to plant out.

2

Two-Litre Bottle Greenhouses

Idea 2 of 30

A two-litre drinks bottle works just like a milk jug, and it is even easier to cut. Slice the bottle in half around the middle, fill the base with compost, sow, and slot the top half back on as a lid. The narrow neck lets rain in and stops the compost drying out, while the clear plastic warms the soil. Bottles are ideal for small batches of one seed type, and they cost nothing.

3

Clear Salad Boxes as Seed Trays

Idea 3 of 30

The clear plastic boxes that salad and fruit come in make perfect small greenhouses, and they already have a hinged lid. Poke drainage holes in the base and a few ventilation holes in the lid, fill with compost, and sow in rows. They are shallow, so they suit small seeds like lettuce, alyssum and lobelia. Stack them in a sheltered corner and check them weekly through winter.

4

Big Tubs and Storage Boxes for Batches

Idea 4 of 30

When you want to sow a lot, a large clear storage box beats a dozen milk jugs. Drill drainage holes in the base, add five centimetres of compost, and sow several types in rows, with a label for each. The lid goes on loosely so air can move, and the whole box sits outside all winter. In spring you will have dozens of seedlings from one box, ready to pot up.

5

Drainage Holes in Every Container

Idea 5 of 30

Every winter sowing container needs drainage holes, without exception. Rain gets in through the open cap, and if it cannot get out, the compost turns to soup and the seeds rot. Drill or melt six to eight holes in the base of each jug or box, each about the width of a pencil. Set the containers on a slatted bench or gravel so the water can actually drain away.

6

Ventilation Holes in the Lid

Idea 6 of 30

A sealed container cooks seedlings on the first sunny day, so every lid needs ventilation holes. Cut or melt four or five holes in the top of each jug, each about a centimetre across, and leave the cap off the milk jug altogether. The holes let hot air out and rain in, and they stop condensation building up and dripping on the seeds. More holes are better than fewer.

7

A Free-Draining Seed Compost Mix

Idea 7 of 30

Winter sowing compost must drain freely, because containers sit outside in the rain for months. Mix multipurpose compost with a third of perlite or coarse grit, and never use garden soil, which compacts and goes airless when wet. Fill each container to about five centimetres deep and press the compost down gently. Good drainage is the difference between seeds that germinate and seeds that rot.

8

Moisten the Compost Before Sowing

Idea 8 of 30

Dry compost is hard to wet once seeds are in, so moisten it before you sow. Pour water through the compost in a bucket, mix it with your hands until it is damp like a wrung-out sponge, then fill the containers. Sow the seeds on the damp surface and cover them lightly. The compost will stay moist for weeks inside a closed jug, and the seeds will not dry out before they germinate.

9

Label Every Jug Clearly

Idea 9 of 30

A jug of bare compost in February looks exactly like every other jug, so label everything the day you sow. Write the plant name and the sowing date on a wooden label or a strip of tape, and push it inside the jug where the rain cannot wash it off. A marker pen on the outside fades in the winter sun. Good labels save you from planting out a mystery seedling in spring.

10

Tape the Jug Shut After Sowing

Idea 10 of 30

Once the seeds are in, tape the jug shut along the cut you made, leaving the cap off. The tape holds the two halves together through winter gales, keeps the warmth in, and stops birds and slugs getting at the seeds. Use wide packing tape and wrap it right around the jug. In spring, peel the tape off and open the jug like a lid when the seedlings need more air.

11

Sweet Peas in Milk Jugs

Idea 11 of 30

Sweet peas are one of the best flowers for winter sowing, because they are hardy and they germinate in the cold. Sow two or three seeds per jug in December or January, and by March you will have strong, stocky plants with roots that fill the jug. Pinch out the growing tip when the plants are ten centimetres tall, and they will branch into bushy plants that flower all summer.

12

Hardy Annuals: Cornflowers and Calendula

Idea 12 of 30

Hardy annuals are made for winter sowing: cornflowers, calendula, larkspur and poppies all germinate in cold soil and flower months earlier than spring-sown plants. Sow them thinly in December, and they will sit quietly through winter, then burst into growth in March. A jug of cornflowers sown in winter gives you flowers by May, a full month ahead of a spring sowing.

13

Larkspur in Winter Jugs

Idea 13 of 30

Larkspur hates being transplanted, which is why winter sowing suits it so well: the seeds germinate in the jug and grow on undisturbed until planting time. Sow the seeds on the surface in December and barely cover them, because larkspur needs light to germinate. The tall spires of blue, pink and white flowers appear in early summer, and they are wonderful for cutting.

14

Poppies That Need the Winter Cold

Idea 14 of 30

Poppies germinate best after a cold spell, and winter sowing gives them exactly that. Scatter the tiny seeds on the surface of damp compost in a jug and do not cover them — poppies need light to germinate, and they will sit dormant until the cold has done its work. In spring the seedlings appear, and by June you will have papery red, pink or orange flowers that self-seed for next year.

15

Nigella in a Milk Jug

Idea 15 of 30

Nigella, or love-in-a-mist, is a hardy annual that sows itself around the garden, so winter sowing is a natural fit. Sow the seeds thinly in a jug in December and leave them outside — they will germinate in early spring and flower by June. The feathery foliage and blue or white flowers are lovely in a border, and the seed pods dry beautifully for flower arrangements.

16

Borage for Spring Bees

Idea 16 of 30

Borage is a hardy annual that bees adore, and winter-sown plants flower weeks earlier than spring-sown ones. Sow the seeds in a jug in December and plant the seedlings out in March, and by May the starry blue flowers will be covered in bees. Borage self-seeds freely, so one winter sowing can stock your garden for years. The flowers are edible too, and lovely frozen in ice cubes.

17

Alyssum for Edging Beds

Idea 17 of 30

Sweet alyssum is a low, spreading annual that makes a wonderful edging plant, and it winter sows easily. Sow the tiny seeds on the surface of compost in a shallow box in December, because alyssum needs light to germinate. By April you will have dozens of small plants to tuck along the front of beds and paths, where their honey-scented white or purple flowers will bloom all summer.

18

Kale and Chard in Winter Jugs

Idea 18 of 30

Kale and chard are hardy vegetables that germinate happily in the cold, so they are ideal for winter sowing. Sow the seeds in a jug in January and they will sprout in March, giving you strong plants to set out in April. Winter-sown kale is stockier and tougher than indoor-sown plants, and it crops from summer right through to the following spring. Chard sown the same way gives you leaves all year.

19

Lettuce in a Milk Jug

Idea 19 of 30

Lettuce germinates in cool soil, so a winter sowing in January gives you the earliest salads of the year. Sow the seeds thinly in a jug and keep the lid on, and the seedlings will appear in March, weeks before a spring sowing would even start. Plant them out in April, a few at a time, and sow a second jug in February for a steady supply. Cut-and-come-again varieties keep cropping for months.

20

Peas in a Deep Jug

Idea 20 of 30

Peas are hardy enough for winter sowing, and a deep jug suits their long roots. Sow three or four seeds in a large jug in January, and they will germinate in the cold and grow slowly through late winter. By March the shoots are strong enough to plant out, and they will flower and pod weeks earlier than peas sown straight into cold ground. A cane or twiggy support helps them climb.

21

Broad Beans in a Milk Jug

Idea 21 of 30

Broad beans are one of the hardiest vegetables you can grow, and winter sowing gives them a head start. Sow one seed per jug in November or December, and the beans will germinate in the cold and grow into stocky plants by March. Plant them out after the worst frosts, spacing them twenty centimetres apart, and they will flower in April and pod by June. Pinch out the tips to keep blackfly away.

22

Parsley and Chives in Jugs

Idea 22 of 30

Herbs are slow to germinate, which makes them perfect for winter sowing: the long cold period gives them time. Sow parsley and chives in a jug in December, and they will sprout in March, ready to plant out in April. Parsley is famously slow — it can take a month to appear — so winter sowing suits it better than a spring rush. Chives come back every year once they are established.

23

Perennial Seeds That Need Chilling

Idea 23 of 30

Many perennial seeds will not germinate at all until they have had weeks of cold — a process called cold stratification. Winter sowing does this naturally: the seeds sit in the jug through the frosts, and the cold breaks their dormancy. Foxgloves, lupins, delphiniums and echinacea all need this chilling, and a winter jug gives it to them with no fridge space at all.

24

Echinacea from Winter-Sown Seed

Idea 24 of 30

Echinacea, or coneflower, needs a cold period before it germinates, so it is a natural for winter sowing. Sow the seeds in a jug in December and leave them outside — the winter cold breaks their dormancy, and they sprout in spring. The plants flower in their second year, with big purple daisies that bees love and that dry well for winter arrangements. Once established, echinacea comes back every year.

25

Lavender from Seed in Winter

Idea 25 of 30

Lavender from seed is slow, but winter sowing gives it the cold it needs to germinate. Sow the tiny seeds on the surface of gritty compost in a jug in December, and do not cover them — they need light. The seedlings appear in spring, and though they take a year to grow into proper plants, they are far cheaper than bought plants. Hidcote and Munstead are the best varieties to grow from seed.

26

Cold Stratification: Let Winter Do the Work

Idea 26 of 30

Cold stratification sounds technical, but it is simple: some seeds need weeks of cold and damp before they will germinate, because in nature they fall in autumn and sprout in spring. Winter sowing does the whole job for you — the jug sits outside through the frosts, and the seeds get their cold naturally. It beats wrapping seeds in damp paper in the fridge for six weeks, and it costs nothing.

27

Vent the Lids on Sunny Days

Idea 27 of 30

A closed jug in winter sun heats up fast, and seedlings can cook before you notice. On any sunny day, even in February, open the jug lids or peel back the tape and prop the top open an inch. The fresh air stops mould and damping off, and it toughens the seedlings for life outside. Close the lids again before dusk, so the night frost cannot get in.

28

Water Only When the Compost Dries

Idea 28 of 30

Winter-sown seeds rarely need watering, because the jug holds moisture and winter rain tops it up through the open cap. Check the compost every week by lifting the lid and pressing a finger into it — water only if the top feels dry. When you do water, use a fine rose or a spray bottle, so the seeds are not washed out of place. Overwatering rots seeds faster than drought ever does.

29

Transplant After the Last Frost

Idea 29 of 30

Winter-sown seedlings are tough, but they are still young plants, so do not rush them out. Wait until after your last frost date, then plant them out on a cloudy day or in the evening, so the sun does not wilt them. Water them in well and protect them from slugs for the first week. A seedling planted out at the right time grows away fast and catches up with indoor-sown plants.

30

Harden Off Seedlings Before Planting Out

Idea 30 of 30

Seedlings that have lived in a jug all winter are used to shelter, so harden them off before they go in the ground. For a week before planting out, open the lids during the day and close them at night, then leave the lids off altogether for the last two days. The seedlings will toughen up, their stems will thicken, and they will hardly check their growth when you plant them out.

Key Takeaways

  • Winter sowing turns old milk jugs into mini greenhouses that cost nothing.
  • Drainage and ventilation holes are essential — a sealed jug cooks seedlings.
  • Hardy annuals, hardy vegetables and perennials that need chilling are the best seeds.
  • Sow in December or January, let winter stratify the seeds, and watch them sprout in spring.
  • Harden seedlings off for a week, then transplant after the last frost.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start winter sowing seeds?

Start winter sowing in December or January, once the cold weather has arrived, and keep sowing into February. Seeds sown too early in autumn can germinate and then be killed by frost, so wait until the garden is properly cold. Hardy annuals and perennials go first, and tender vegetables like tomatoes wait until spring.

What containers can I use for winter sowing?

Any clear plastic container with a lid works: milk jugs, two-litre bottles, salad boxes, yoghurt tubs and large storage boxes. The container must have drainage holes in the base and ventilation holes in the lid, and it must let light through. Dark or opaque containers will not work, because seeds need light to grow.

Do winter sown seeds need watering?

Rarely — the closed jug holds moisture, and rain gets in through the open cap and the ventilation holes. Check the compost weekly by lifting the lid and pressing a finger in: water only if the top feels dry. Use a fine rose or a spray bottle, so the seeds are not washed out of place.

Which seeds are best for winter sowing?

Hardy annuals like sweet peas, cornflowers, calendula, larkspur, poppies and nigella are the best, along with hardy vegetables like kale, chard, peas, broad beans and lettuce. Perennials that need cold stratification — echinacea, foxgloves and lupins — also do well. Tender plants like tomatoes and courgettes wait until spring.

What is cold stratification and why does it matter?

Cold stratification is the weeks of cold and damp that some seeds need before they will germinate. In nature, seeds fall in autumn, spend winter in the cold ground, and sprout in spring. Winter sowing does this naturally, because the jug sits outside through the frosts. Without it, seeds like echinacea and foxgloves may never germinate at all.

When do winter sown seedlings appear?

Most winter sown seeds germinate in March or April, when the days lengthen and the soil in the jug warms up. Some, like sweet peas, may show in February in a mild winter. Do not worry if nothing appears for months — the seeds are waiting for the right conditions, and they will come up together when spring arrives.

When should I transplant winter sown seedlings?

Transplant seedlings after your last frost date, when they have two or three true leaves and the roots fill the jug. Harden them off first by opening the lids during the day for a week. Plant them out on a cloudy day or in the evening, water them in well, and protect them from slugs for the first week.

Can I winter sow seeds in a cold climate?

Yes — winter sowing works in cold climates, because the seeds stay dormant until the weather warms. In zones 3 to 5, sow in January or February, when the cold is reliable, and use a sheltered spot out of the wind. Check your hardiness zone and your last frost date, and sow to suit them.
