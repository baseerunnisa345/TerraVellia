26 Ways to Feed Birds in Your Winter Garden

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Winter is the hardest season for garden birds: natural food is scarce, the nights are long and cold, and a small bird can lose a tenth of its body weight overnight just keeping warm. Feeding birds in winter garden is the simplest way to help them through — a full feeder and fresh water can be the difference between surviving and not. These 26 ideas cover every way of feeding birds in your winter garden — sunflower hearts, peanuts, nyjer seed, suet, fat balls and mealworms, with the feeders, water and shelter that turn a garden into a winter refuge, and the hygiene and cat-proofing that keep your visitors safe. Before you start, check your hardiness zone and your last frost date: in cold zones, feeding matters most from the first frost until the last frost in spring, while in milder zones birds find natural food sooner and need less help.

1

Sunflower Hearts in a Tube Feeder

Idea 1 of 26

Sunflower hearts are the single best winter bird food: high in fat and energy, eaten whole with no husks left behind, and loved by almost every garden bird. Fill a tube feeder with hearts and hang it from a branch, and blue tits, great tits, finches and nuthatches will find it within a day. Hearts cost more than seed in the shell, but nothing is wasted, so a bag lasts well.

2

Peanuts in a Mesh Feeder

Idea 2 of 26

Peanuts are packed with fat and protein, which is exactly what birds need in cold weather, but they must always go in a mesh feeder, never loose. Whole peanuts can choke nestlings, so use a fine mesh feeder that lets birds peck small pieces off. Hang it near cover so tits and finches can dart in and out safely, and buy only bird-grade peanuts, never salted or roasted ones meant for people.

3

Nyjer Seed for Goldfinches

Idea 3 of 26

Nyjer seed is a tiny, oil-rich seed that goldfinches adore, and it is the surest way to bring them into your garden. It needs a special feeder with very small holes, because the seed is so fine it pours straight out of an ordinary one. Hang the feeder in the open, where goldfinches can see it, and be patient — it can take a few weeks for them to find it, but once they do they come back daily.

4

Fat Balls in a Suet Cage

Idea 4 of 26

Fat balls are a winter staple: balls of suet mixed with seed, nuts and mealworms that give birds a big hit of energy. Hang them in a wire suet cage, never in their plastic net, because birds' feet and beaks can tangle in the mesh. A cage keeps the ball together as birds peck at it, and it stops larger birds from carrying the whole thing off. Buy fat balls without netting.

5

Suet Blocks for Woodpeckers

Idea 5 of 26

A suet block in a cage feeder is the best way to attract woodpeckers, which cling to the cage and peck at the fat. Suet is pure energy, and in a hard winter it can be the difference between a woodpecker surviving and not. Choose blocks with added seed, insects or fruit, and hang the cage on a tree trunk or a sturdy post where a woodpecker can brace its tail against the bark.

6

Dried Mealworms for Robins

Idea 6 of 26

Dried mealworms are the food robins love most in winter, and they are also popular with blackbirds, wrens and dunnocks. Soak them in warm water for half an hour before putting them out, so they are soft and easy to eat, and offer them in a ground tray or on a bird table. Mealworms are soft-billed birds' food, so put them where ground feeders can reach them, under a shrub for cover.

7

Mixed Seed on a Ground Tray

Idea 7 of 26

A ground tray of mixed seed feeds the birds that will not use a hanging feeder: blackbirds, dunnocks, chaffinches and robins. Scatter a small amount each morning and clear away what is left at night, so rats are not attracted. A tray with a rim keeps the seed off the wet ground, and a roof or a cover keeps the rain off. Move the tray every few days so the ground underneath does not get fouled.

8

Apples and Pears on a Bird Table

Idea 8 of 26

Windfall apples and pears are a free winter food that blackbirds, thrushes and fieldfares love. Cut the fruit in half and set it on a bird table or spike it on a branch, and the birds will peck at it for days. Soft fruit is especially valuable in a hard frost, when the ground is frozen and birds cannot find worms. Use fruit that is going soft, but never mouldy fruit.

9

Homemade Fat Cakes in a Yoghurt Pot

Idea 9 of 26

A homemade fat cake costs very little and birds love it: melt lard or beef suet, stir in seed, peanuts, oats and dried fruit, and pour the mix into a yoghurt pot with a loop of string. Let it set in the fridge, then hang it from a branch. Never use polyunsaturated spreads or butter, which go soft and smear on feathers. Make a few at a time and keep the spares in the fridge.

10

No-Mess Seed Mix Under the Feeder

Idea 10 of 26

A no-mess seed mix — sunflower hearts, peanut pieces and kibbled maize with no husks — stops the pile of shells that builds up under a feeder and the weeds that sprout from it in spring. It is also the mix to use if your feeder hangs over a patio or a lawn you care about. The birds eat every bit of it, so there is less waste, less mess and fewer rats under the feeder.

11

Seed Mix for Finches and Tits

Idea 11 of 26

A good general seed mix suits finches, tits and sparrows, but check the label: cheap mixes are bulked out with wheat, barley and split peas that garden birds simply will not eat, and they end up rotting on the ground. Look for a mix with sunflower hearts, peanut pieces, millet and flaked maize, and buy it from a bird-food supplier rather than a supermarket bargain bin. Quality seed is cheaper in the long run.

12

Hang Feeders Near Cover

Idea 12 of 26

Birds will not use a feeder in the open, where a sparrowhawk can strike: hang your feeders near a shrub or a hedge, so birds can dart to cover in a second. The ideal spot is about two metres from dense cover — close enough to reach quickly, far enough that a cat cannot leap from the bush onto the feeder. Watch where the birds fly in from, and move the feeder until they use it confidently.

13

A Bird Table for Ground Feeders

Idea 13 of 26

A bird table is the classic way to feed ground feeders, and it suits blackbirds, thrushes, dunnocks and robins that will not use a hanging feeder. Choose a table with a rim to keep the seed on, a roof to keep the rain off, and a surface you can scrub clean. Put a little food out each morning rather than a lot at once, and clear the table at night so rats are not drawn in.

14

Keep Feeders at Different Heights

Idea 14 of 26

Different birds feed at different heights, so a garden with feeders at several levels attracts more species. Hang tube feeders high for tits and finches, put a bird table at waist height for blackbirds and thrushes, and scatter seed on a ground tray for dunnocks and robins. The mix also cuts down on squabbles, because birds that would fight over one feeder can feed at different levels in peace.

15

A Window Feeder for Close-Up Views

Idea 15 of 26

A window feeder brings birds right up to the glass, so you can watch a blue tit or a robin from your armchair. Suction-cup feeders stick to a clean window, and birds learn to use them within a few days, though you may need to scatter seed on the sill at first to show them the way. Keep the feeder small and fill it daily, because a window feeder holds little and empties fast.

16

Clean Feeders Every Week

Idea 16 of 26

Dirty feeders spread disease, and in winter, when birds crowd together to feed, the risk is highest. Take feeders down once a week, scrub them with hot soapy water, rinse well and let them dry before refilling. Use a separate brush for feeders, wear gloves, and never wash feeders in the kitchen sink. Clean feeders are the single most important thing you can do for the birds that visit you.

17

Fresh Water in a Bird Bath Every Day

Idea 17 of 26

Birds need water in winter just as much as food: they drink it, and they bathe in it to keep their feathers in good condition, and feathers are what keep them warm. Put out fresh water every morning in a bird bath or a shallow dish, and change it again if it gets dirty. A bath on the ground is fine, but one on a stand is safer, because cats cannot creep up on a bathing bird unseen.

18

A Heated Bird Bath in a Hard Frost

Idea 18 of 26

In a long hard frost, when every puddle and bath freezes, a heated bird bath is the only reliable water source in the garden. A low-wattage heater, made for bird baths, keeps a small patch of water open without making it hot, and it uses very little electricity. Put the bath near a socket, or use a solar-powered model, and check the water level daily, because open water evaporates even in cold weather.

19

A Shallow Dish for Drinking and Bathing

Idea 19 of 26

Birds bathe in shallow water, not deep: a dish with a gently sloping edge and water no more than five centimetres deep at the centre lets a robin or a blackbird splash safely. Add a few stones as perches, so small birds can stand and drink without getting soaked, and put the dish where you can see it — watching birds bathe is one of the great pleasures of a winter garden. Empty and scrub the dish weekly.

20

Plant Berries for Winter Birds

Idea 20 of 26

The best long-term way to feed birds is to plant for them: holly, rowan, hawthorn, cotoneaster and pyracantha all carry berries through winter, and birds eat them when feeders are empty. Plant a berry hedge or a rowan tree now, and in a few years it will feed fieldfares, redwings and waxwings as well as your garden birds. Leave the berries alone — they are the birds' food, not yours.

21

Leave Seed Heads Standing

Idea 21 of 26

Do not cut everything down in autumn: the seed heads of sunflowers, teasels, thistles and grasses are a winter larder for finches and sparrows. Goldfinches strip teasel heads all winter, and sparrows pick at grass seed in the borders. Leave the stems standing until spring, when you can cut them back before new growth starts. A messy corner of seed heads is worth more to birds than a tidy border.

22

Evergreen Shelter for Roosting Birds

Idea 22 of 26

Birds need somewhere to roost on a cold night, and evergreen shrubs are the best shelter a garden can offer: ivy, holly, yew and dense conifers hold the warmth of the day and break the wind. A bird roosting in thick evergreen can be several degrees warmer than one out in the open. Let your evergreens grow dense, and leave a few untrimmed — the birds will thank you on the coldest nights.

23

Put Up a Nest Box Now

Idea 23 of 26

Winter is the right time to put up a nest box, because birds use them as roosting shelters long before they nest in them. Several small birds may cram into one box on a cold night, sharing their warmth. Fix the box to a tree or a wall, facing north-east so it avoids the worst sun and wind, at least two metres up and clear of branches a cat could use. Clean last year's box out first.

24

A Brush Pile for Shelter

Idea 24 of 26

A brush pile — a loose heap of prunings and branches in a quiet corner — is a winter shelter that costs nothing and helps wrens, dunnocks and robins enormously. The birds roost in the gaps between the branches, out of the wind and out of sight of predators, and they find insects in the rotting wood. Build it in a corner you do not need, and add to it through the winter as you prune.

25

Keep Cats Away from the Feeders

Idea 25 of 26

Cats kill garden birds each year, and a feeder is the easiest place for a cat to hunt, because the birds are distracted. Put feeders at least two metres from any bush or fence a cat could leap from, and hang them high on a smooth metal pole a cat cannot climb. A bell on a cat's collar helps, and a thorny shrub under the feeder makes the approach uncomfortable. Never place a feeder where a cat can hide.

26

Squirrel-Proof Feeders That Work

Idea 26 of 26

A grey squirrel can empty a feeder in minutes, so in a garden with squirrels you need a feeder they cannot beat. A caged feeder with a metal grid lets small birds through but keeps a squirrel out, and a weight-sensitive feeder closes its ports under a squirrel's weight. Hang feeders on a smooth metal pole with a baffle, and never hang them from a branch a squirrel can jump from — they can leap two metres.

Key Takeaways

  • Feed every day from the first frost until the last frost in spring.
  • Sunflower hearts, peanuts, suet and mealworms cover almost every bird.
  • Fresh water matters as much as food — and never add salt or antifreeze.
  • Clean feeders weekly to stop disease spreading between visitors.
  • Give birds cover, shelter and a cat-free approach, and they will come back.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start feeding birds in winter?

Start when the weather turns cold and natural food gets scarce, usually late October or November, and keep feeding until the last frost in spring. Once you start, keep going every day — birds rely on your feeder, and in a hard frost a missed day can matter. Put food out in the morning, when birds are hungriest.

What is the best food for garden birds in winter?

Sunflower hearts are the best all-round winter food: high in fat, eaten by almost every garden bird, and with no husks left behind. Add peanuts in a mesh feeder, nyjer seed for goldfinches, and suet or fat balls for energy. A mix of these covers most species, and variety brings more birds than any single food. Avoid cheap mixes.

Should I feed birds bread?

Bread is not a good food for birds: white bread fills them up without giving them the fat and protein they need, and wet bread can go mouldy and make birds ill. Small amounts of wholemeal bread crumbs are acceptable as an occasional treat, but seed, peanuts and suet are far better. Never feed birds mouldy bread.

How often should I clean my bird feeders?

Clean feeders once a week in winter, when birds crowd together and disease spreads easily. Take the feeder down, scrub it with hot soapy water, rinse it well and let it dry before refilling. Wear gloves and use a separate brush, and never wash feeders in the kitchen sink.

Do birds need water in winter?

Yes — birds need water in winter for drinking and for bathing, and clean feathers are what keep a bird warm. Put out fresh water every morning, and never add salt or antifreeze, which are poisonous. A shallow dish with a sloping edge is safest.

How do I keep squirrels off my bird feeders?

Use a caged feeder or a weight-sensitive feeder that closes under a squirrel, hang it on a smooth metal pole with a baffle, and never hang it from a branch a squirrel can jump from. Squirrels can leap two metres, so give the feeder plenty of clear space. Offer squirrels food elsewhere, and they will bother your feeders less.

How do I stop cats catching birds at my feeders?

Place feeders at least two metres from any bush or fence a cat could leap from, hang them high on a smooth pole, and put a thorny shrub underneath. A bell on a cat's collar warns birds, and keeping your own cat indoors at dawn and dusk helps most. Never put a feeder where a cat can hide.

When should I stop feeding birds in spring?

Keep feeding until natural food is plentiful again, usually after the last frost in late spring, when insects, worms and seeds are easy for birds to find. Taper off gradually rather than stopping suddenly, so birds adjust. Keep water out all year round, and keep a feeder up for fledglings, which need easy food in their first weeks.
