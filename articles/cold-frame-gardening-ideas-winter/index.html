30 Cold Frame Gardening Ideas for Winter

Raised BedsUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

A cold frame is the simplest heated greenhouse you will ever own — except it has no heater at all. It is a low box with a transparent lid that catches the winter sun, shelters plants from frost and wind, and lets you grow salads and hardy herbs while the garden sleeps. These 30 ideas cover every part of cold frame gardening winter — choosing a spot, building a frame from timber, straw bales or an old window, insulating the lid, ventilating on sunny days, watering little, and choosing what to grow. In cold hardiness zones 3 to 6 a cold frame needs extra insulation and hardy plants only, while in mild zones 8 to 11 it stays frost-free most of the winter, so check your hardiness zone and your last frost date before you plant.

1

How a Cold Frame Works in Winter

Idea 1 of 30

A cold frame is a low, bottomless box with a transparent lid, and it works like a tiny greenhouse: the lid lets the winter sun in and traps the warmth, so the plants inside stay several degrees warmer than the open garden. At night the lid holds that warmth in, and on a cold night the frame can save a plant that would die outside. It needs no electricity — just sun, a lid and a little management.

2

A South-Facing Spot Out of the Wind

Idea 2 of 30

Put the frame where it gets the most winter sun: against a south-facing wall or fence, out of the cold north and east winds. A wall stores the day's heat and gives it back at night, so a frame beside one stays a few degrees warmer than a frame in the open. The spot should be close to the house, because you will open the lid, water and pick from it all winter. Avoid frosty hollows where cold air pools.

3

An Old Window Sash as a Lid

Idea 3 of 30

An old window sash makes the perfect cold frame lid: it is already glazed and framed, and the right size for a low box. Lay it on a timber or brick base, hinge it at the back so you can lift it to water and pick, and prop it open on sunny days. Check the glass for cracks and the putty for gaps first. One sash makes a small frame; two side by side make a generous one.

4

A Timber Frame Built in an Afternoon

Idea 4 of 30

A timber cold frame is a Saturday job: four boards screwed into a rectangle, a lid hinged at the back, and a sheet of glass or polycarbonate on top. Make the back board taller than the front so the lid slopes and rain runs off, and the low winter sun reaches the plants. Use untreated timber or line the inside with plastic, and set the frame on level ground. A metre-long frame half a metre wide suits most gardens.

5

A Straw Bale Cold Frame

Idea 5 of 30

Straw bales make a cold frame with no tools at all: lay them in a rectangle, fill the middle with soil, and rest a window or glass on top. The bales insulate the sides far better than timber, and they stay warm to the touch even on a frosty morning. They rot down in a season or two, so treat it as a one-winter build — and in spring, break the bales up and use the straw as mulch.

6

A Brick Cold Frame That Holds Heat

Idea 6 of 30

A brick cold frame is the warmest kind you can build: bricks store the day's heat and give it back slowly all night, so the inside stays frost-free longer than a timber frame. Lay a single course of bricks in a rectangle, fill the inside with soil, and rest a glazed lid on top. It is heavy and permanent, so choose the spot carefully. A brick frame against a sunny wall is the best cold frame of all.

7

Glass or Polycarbonate: Which Lid Is Best

Idea 7 of 30

Glass lets in the most light and lasts for decades, but it is heavy and it breaks. Polycarbonate is light, tough and insulating, but it scratches and it costs more. For a lid you lift every day, polycarbonate is the easier choice — a twin-wall sheet insulates better than a single pane of glass. For a lid that stays put, glass is hard to beat. Either way, the lid must be clear and clean, because winter light is precious.

8

Prop the Lid Open on Sunny Days

Idea 8 of 30

A closed cold frame in winter sun can hit 25 degrees by midday, then plunge below freezing at night, and that swing kills plants and breeds disease. Prop the lid open a few centimetres on every sunny day, even in January, to let damp air out and keep the temperature even. A brick, a block of wood or a notched stick makes a simple prop. Close the lid before dusk so the day's warmth stays trapped overnight.

9

An Automatic Vent Opener for the Lid

Idea 9 of 30

An automatic vent opener is a wax-filled cylinder that opens the lid as the frame warms up and closes it as it cools down — no electricity, no remembering. It is the best insurance against a sunny day cooking your plants while you are out. Fit one to the lid with the screws supplied, set it to start opening at about 15 degrees, and check it still moves freely in autumn. One opener per lid is enough for most frames.

10

Bank Straw Against the Outside Walls

Idea 10 of 30

Banking straw against the outside of a cold frame is the simplest insulation there is: it stops the wind stripping heat from the walls and keeps the soil inside warmer for longer. Pile dry straw bales or loose straw against the north and east sides, where the cold wind hits, and hold it in place with a board or a piece of wire. In spring, fork the straw onto the compost heap. It costs nothing and it works.

11

Bubble Wrap Under the Lid

Idea 11 of 30

A sheet of large-bubble wrap fixed under the lid is the cheapest insulation for a cold frame: it traps air against the glazing and can keep the inside several degrees warmer on a frosty night. Pin it to the underside of the lid in late autumn, leaving a gap at the front so air can move, and take it down in spring — it costs you light, and plants need every ray by March. Replace it when it goes brittle.

12

Old Carpet Over the Glass on Frosty Nights

Idea 12 of 30

On the coldest nights, a piece of old carpet over the glass is worth several degrees: it stops the heat escaping to the clear night sky, which is how frost forms. Lay it over the lid at dusk, weigh it down with bricks, and lift it off in the morning so the plants get light. Keep the carpet dry — wet carpet freezes to the glass and is heavy to lift. Hessian sacking works the same way.

13

Bank Soil Around the Outside

Idea 13 of 30

Banking soil up against the outside walls of a cold frame seals the gaps where cold air creeps in and warms the frame from the ground up. Heap soil or compost against the sides in autumn, up to the top of the frame, and firm it down. It also stops wind rattling the lid and keeps the frame steady in a storm. In spring, level the bank back down and fork the soil into the bed.

14

Water the Frame in the Morning Only

Idea 14 of 30

Plants in a cold frame need far less water than plants in the open: the lid keeps the rain off and the soil dries slowly in the cold. Water in the morning only, so the leaves and the surface dry before nightfall, and water the soil, never the leaves. Check with your finger first — if the top two centimetres feel damp, leave it. Wet plants on a frosty night freeze, and wet soil in the cold rots roots.

15

Winter Salad Leaves Under the Lid

Idea 15 of 30

A cold frame keeps winter salads growing when the garden is bare: sow lettuce, rocket, spinach, mizuna and lamb's lettuce in autumn, and pick the outer leaves all winter. The lid keeps the frost off and the rain away, so the leaves stay clean and crisp. Sow little and often, keep the soil just damp, and throw fleece over the plants inside the frame on the coldest nights for an extra few degrees of protection.

16

Hardy Herbs That Keep Growing

Idea 16 of 30

Parsley, chives, winter savory and thyme all keep growing in a cold frame through winter, giving you fresh herbs when the garden has none. Grow them in the soil of the frame or in pots stood inside it, and pick little and often so the plants stay bushy. Keep them on the dry side — wet herbs in a cold frame rot — and cut chives back hard in late winter before they take over the frame.

17

Overwinter Cuttings in the Frame

Idea 17 of 30

A cold frame is the best place to overwinter cuttings: it shelters them from frost and wind while keeping them cool enough to rest. Take hardwood cuttings of roses, currants and shrubs in autumn, push them into pots of gritty compost, and stand the pots in the frame. Keep the compost just damp, ventilate on sunny days, and leave the cuttings alone until spring, when they will root and leaf out.

18

Harden Off Seedlings in the Frame

Idea 18 of 30

Hardening off is the step most gardeners skip — and the reason seedlings collapse outside. A cold frame does it gently: move trays of seedlings into the frame for a week, opening the lid wider each day, then leave the lid open all day and shut at night. After ten days the plants are used to wind, sun and cold nights. Move them out for good only after the last frost, and they will barely check their growth.

19

Sow the First Spring Seeds Early

Idea 19 of 30

A cold frame lets you sow weeks earlier than the open garden: the soil inside warms up sooner, and the lid shelters the seedlings from frost. Sow broad beans, peas, early carrots and hardy salads in the frame from late February, and they will be up and growing while the garden is still cold. Sow thinly, label every row, and ventilate on sunny days so the seedlings do not cook. Check your last frost date before you sow tender things.

20

Force Rhubarb Under the Lid

Idea 20 of 30

Forcing rhubarb is one of the cold frame's oldest tricks: cover a rhubarb crown with the frame in late winter, shut out the light with carpet or sacking, and the stalks grow long, pale and sweet, weeks before the garden crop. The darkness makes them tender, so keep the lid covered and the frame shut. Pick the stalks when they are 30 centimetres long, then uncover the crown and let it recover for the rest of the year.

21

Overwinter Tender Plants in the Frame

Idea 21 of 30

Pelargoniums, fuchsias, tender succulents and young plants all overwinter in a cold frame if you keep them dry and frost-free. Cut them back by a third, stand the pots in the frame, and cover them with fleece on the coldest nights. Keep them just damp through winter, and give them the brightest spot in the frame. A cool rest is what they need — a warm frame forces soft growth that frost kills.

22

Succession Sowing in the Frame

Idea 22 of 30

Succession sowing keeps the frame full all winter: sow a row of salad every two weeks, and as one row is picked out, the next is ready. Sow lettuce, rocket, spinach and mizuna in small rows, label each sowing with the date, and pick the outer leaves so the plants keep producing. A frame sown in succession gives you a salad a week from autumn to spring, with never a gap and never a glut.

23

A Month-by-Month Cold Frame Calendar

Idea 23 of 30

A cold frame calendar keeps the work in order: in December, insulate the lid, check the frame for draughts and sow winter salads; in January, ventilate on sunny days, check for mould and start the first cuttings; in February, sow broad beans and peas, harden off seedlings and plan the spring. A simple list on the lid stops the big jobs being forgotten, and it reminds you what to do on a dark afternoon when the garden is asleep.

24

Do Not Seal the Frame Shut

Idea 24 of 30

Sealing a cold frame shut all winter feels sensible, but it is a mistake: still, damp air breeds grey mould and damping off, and plants suffocate without fresh air. Open the lid a crack on every mild, dry day, even for an hour, and let the air change. A cold frame needs to breathe, even in January — the plants inside are alive, and they need moving air as much as they need light and water.

25

Do Not Overwater in a Cold Frame

Idea 25 of 30

Overwatering is the commonest cold frame mistake, and it kills more plants than frost. In cold, slow growth, soil that stays wet for weeks rots the roots, and the plant collapses without warning. Water little, let the soil drain, and check with your finger before you water again. If in doubt, wait a day — a dry plant revives with a drink, but a rotted plant never comes back.

26

Do Not Sow Too Early

Idea 26 of 30

Sowing too early is the cold frame's classic trap: the seeds germinate in a warm spell, then a frost kills the seedlings overnight. Wait until the soil in the frame feels warm to your hand and the days are lengthening — late February for hardy salads, March for most other things. Check your last frost date and work back from it, not from the first sunny day. A seed sown a fortnight late always beats a seedling killed by frost.

27

Wind and Snow: Keep the Frame Standing

Idea 27 of 30

A cold frame is light and low, and a winter storm can lift the lid clean off or slide the whole frame across the garden. Weigh the lid down with a brick, peg the frame to the ground with stakes, and shelter it behind a wall or a hedge. Clear heavy snow off the lid with a soft brush so the glass does not crack under the weight, and never stand on the frame to reach it.

28

A Thermometer Inside the Frame

Idea 28 of 30

A max-min thermometer is the most useful tool in a cold frame: it records the coldest night and the hottest day, so you know whether the insulation is working and whether the lid needs opening. Stand it at plant height, out of direct sun, and check it every morning. If the night low keeps touching zero, add insulation or cover the lid at night; if the day high keeps soaring, ventilate more.

29

Condensation and Damping Off Under the Lid

Idea 29 of 30

Condensation is the cold frame's worst enemy: warm, damp air meets the cold lid, drips onto the seedlings, and damping off follows — seedlings that keel over at the base and die. Ventilate on every mild day, water in the morning, and space plants so air moves between them. Wipe the lid dry if drips fall on the plants, and pick off dead leaves at once. A little condensation is normal; puddles are not.

30

Use the Frame as a Mini Greenhouse in Spring

Idea 30 of 30

In spring, the cold frame becomes a mini greenhouse: open the lid wide by day, shut it at night, and grow on tomatoes, peppers and tender annuals weeks before they can go outside. It is the bridge between the windowsill and the garden — seedlings hardened off in the frame go out stronger than seedlings raised indoors. In May, take the lid off altogether, and the frame becomes a raised bed for summer salads.

Key Takeaways

  • A cold frame buys about one hardiness zone — know what it can and cannot protect.
  • Ventilate on sunny days and water little, in the morning — damp, still air breeds mould.
  • Bank straw or soil outside, bubble wrap under the lid, carpet over the glass on frosty nights.
  • Sow hardy things from late February, tender things only after the last frost.
  • Winter salads, herbs, cuttings and forced rhubarb are the crops a cold frame does best.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a cold frame and how does it work?

A cold frame is a low, bottomless box with a transparent lid that sits on the soil. The lid lets the winter sun in and traps the warmth, so the plants inside stay several degrees warmer than the open garden. It needs no heating or electricity, and it shelters plants from frost, wind and heavy rain all winter.

Where should I put a cold frame?

Put it against a south-facing wall or fence, out of the cold north and east winds, where it gets the most winter sun. A wall stores the day's heat and gives it back at night, so a frame beside one stays warmer. Keep it close to the house and the tap, and avoid low, frosty hollows where cold air pools.

What can I grow in a cold frame in winter?

Winter salads are the best crop: lettuce, rocket, spinach, mizuna, lamb's lettuce and winter herbs all grow slowly but steadily under the lid. You can also overwinter cuttings and tender plants, force rhubarb, and sow the first spring seeds early. Tender summer crops need real warmth, so save those for spring.

How do I stop a cold frame overheating on sunny days?

Prop the lid open a few centimetres on every sunny day, even in January, and shut it before dusk. A closed frame in winter sun can reach 25 degrees by midday, then plunge below freezing at night. An automatic vent opener does the job for you, opening as the frame warms and closing as it cools.

How often should I water a cold frame in winter?

Far less than in summer. Water in the morning only, and only when the top two centimetres of soil feel dry. The lid keeps rain off and the soil dries slowly in the cold, so wet soil rots roots. Check with your finger before every watering, and if in doubt, wait a day.

Do I need to insulate a cold frame?

Yes, on the coldest nights. Bank straw or soil against the outside walls, pin bubble wrap under the lid, and throw old carpet over the glass at dusk. In cold hardiness zones 3 to 6, insulation is essential; in mild zones 8 to 11, the frame alone is often enough.

When can I sow seeds in a cold frame?

Sow hardy salads, broad beans and peas from late February, when the soil in the frame feels warm to your hand and the days are lengthening. Sow tender things only after your last frost date — a seedling killed by frost is worse than a seed sown a fortnight late. Sow thinly, label every row, and ventilate on sunny days.

Can a cold frame replace a greenhouse?

For hardy salads, cuttings and overwintering, yes — a cold frame does the same job as an unheated greenhouse, for far less money. It cannot do what a heated greenhouse does: tender crops and citrus need real warmth. Think of the frame as a mini greenhouse for the hardy end of the garden.
