29 Microgreens to Grow Indoors This Winter

Vegetable GardeningUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Microgreens are the fastest food you can grow in winter: sow a tray of seed on a Monday and you can be cutting peppery radish or tender pea shoots before the weekend is over. These 29 ideas cover every part of grow microgreens indoors winter — which crops to grow, which trays and growing mediums to use, how much light and water they need, and how to cut and store the harvest. Because microgreens grow indoors, your hardiness zone and your last frost date do not decide the harvest — a warm windowsill, a shallow tray and a few minutes a day do.

1

Radish Microgreens in Seven Days

Idea 1 of 29

Radish microgreens are the fastest crop you can grow indoors in winter, ready to cut just seven days after sowing. Scatter the seed thickly over damp compost in a shallow tray, press it down gently, and cover the tray for two dark days so the seed germinates evenly. Once the seedlings stand up, give them bright light and mist them daily. The pink stems and green leaves taste mildly peppery, and they are wonderful on sandwiches and in salads.

2

Broccoli Microgreens for Everyday Nutrition

Idea 2 of 29

Broccoli microgreens are the everyday staple of the microgreen tray, mild enough for children and quick enough to sow every week. Scatter the tiny seed evenly over damp compost, cover it for two days, then move the tray into bright light. The seedlings are ready to cut in ten to fourteen days, when the first true leaves appear. They are mild and fresh, good in salads, sandwiches and smoothies, and they regrow poorly, so sow a fresh tray each week.

3

Pea Shoots on a Windowsill

Idea 3 of 29

Pea shoots are the tallest microgreens you can grow, and they are the most generous: soak the peas overnight, sow them thickly in a deep tray of compost, and cover them until they sprout. They need a little more room than other microgreens, and they like a bright, cool windowsill. Cut the shoots when they are ten centimetres tall, just above the lowest leaves, and the plants will often regrow for a second picking.

4

Sunflower Microgreens with Big Cotyledons

Idea 4 of 29

Sunflower microgreens are the most satisfying to grow, because the seeds are big, the shoots are sturdy, and the harvest is generous. Soak the seed overnight, sow it thickly on damp compost, and weight the tray down for two days so the roots grip. The thick stems and lemon-yellow cotyledons taste nutty and fresh, and they are wonderful in salads and sandwiches. They need a deeper tray than most microgreens.

5

Beet Microgreens for Colour

Idea 5 of 29

Beet microgreens earn their place for colour alone: the stems are deep red, the leaves are dark green, and a handful turns a plain salad into something special. Sow the seed on damp compost, cover it for three days, and give the tray bright light. They are slower than radish, taking about two weeks, and the flavour is mild and earthy. Cut them young, when the stems are still tender and sweet.

6

Kale Microgreens, Cut Young

Idea 6 of 29

Kale microgreens taste nothing like the tough leaves of a garden kale plant: cut young, they are mild, sweet and tender. Sow the seed thickly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and move the tray into the light. They are ready in ten to twelve days, when the first true leaves are the size of a fingernail. Purple varieties look beautiful, and they are just as easy to grow as green ones.

7

Mustard Microgreens with a Warm Bite

Idea 7 of 29

Mustard microgreens bring a warm, peppery bite to winter food, and they are ready in about a week. Sow the seed thickly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and give the tray bright light. Cut the leaves young, when they are mild, because older leaves turn hot and tough. A small tray is enough for a household, because a little goes a long way in salads, sandwiches and noodle bowls.

8

Arugula Microgreens, Peppery and Fast

Idea 8 of 29

Arugula microgreens, also called rocket, are ready to cut in about ten days, and they carry the same peppery flavour as the full-grown leaf. Sow the tiny seed thinly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and move the tray into bright light. The leaves are best cut young, when they are mild and nutty, and they are wonderful with tomatoes, cheese and eggs. Sow a fresh tray every week to keep the supply going.

9

Red Cabbage Microgreens for Purple Leaves

Idea 9 of 29

Red cabbage microgreens are one of the prettiest crops you can grow indoors: the leaves are deep purple, the stems are pink, and they glow on a winter plate. Sow the seed on damp compost, cover it for two days, and give the tray plenty of light, which deepens the colour. They are ready in about twelve days, mild and crisp, and they are lovely in salads, slaws and tacos.

10

Kohlrabi Microgreens with Purple Stems

Idea 10 of 29

Kohlrabi microgreens are a quiet favourite: the stems are purple, the leaves are green, and the flavour is mild and sweet, like a young cabbage. Sow the seed thickly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and move the tray into bright light. They are ready in ten to twelve days, and they are good in salads and sandwiches. Purple kohlrabi is the prettiest variety, and it grows just as fast as the green kind.

11

Amaranth Microgreens for Red Leaves

Idea 11 of 29

Amaranth microgreens are the reddest crop on the windowsill, with deep burgundy leaves that colour any salad. Sow the tiny seed on the surface of damp compost — do not bury it, because it needs light to germinate — and mist it gently. It is slower than radish, taking two to three weeks, and it likes warmth, so keep the tray in the warmest spot you have. The flavour is mild and earthy.

12

Basil Microgreens for Warm Rooms

Idea 12 of 29

Basil microgreens are the slowest and warmest crop in this list, and they reward patience with the sweetest scent of any microgreen. Sow the tiny seed on damp compost, keep the tray warm — above 20 degrees — and be patient, because germination can take a week. They are ready in about three weeks, and the small leaves taste intensely of basil. Use them on pasta, pizza and tomatoes, and keep the tray away from cold draughts.

13

Cilantro Microgreens, Slow but Worth It

Idea 13 of 29

Cilantro microgreens are slow to germinate, taking up to two weeks, but they are worth the wait for the fresh, citrusy flavour. Crush the seed husks gently before sowing, scatter the seed on damp compost, and keep the tray warm and covered. Once the seedlings appear, give them bright light and mist them daily. They are ready in about three weeks, and the leaves are wonderful in curries, tacos and noodle dishes.

14

Dill Microgreens for Fish and Salads

Idea 14 of 29

Dill microgreens are the feathery, fragrant crop for fish, eggs and salads, and they are easy to grow indoors. Sow the seed thinly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and move the tray into bright light. The seedlings are ready in about two weeks, when the fronds are soft and full of flavour. Cut them with scissors and use them fresh, because dried dill microgreens lose their scent. They like a cool windowsill better than a warm room.

15

Cress Microgreens on Damp Paper

Idea 15 of 29

Cress is the classic first microgreen, and it will grow on damp paper with no compost at all, which makes it perfect for children. Line a tray with folded kitchen paper, wet it, scatter the seed thickly, and cover the tray for a day. The seedlings appear in two days and are ready to cut in a week. They taste fresh and peppery, and they are wonderful in egg sandwiches. Keep the paper damp, never dry and never swimming.

16

Buckwheat Microgreens for Mild Leaves

Idea 16 of 29

Buckwheat microgreens are a gentle, mild crop, with pale green leaves and a soft, lettuce-like flavour. Soak the seed for a few hours, sow it thickly on damp compost, and cover the tray for two days. They are ready in about ten days, and they are good in salads and smoothies. Buckwheat grows tall and can go leggy in dim light, so give the tray your brightest windowsill and cut the leaves while they are still young and tender.

17

Wheatgrass for Juicing at Home

Idea 17 of 29

Wheatgrass is the microgreen for juicing: the tall, bright green blades are cut at fifteen centimetres and juiced fresh, and they are packed with chlorophyll. Soak the wheat berries overnight, sow them thickly on damp compost, and cover the tray for two days. The grass is ready in seven to ten days, and you cut it once, at the base, and juice it straight away. It does not regrow, so sow a fresh tray every week.

18

Chard Microgreens for Bright Stems

Idea 18 of 29

Chard microgreens give you two colours in one tray: the leaves are dark green and the stems are bright red, yellow or pink. Sow the seed on damp compost, cover it for three days, and give the tray bright light. They are slower than most microgreens, taking two to three weeks, and the flavour is mild and earthy. Cut the leaves young, when the stems are still tender, and use them in salads and pasta dishes.

19

Mizuna Microgreens in Winter

Idea 19 of 29

Mizuna microgreens are the feathery, mild mustard leaf of the winter tray, and they grow fast in cool rooms. Sow the seed thickly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and move the tray into bright light. The leaves are ready in about ten days, and they are mild and fresh when cut young. They are wonderful in salads and stir-fries, and they cope with a north-facing windowsill better than most microgreens.

20

Pak Choi Microgreens for Stir-Fries

Idea 20 of 29

Pak choi microgreens are the stir-fry crop of the winter windowsill, mild and crisp, with a gentle cabbage flavour. Sow the seed thickly on damp compost, cover it for two days, and give the tray bright light. The leaves are ready in ten to twelve days, when the first true leaves appear. Cut them with scissors and add them to stir-fries, noodle soups and salads at the last minute, so they stay crisp.

21

Popcorn Shoots for Sweet Crunch

Idea 21 of 29

Popcorn shoots are the sweetest microgreens you can grow, with a crunchy stem and a flavour like sweetcorn. Soak the popcorn kernels overnight, sow them thickly on damp compost, and weight the tray down for two days so the roots grip. They are ready in about ten days, when the shoots are yellow-green and tender. Keep them in the dark a day longer than other crops for the sweetest, palest shoots.

22

Nasturtium Microgreens, Peppery and Pretty

Idea 22 of 29

Nasturtium microgreens are peppery, pretty and edible, with round leaves that taste like a mild radish. Soak the seed overnight, sow it in a tray of compost, and give the tray a warm, bright spot. The seedlings are ready in about two weeks, and the leaves are wonderful in salads, where they add colour and bite. The flowers are edible too, though they rarely appear on plants cut this young.

23

A Shallow Tray and Fine Compost Setup

Idea 23 of 29

The setup for microgreens is simple: a tray about five centimetres deep, with drainage holes, filled with fine, damp seed compost. Fill the tray level, press the compost down gently, and water it before you sow, so the seed sits on damp ground. Shallow trays warm up fast on a sunny windowsill, which helps the seed germinate, and they are easy to stack. Just remember they dry out quickly, so check the water daily.

24

Coco Coir as a Clean Growing Medium

Idea 24 of 29

Coco coir is the cleanest growing medium for microgreens: it is light, holds water well, and brings no weed seed or soil-borne disease into the house. Soak a coir brick in water, fluff it up, and fill the tray to the brim. Sow the seed on top, mist it, and keep the coir damp like a wrung-out sponge. Coir holds no food, so the seedlings rely on the seed itself, which is why you cut them young.

25

Hemp Mats for Soilless Microgreens

Idea 25 of 29

Hemp mats let you grow microgreens with no soil at all, which means no mess on the windowsill. Cut the mat to fit the tray, soak it until it is damp, and scatter the seed on top. The roots grip the fibres, and the mat holds water for days. Keep the mat damp with a mister, and cut the microgreens just above the mat. Hemp mats are compostable, so the whole mat goes in the compost bin after harvest.

26

Misting, Not Watering, Your Microgreens

Idea 26 of 29

Microgreens are watered with a mister, not a can, because a jet of water flattens the seedlings and washes the seed away. Mist the surface once or twice a day, just enough to keep the compost damp, and water from below when the tray feels light. A tray that is too wet grows mould, while a tray that is too dry stops growing. Always mist in the morning, so the leaves dry before night.

27

A Small LED Light Above the Trays

Idea 27 of 29

In the darkest weeks of winter, a small LED grow light can double the speed of your microgreens. Hang the light about fifteen centimetres above the trays, and switch it on for fourteen to sixteen hours a day. Keep the light close, because a light hung too high does nothing, and raise it as the seedlings grow. Microgreens under lights grow stocky and green, instead of pale and leggy.

28

Harvesting at the Cotyledon Stage

Idea 28 of 29

Microgreens are cut at the cotyledon stage, when the seed leaves have opened and the first true leaves are just appearing — usually seven to twenty-one days after sowing. Cut with clean scissors, just above the growing medium, and cut in the morning, when the leaves are crisp and full of water. Cut only what you need, because cut microgreens wilt fast, and a tray left standing keeps growing for a few more days.

29

Storing Cut Microgreens in the Fridge

Idea 29 of 29

Cut microgreens keep for three to five days in the fridge if you store them dry. Line a container with a dry paper towel, lay the cut leaves on top, cover them loosely, and keep the container in the salad drawer. Do not wash microgreens until you eat them, because wet leaves go slimy fast. If the leaves wilt, a five-minute soak in cold water will often bring them back to life.

Key Takeaways

  • Microgreens are cut at the cotyledon stage, seven to twenty-one days after sowing — not grown to full size.
  • Sow thickly in a shallow tray, give them a dark blackout period, then bright light.
  • Mist the surface daily and water from below — wet leaves and soggy compost bring mould.
  • Cut with scissors just above the medium, in the morning, and eat them the same day.
  • A new tray every week means fresh microgreens all winter, whatever the weather outside.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are microgreens, and how are they different from sprouts?

Microgreens are plants grown in soil or on a mat in a shallow tray, cut with scissors just above the medium when the first true leaves appear. Sprouts are seeds grown in a jar with water only, rinsed daily, and eaten whole, roots and all. Microgreens need light and a growing medium; sprouts need only water and darkness.

How long do microgreens take to grow indoors in winter?

Most microgreens take seven to twenty-one days from sowing to cutting, depending on the crop. Radish, mustard and cress are ready in seven to ten days, while basil, cilantro and amaranth take two to three weeks. Winter light is weaker, so a bright windowsill or a small grow light grows them fastest, and warmth helps slow crops along.

Do microgreens need grow lights in winter?

Microgreens do not need grow lights if you have a bright, south-facing windowsill, but a small LED light helps in December and January, when daylight is short. Hang the light fifteen centimetres above the trays for fourteen to sixteen hours a day. Without enough light, seedlings grow pale, leggy and slow.

What growing medium is best for microgreens?

Fine seed compost is the simplest medium: it holds water, suits every crop, and gives roots something to grip. Coco coir is cleaner and lighter, with no weed seed, but it holds no food. Hemp mats are the tidiest option, with no soil at all, and they compost after harvest. Any of the three works.

How do I stop microgreens going mouldy?

Mould comes from wet, still air, so keep the tray damp, never soaking, and give the seedlings fresh air every day. Mist in the morning, water from below, and never leave water standing in the saucer. Sow the seed evenly, not in clumps. If mould appears, improve the air flow and cut the healthy leaves early.

When should I cut microgreens?

Cut microgreens when the cotyledons have opened fully and the first true leaves are just appearing, which is usually seven to twenty-one days after sowing. Use clean scissors, cut just above the growing medium, and cut in the morning, when the leaves are crisp. Cut only what you need for the day, because cut microgreens wilt quickly.

Can I regrow microgreens after cutting them?

Most microgreens do not regrow after cutting, because you cut off the growing point with the leaves. Pea shoots are the exception — they often regrow for a second picking if you cut above the lowest leaves. For everything else, sow a fresh tray each week instead. A weekly sowing routine gives you a steady supply all winter.

Are microgreens safe to eat raw?

Yes, microgreens are safe to eat raw when grown cleanly indoors, in fresh compost or on a mat, with clean water and clean trays. Wash your hands before handling them, rinse the cut leaves gently, and eat them the same day you cut them. Do not eat microgreens from a mouldy tray, and throw away any tray that smells sour.
