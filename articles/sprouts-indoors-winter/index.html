29 Sprouts to Grow Indoors This Winter

Vegetable GardeningUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Sprouts are the fastest fresh food you can grow in winter: soak a jar of seed on a Monday and you can be eating crisp, crunchy sprouts before the weekend is over. These 29 ideas cover every part of growing sprouts indoors winter — which seeds to sprout, how to soak, rinse and drain a jar, how to tell root hairs from mould, and how to use the harvest. Sprouts are eaten raw, so clean jars, clean water and twice-daily rinsing matter more than anything. Because sprouts grow indoors, your hardiness zone and your last frost date do not decide the harvest — a warm kitchen, a clean jar and a few minutes a day do.

1

Alfalfa Sprouts in a Jar

Idea 1 of 29

Alfalfa is the classic jar sprout, and the easiest place to start: the seeds are tiny, they sprout in three to four days, and they taste mild and fresh. Soak a tablespoon of seed overnight, rinse it twice a day, and keep the jar in the dark until the sprouts green up. They are wonderful in sandwiches and salads, and one jar gives you a generous handful every day.

2

Mung Bean Sprouts for Stir-Fries

Idea 2 of 29

Mung bean sprouts are the crunchy star of stir-fries, and they grow faster than almost any other sprout. Soak the beans overnight, then rinse them twice a day and keep the jar in a warm, dark place. In three to five days you will have crisp, white sprouts with small green leaves. Add them to a hot wok at the very last minute, so they stay crunchy and fresh.

3

Lentil Sprouts, Cheap and Fast

Idea 3 of 29

Lentils are the cheapest seed you can sprout, and they are ready in just two to three days. Use whole green or brown lentils — never split red lentils, which turn to mush. Soak them for eight hours, rinse twice a day, and eat them when the tails are about a centimetre long. They are nutty, filling and full of protein, and they cost pennies a jar.

4

Chickpea Sprouts for Hummus

Idea 4 of 29

Chickpea sprouts make the smoothest, freshest hummus you will ever taste, and they are easy to grow in a jar. Soak the chickpeas for twelve hours, then rinse them twice a day until the tails appear. They take three to four days, and they are ready when the tails are about a centimetre long. Blend them with tahini, lemon and garlic for a hummus that tastes alive.

5

Radish Sprouts with a Peppery Bite

Idea 5 of 29

Radish sprouts bring a warm, peppery bite to winter food, and they are ready in four to five days. Soak the tiny seed for six hours, then rinse twice a day and keep the jar in the dark for the first two days. The sprouts turn pink and green as they grow, and they look beautiful in salads. Use a small handful at a time, because the flavour is strong.

6

Broccoli Sprouts for Everyday Health

Idea 6 of 29

Broccoli sprouts are the most nutritious sprout you can grow, and they are ready in four to five days. Soak the tiny seed overnight, rinse twice a day, and keep the jar in the dark until the sprouts are about two centimetres long. They taste mild and fresh, like young broccoli, and they are wonderful in sandwiches, salads and smoothies. A tablespoon of seed fills a whole jar.

7

Clover Sprouts, Mild and Crunchy

Idea 7 of 29

Clover sprouts are the mildest sprout in the jar, with a gentle, grassy flavour and a satisfying crunch. Soak the tiny seed overnight, rinse twice a day, and keep the jar out of direct sun until the sprouts green up. They are ready in four to six days, and they are lovely in sandwiches and wraps, where their mildness lets other flavours shine. Children usually love them.

8

Fenugreek Sprouts for Warmth

Idea 8 of 29

Fenugreek sprouts are the warming sprout of winter, with a flavour like maple syrup and curry leaves. Soak the hard, golden seeds for twelve hours, then rinse twice a day and keep the jar warm. They are ready in four to five days, when the tails are short and the leaves are just opening. Use them sparingly in curries and salads, because the flavour is strong and slightly bitter.

9

Sunflower Sprouts in a Jar

Idea 9 of 29

Sunflower sprouts grown in a jar are crunchy, nutty and generous, with thick stems and big leaves. Soak the hulled seed overnight, rinse twice a day, and keep the jar in the dark for two days so the roots grip. They are ready in five to seven days, when the leaves have opened. Eat them whole — seed, stem and leaves — in salads and sandwiches.

10

Green Pea Sprouts in a Jar

Idea 10 of 29

Green pea sprouts are the sweetest jar sprout, with crisp stems and a flavour like fresh peas. Soak whole dried peas overnight, rinse twice a day, and keep the jar in the dark until the shoots are about five centimetres tall. They are ready in five to seven days, and they are wonderful in salads, stir-fries and noodle bowls. Eat them whole, pods and all.

11

Choosing the Right Jar for Sprouting

Idea 11 of 29

The right jar makes sprouting easy: choose a wide-mouthed glass jar of about one litre, so you can reach inside to rinse and harvest. Glass is best, because it is easy to clean and you can see the sprouts growing. Avoid narrow-necked bottles, which trap water and make rinsing hard. A jam jar works well for a first batch, and a kilner jar is better for larger ones.

12

Mesh Lids for Easy Rinsing

Idea 12 of 29

A mesh lid turns an ordinary jar into a sprouting jar, because you can rinse and drain without ever opening it. Buy a stainless-steel mesh lid, or make one from a square of muslin held on with a rubber band. Rinse by filling the jar with water, swirling it, and tipping it out through the mesh. The lid keeps the sprouts in and the fruit flies out.

13

A Sprout Bag for the Kitchen

Idea 13 of 29

A sprout bag is a cloth bag that holds the seed while you rinse it under the tap, and it is the simplest sprouting kit there is. Soak the seed in the bag, hang the bag on a hook, and rinse it twice a day under running water. The cloth drains fast, so the seed never sits in water. Sprout bags are light, cheap and easy to wash, and they travel well.

14

A Sprouting Tray That Drains Itself

Idea 14 of 29

A sprouting tray is a shallow tray with drainage holes and a lid, made for growing larger batches of sprouts. Spread the soaked seed in a thin layer, rinse it twice a day by pouring water through the tray, and let it drain into the tray below. The tray keeps the sprouts in the dark and stops them drying out. It is the best option for mung beans and chickpeas.

15

How Much Seed to Use in Each Jar

Idea 15 of 29

The most common sprouting mistake is using too much seed, which packs the jar and stops the air moving. Start with one tablespoon of small seed, like alfalfa or broccoli, or three tablespoons of large seed, like mung beans or chickpeas. The seed will swell to many times its dry size, and the sprouts need room to breathe. A jar that looks half empty at sowing time is just right.

16

Where to Keep the Jar in Winter

Idea 16 of 29

Sprouts like warmth and darkness, so keep the jar in a kitchen cupboard or on a shelf away from the window. A steady temperature of about 18 to 22 degrees is ideal, and a warm kitchen is usually perfect in winter. Keep the jar out of direct sun, which heats the glass and dries the seed. A dark cupboard is better than a sunny windowsill for the first few days.

17

Soaking Times for Every Seed

Idea 17 of 29

Every seed has its own soaking time, and getting it right is the first step to good sprouts. Soak small seed, like alfalfa, broccoli and radish, for four to six hours; soak medium seed, like lentils and mung beans, for eight to twelve hours; and soak large seed, like chickpeas and peas, for twelve hours. Use cool, clean water, and rinse the seed well after soaking.

18

Rinsing Twice a Day, Every Day

Idea 18 of 29

Rinsing is the most important job in sprouting, because it washes away the bacteria that grow in warm, damp seed. Rinse the sprouts twice a day, morning and evening, by filling the jar with cool water, swirling it gently, and draining it fully. Never skip a rinse, even when the sprouts look fine, because bacteria are invisible. Clean water and a good drain keep sprouts safe.

19

Draining the Jar at an Angle

Idea 19 of 29

Sprouts must never sit in water, because standing water is where bacteria and mould grow. After every rinse, drain the jar fully, then rest it upside down at an angle, in a bowl or a dish rack, so the last drops run out. The mesh lid lets the water escape while the sprouts stay inside. A jar that drains well smells fresh; a jar that does not smells sour.

20

The Dark Period for Strong Sprouts

Idea 20 of 29

Sprouts grow stronger in the dark, because darkness makes the stems stretch and the roots grip. Keep the jar in a cupboard, or cover it with a tea towel, for the first two to three days after soaking. The sprouts will be pale and yellow at first, and that is normal. Once they are two centimetres long, move the jar into the light so the leaves turn green.

21

Greening Sprouts in the Light

Idea 21 of 29

The last day of sprouting is the greening day, when the sprouts move into the light and the leaves turn green. Put the jar on a windowsill, out of direct sun, for six to twelve hours, and the pale shoots will colour up. Green sprouts taste fresher and look better in salads, and the light also helps the leaves open. Do not leave them in the sun for days, or they will dry out.

22

When Sprouts Are Ready to Eat

Idea 22 of 29

Sprouts are ready to eat when the tails are about two to five centimetres long and the first leaves have opened. Small seed, like alfalfa and broccoli, is ready in three to five days; large seed, like chickpeas and peas, takes five to seven days. Taste a sprout: it should be crisp, fresh and sweet, with no sour smell. If the sprouts smell sour or look slimy, throw them away.

23

A Second Batch Every Three Days

Idea 23 of 29

The secret to fresh sprouts all winter is not one big jar but many small ones. Start a new jar every three days, and by the time the first jar is finished, the next one is ready to eat. Keep a simple routine: soak on Monday and Thursday, rinse morning and evening, and harvest on the third day. Three jars in rotation give you sprouts every day from December to March.

24

Root Hairs or Mould? How to Tell

Idea 24 of 29

New sprouters often mistake root hairs for mould, and throw away perfectly good sprouts. Root hairs are fine, white, even fuzz that grows in a neat ring around each root, and they appear in the first two days. Mould is patchy, grey or black, and it smells musty. If the fuzz is even and the sprouts smell fresh, they are root hairs, and the sprouts are safe to eat.

25

Keeping Jars and Lids Spotlessly Clean

Idea 25 of 29

Clean jars are the foundation of safe sprouts, because bacteria love warm, damp places. Wash every jar and lid in hot, soapy water before each new batch, and rinse them well. Once a week, soak the mesh lids in a little white vinegar to dissolve any mineral build-up. Never reuse a jar that smells sour, and never sprout in a jar that has held chemicals or paint.

26

Why Clean Water Matters for Raw Sprouts

Idea 26 of 29

Sprouts are eaten raw, so the water you rinse them with ends up in your food. Always use clean, drinkable tap water for soaking and rinsing, and never use water from a garden butt or a pond. If your tap water tastes of chlorine, leave a jug of it standing overnight, so the chlorine escapes. Clean water, clean jars and a good drain are the three rules of safe sprouting.

27

Storing Sprouts in the Fridge

Idea 27 of 29

Sprouts keep for three to five days in the fridge if you store them dry. Rinse the finished sprouts once more, drain them well, and spread them on a clean tea towel to dry for an hour. Then pack them loosely in a container lined with a dry paper towel, and keep it in the salad drawer. Do not wash sprouts again until you eat them, because wet sprouts go slimy fast.

28

Sprouts in Sandwiches and Wraps

Idea 28 of 29

Sprouts were made for sandwiches: a generous handful adds crunch, freshness and flavour to every bite. Pile alfalfa, clover or radish sprouts on bread with cheese, egg or cold meat, and add a little mustard or mayonnaise. The sprouts keep the sandwich moist without making it soggy, and they turn a plain lunch into something special. Use them the day you harvest them for the best crunch.

29

Sprouts in Winter Salads and Soups

Idea 29 of 29

A handful of sprouts can lift a winter salad or a bowl of soup from plain to special. Scatter mung bean, lentil or pea sprouts over a salad of lettuce and beetroot, or stir them into a hot soup just before serving, so they stay crisp. The sprouts add crunch, colour and a fresh, green flavour that winter food often lacks. Eat them the same day for the best taste and texture.

Key Takeaways

  • Sprouts grow in a jar with water only — no soil, no trays, no grow lights needed.
  • Soak the seed, then rinse twice a day and drain fully — standing water is what brings mould.
  • Root hairs are fine and even; mould is patchy and smells — when in doubt, throw them out.
  • Eat sprouts within a few days, stored dry in the fridge, and rinse them before eating.
  • A new jar every three days means fresh sprouts all winter, whatever the weather outside.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are sprouts, and how are they different from microgreens?

Sprouts are seeds grown in a jar, rinsed twice a day, and eaten whole — seed, root, stem and leaves — in three to seven days. Microgreens are seeds sown on soil or a mat in a tray, grown in light, and cut with scissors at the cotyledon stage. Sprouts need no soil, no light and no growing medium.

How long do sprouts take to grow indoors in winter?

Most sprouts take three to seven days from soaking to eating, depending on the seed. Lentils and alfalfa are ready in two to four days, mung beans and radish in three to five, and chickpeas and peas in five to seven. A warm kitchen is enough, because sprouts grow in the dark and need no sunlight until greening day.

How often should I rinse sprouts?

Rinse sprouts twice a day, morning and evening, without fail. Fill the jar with cool, clean water, swirl it gently, and drain it fully through the mesh lid. Rinsing washes away bacteria and keeps the seed damp, and draining stops the water standing. Never skip a rinse, even when the sprouts look fine, because bacteria are invisible to the eye.

How do I tell root hairs from mould on sprouts?

Root hairs are fine, white, even fuzz in a neat ring around each root, and the sprouts smell fresh. Mould is patchy, grey or black, often in clumps, and it smells musty or sour. If the fuzz is even and the jar smells fresh, they are root hairs. If it smells wrong, throw the batch away.

Are sprouts safe to eat raw?

Yes, sprouts are safe to eat raw when they are grown cleanly: clean jars, clean drinkable water, rinsing twice a day, and a full drain after every rinse. Only sprout seed sold for sprouting, never garden seed, which may be treated. Throw away any batch that smells sour or looks slimy, and never eat sprouts from a mouldy jar.

Which sprouts are best for beginners?

Alfalfa, mung bean and lentil are the best sprouts for beginners, because they are fast and forgiving. Alfalfa is ready in three to four days, lentils in two to three, and mung beans in three to five. Start with one jar and one seed, learn the rinse-and-drain routine, and add a second crop once the first jar succeeds.

Why do my sprouts smell bad?

Bad-smelling sprouts usually mean standing water, too much seed, or a missed rinse. Drain the jar fully after every rinse, rest it at an angle, and use less seed next time. A sour smell means bacteria are growing, so throw the batch away, wash the jar in hot soapy water, and start again.

How long do sprouts keep in the fridge?

Sprouts keep for three to five days in the fridge if you store them dry. Rinse the finished sprouts once more, drain them well, dry them on a clean tea towel, then pack them loosely in a container lined with a paper towel. Keep them in the salad drawer, and throw away any that turn slimy or smell sour.
