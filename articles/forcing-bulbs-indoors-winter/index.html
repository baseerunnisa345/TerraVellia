23 Bulbs to Force Indoors This Winter

Indoor PlantsUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

Gardeners have been forcing bulbs indoors winter after winter, and it is the winter gardener's best trick: it brings spring flowers into the house in December and January, weeks before the garden wakes up. These 23 ideas cover every way of forcing bulbs indoors — paperwhites and amaryllis that need no chilling, hyacinths in a forcing vase, tulips and daffodils in pots, and crocus, muscari and snowdrops on a windowsill — with the chilling hours, watering and feeding, and the bloom time for each bulb. Before you start, check your hardiness zone: in cold zones, forced bulbs go outside only after the last frost, and the aftercare you give them decides whether they ever flower again.

1

Paperwhites in Pebbles and Water

Idea 1 of 23

Paperwhites are the easiest bulbs to force indoors because they need no chilling at all. Fill a shallow bowl with pebbles, set the bulbs on top with their bases touching the water, and keep the water level just below the bulbs. In three to five weeks you will have tall stems and clusters of sweetly scented white flowers. They are perfect for a windowsill in December.

2

Amaryllis in a Pot for Christmas Blooms

Idea 2 of 23

An amaryllis bulb is the surest way to have big flowers for Christmas: plant it in a pot in late November, with the top third of the bulb above the compost, water once, and keep it warm and bright. A thick flower stalk appears in two to three weeks, followed by huge trumpet blooms. Choose a large, firm bulb — the bigger the bulb, the more flowers you get.

3

Hyacinths in a Forcing Vase

Idea 3 of 23

A hyacinth forcing vase is a glass with a narrow waist that holds the bulb just above the water, so you can watch the roots grow down. Fill the vase to just below the bulb, keep it in a cool dark place for six to eight weeks while roots form, then bring it into the light. The scent of a forced hyacinth will fill a whole room.

4

Daffodils Forced in Pots on a Windowsill

Idea 4 of 23

Daffodils force well in pots if you give them their cold first: pot the bulbs in autumn, water them, and leave the pots in a cold garage or shed for twelve weeks. When shoots are five centimetres tall, bring the pots onto a cool windowsill. In two to three weeks you will have cheerful yellow flowers while the garden is still bare. Choose early varieties like February Gold for the fastest results.

5

Crocus in a Shallow Bowl

Idea 5 of 23

Crocus are small, quick and charming forced in a shallow bowl on a table. Pot the corms close together in gritty compost in autumn, keep the bowl cool and dark for ten weeks while roots form, then bring it into a bright, cool room. The flowers open in the sun and close at night, which makes them lovely to watch. They flower for about two weeks.

6

Muscari in a Glass Jar

Idea 6 of 23

Muscari, or grape hyacinths, look wonderful forced in a glass jar where you can see the roots. Set the bulbs on pebbles with water just touching their bases, keep the jar in a cool dark cupboard for eight weeks, then move it to a bright windowsill. The little blue bells open in clusters and smell faintly of grapes. Several jars together make a lovely display.

7

Snowdrops in a Pot Indoors

Idea 7 of 23

Snowdrops can be forced in a pot, though they are slower than paperwhites and need their chill. Plant the bulbs in autumn in humus-rich compost, keep the pot in a cold shed or fridge for twelve to fourteen weeks, then bring it into a cool room. The nodding white flowers open in January, weeks before the garden snowdrops. They hate drying out, so keep the compost just moist.

8

Forcing Bulbs in Water Only

Idea 8 of 23

Some bulbs need no soil at all: hyacinths, paperwhites and muscari all force in water alone. The bulb sits just above the water, and the roots grow down into it. Change the water weekly to keep it fresh and stop it smelling, and keep the water level just below the base of the bulb. Water forcing lets you watch the whole process, roots and all.

9

Forcing Bulbs in Pebbles

Idea 9 of 23

Pebbles are the simplest forcing medium: they hold the bulb upright, let you see the roots, and need no soil. Fill a bowl or glass with clean pebbles, nestle the bulbs in with their bases touching the water, and top the water up as it evaporates. Paperwhites and muscari are the best bulbs for pebbles. The display looks clean and modern, and there is no compost to spill.

10

Forcing Bulbs in Compost

Idea 10 of 23

Compost is the most forgiving forcing medium, and it suits every bulb: tulips, daffodils, hyacinths and crocus all force well in pots of free-draining compost. Plant the bulbs close together — a bulb's width apart — with their tips just below the surface, water once, then give them their cool dark period. Compost holds moisture steadily, so the bulbs rarely dry out.

11

Timing Tulips for Christmas Day

Idea 11 of 23

For tulips on Christmas Day, count backwards: they need twelve to fourteen weeks of chilling, then three to four weeks in the light. Start chilling in early September, pot them up in early December, and bring them into a cool room two weeks before Christmas. If they are opening too fast, move them somewhere cooler to slow them down. A cool room keeps the flowers open for two weeks.

12

Timing Hyacinths for January Blooms

Idea 12 of 23

Hyacinths started in October flower in January, which is exactly when the house needs them most. Pot the bulbs in autumn, give them ten to twelve weeks in a cool dark place, then bring them into a bright room in early January. They open slowly in a cool room and fill the house with scent. If you want February flowers instead, start a second pot in November.

13

Paperwhites for a Scented Windowsill

Idea 13 of 23

Paperwhites are the classic scented windowsill bulb: their clusters of white flowers perfume a whole room. Grow them in pebbles and water or in compost, and give them the brightest windowsill you have — weak light makes them flop. A cool windowsill is better than a warm one, because the flowers last longer. They need no chilling, so you can start them in December and still have flowers for Christmas.

14

Amaryllis Aftercare: Keep the Bulb for Next Year

Idea 14 of 23

An amaryllis bulb can flower again next year if you look after it: after the flowers fade, cut the stalk down but leave the leaves, and keep watering and feeding the plant through spring and summer so the bulb rebuilds. In August, stop watering, let the leaves die back, and rest the bulb in a cool dark place for eight weeks. Then repot it and start the whole cycle again.

15

Hyacinth Forcing Vase Care

Idea 15 of 23

A forcing vase needs very little care, but the water level matters: keep it just below the base of the bulb, so the roots reach down but the bulb itself never sits in water. A bulb sitting in water rots. Top the vase up weekly, and change the water if it turns cloudy. Once the flower opens, move the vase to a cool spot and the bloom will last for two weeks or more.

16

Bringing Forced Bulbs into the Light

Idea 16 of 23

The move from dark to light is the most delicate step in forcing: bring the bulbs out gradually, not straight into full sun. Start them in a cool, shaded spot for a few days, then move them to a bright windowsill. Bulbs rushed into strong light and warmth grow leggy and flop. A cool room, about 15 degrees, gives you the sturdiest stems and the longest-lasting flowers.

17

Watering Forced Bulbs Without Rotting Them

Idea 17 of 23

Forced bulbs rot easily if they sit in wet compost, so water little and often, and always let the pot drain. Water the compost, never the bulb, and empty any saucer after half an hour. In the cool dark period the bulbs need very little water — just enough to keep the compost damp. Once they are in flower, check the compost with your finger and water only when the top feels dry.

18

Feeding Forced Bulbs Lightly

Idea 18 of 23

Forced bulbs need very little feeding, because the bulb itself holds most of the food the flower needs. A weak liquid feed — half strength, once a fortnight — while the leaves are growing is enough, and it helps the bulb rebuild for next year. Never feed a bulb that has not rooted yet, and never use full-strength fertiliser, which can burn the roots. Bulbs in water need no feeding at all.

19

Forced Bulbs on a Cool Windowsill

Idea 19 of 23

A cool windowsill is the best place for forced bulbs: bright light keeps the stems short and strong, and cool air makes the flowers last for two weeks instead of a few days. A north-facing or east-facing sill is ideal — a hot south-facing sill in winter sun can cook the flowers. Turn the pot every few days so the stems grow straight instead of leaning towards the light.

20

After Forcing: Plant Bulbs Out in Spring

Idea 20 of 23

Forced bulbs are not finished when the flowers fade: most can be planted out in the garden after the last frost and will flower again in later years. Let the foliage die back naturally so the bulb stores energy, then plant the bulbs out at the right depth. Tulips forced in water rarely recover, but daffodils, crocus and hyacinths forced in compost usually come back strongly.

21

Forcing Bulbs with Children

Idea 21 of 23

Forcing bulbs is a wonderful project with children, because the roots grow visibly in a glass jar and the flowers appear in the darkest weeks of winter. Let a child plant paperwhites in pebbles and water, mark the growth on the jar with a sticker, and measure the stem each week. Paperwhites are best for children — they need no chilling, grow fast, and flower in three to five weeks.

22

Forced Bulbs as Christmas Gifts

Idea 22 of 23

A potted amaryllis or a hyacinth in a forcing vase makes a Christmas gift that keeps growing after the day itself. Pot the bulb in early December, wrap the pot in paper, and add a tag with simple care instructions: keep it cool and bright, water sparingly, and enjoy the flowers. The recipient gets weeks of flowers from a gift that cost very little.

23

Choosing the Best Bulbs for Forcing

Idea 23 of 23

Forcing starts at the shop: choose the largest, firmest bulbs you can find, because a big bulb holds more food and gives a bigger flower. Avoid soft, mouldy or damaged bulbs, and buy bulbs sold as prepared or pre-chilled if you are starting late — they have already had their cold period. Good varieties for forcing include Paperwhite, Amaryllis Apple Blossom, hyacinth Delft Blue and tulip Christmas Dream.

Key Takeaways

  • Paperwhites and amaryllis need no chilling — start them any time in December.
  • Most spring bulbs need 12 to 16 weeks of cold before they will flower.
  • Give forced bulbs a cool, dark period first, so roots grow before leaves.
  • Bring bulbs into the light gradually, and keep them cool for longer blooms.
  • After forcing, plant bulbs out after the last frost and let them recover.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many chilling hours do tulips need to force indoors?

Tulips need about twelve to sixteen weeks of cold, at around 4 degrees, before they will flower indoors — that is roughly 2,000 chilling hours. Start chilling in September for Christmas flowers, or buy prepared bulbs that have already been chilled. Without enough cold, tulips grow leaves but no flowers, so never rush this step.

Can I force bulbs indoors without a fridge?

Yes — paperwhites and amaryllis need no chilling at all, so they force without a fridge. Other bulbs need cold, but a cold garage, shed or unheated porch at 4 to 9 degrees works just as well. If you have no cold spot at all, buy prepared bulbs, which have already been chilled and can go straight into a pot.

Why do my forced paperwhites fall over?

Paperwhites fall over when they grow too fast in warm, dim light, which makes tall, weak stems. Give them the brightest windowsill you have and a cool room, and they will grow shorter and stronger. A useful trick is weak alcohol in the water — one part vodka to seven parts water — once stems are five centimetres tall.

When should I start forcing bulbs for Christmas flowers?

For Christmas flowers, start in September: chill tulips and hyacinths for twelve to fourteen weeks, pot them in early December, and bring them into a cool room two weeks before Christmas. Paperwhites and amaryllis need no chilling, so start them in late November and still have flowers. A cool room slows opening, so you can time the blooms.

Can forced bulbs be planted outside after flowering?

Yes — most forced bulbs can be planted outside after the last frost, once the foliage has died back naturally. Daffodils, crocus, hyacinths and muscari forced in compost usually recover and flower again in later years. Tulips forced in water rarely recover, so compost them. Plant them at the right depth, in the right spot, and leave them alone.

How much water do forcing bulbs need?

Forcing bulbs need very little water: keep compost just damp, never wet, and empty any saucer after half an hour. In the cool dark period, water only enough to stop the compost drying out. In water forcing, keep the level just below the bulb and change it weekly. Too much water rots the bulb — the commonest cause of failure.

Do forced bulbs need feeding?

Forced bulbs need almost no feeding, because the bulb itself stores the food the flower needs. A half-strength liquid feed once a fortnight, while the leaves are growing, is enough — and it helps the bulb rebuild for next year. Never feed a bulb that has not rooted yet, and never use full-strength fertiliser, which can burn the roots.

Why are my forced bulbs blind with no flowers?

Blind bulbs usually mean the chilling was too short, the bulb was too small, or it was brought into warm light before its roots had formed. Next time, give the full twelve to sixteen weeks of cold, keep the bulbs cool and dark until rooted, and let the leaves die back naturally after flowering.
