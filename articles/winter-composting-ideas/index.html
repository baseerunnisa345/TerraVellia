30 Winter Composting Ideas

Seasonal Planting GuidesUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

A compost heap does not stop in winter — it slows down, and how much it slows depends on how cold your garden gets. These 30 ideas cover every part of composting in winter: keeping the microbes warm, feeding the heap when greens are scarce, storing kitchen scraps, and restarting a frozen heap in spring. In cold hardiness zones 3 to 6 the heap may freeze solid for weeks, while in mild zones 8 to 11 it keeps working slowly all winter. Either way, the work you do now decides how much finished compost you have when the last frost passes and spring sowing begins.

1

Why Compost Slows Down in Winter

Idea 1 of 30

Compost is made by microbes, and microbes slow down as the temperature falls. Below about 10 degrees, the bacteria and fungi that break down kitchen scraps and garden waste work at a fraction of their summer speed, and in a hard frost they stop almost completely. A heap that rotted in six weeks in July can sit unchanged all January. Every idea here is about keeping those microbes warm, fed and damp enough to keep working.

2

How Cold Is Too Cold for Microbes

Idea 2 of 30

The microbes in a compost heap are happiest between 40 and 60 degrees, when a heap is hot enough to steam on a cold morning. Below 10 degrees they go quiet, and below freezing they stop, though they do not die — they wait. A compost thermometer tells you where your heap stands. If it is warm in the middle, the microbes are working; if it is as cold as the air, it needs insulation, more material, or a turn.

3

Build a Heap Big Enough to Hold Heat

Idea 3 of 30

A small heap loses heat faster than it makes it, which is why winter heaps need to be big. Aim for at least one cubic metre, because a heap that size has enough mass in the middle to stay warm. Smaller heaps freeze through in a cold snap, while a big heap keeps a warm core. If your heap is small, join two bays together or add a neighbour's leaves.

4

Insulate the Heap with Straw Bales

Idea 4 of 30

Straw bales are the simplest winter insulation for a compost heap, and they cost little. Stack them around the sides of the heap like a wall, leaving the top open for rain or covered with a board. The bales trap the heat the microbes make and keep the frost out, and they also stop the wind stripping warmth away. In spring, the bales themselves can be pulled apart and composted, so nothing is wasted.

5

Cover the Heap with Old Carpet

Idea 5 of 30

An old piece of carpet is a free, heavy blanket for a compost heap, and it works better than a thin plastic sheet. Lay it over the top of the heap with the pile side down, and weigh the edges with bricks so the wind cannot lift it. The carpet holds in heat and keeps the rain off, but it still lets the heap breathe. Use natural-fibre carpet if you can, and avoid foam-backed carpet, which breaks into crumbs.

6

Insulate with Cardboard and Bubble Wrap

Idea 6 of 30

Cardboard and bubble wrap are free insulation that most households already have. Line the inside of a compost bin with flattened cardboard boxes, or wrap the outside of a plastic bin in bubble wrap held on with string. Both materials trap air, which is what insulation really is, and they keep the frost from reaching the heap. Replace the cardboard in spring, when it is damp and starting to rot, by adding it to the heap itself.

7

Site the Heap in Winter Sun

Idea 7 of 30

Where the heap sits matters more in winter than in summer, because the low sun is weak. Put the heap where it catches the most winter sun, ideally facing south, so the sun warms the bin through the day. A heap tucked into a cold, shady corner behind a fence will freeze sooner and thaw later. If you are building a new heap, choose the sunniest spot you have, even if it is less convenient to reach.

8

Shelter the Heap from Cold Winds

Idea 8 of 30

Wind strips heat from a compost heap faster than cold air alone, so shelter is worth more than you think. A fence, a hedge, a wall or a shed all break the wind and let the heap keep its warmth. If the heap stands in an open, windy corner, move it in autumn, or put up a simple windbreak of pallets or straw bales. The shelter also makes the heap pleasanter to visit on a cold morning.

9

Keep Rain and Snow Off the Heap

Idea 9 of 30

A heap that is soaked through in winter goes cold and airless, because water fills the gaps the air should be in. Keep the rain off with a lid, a board, a piece of old carpet or a tarpaulin weighed down with bricks, and make sure the cover slopes so water runs off. Snow is less of a problem, because it insulates, but meltwater soaks in. A covered heap stays damp like a wrung-out sponge, never sodden.

10

Turn the Heap Less Often in Winter

Idea 10 of 30

Turning a heap lets in air, but it also lets out heat, so in winter turn less, not more. A summer heap gets turned every few weeks, but a winter heap is better left alone for two or three months, so the warm core stays intact. Turn it once in midwinter, on a mild day, to mix the cold outer layers into the warm middle. Every turn costs heat, so cover the heap again at once.

11

Hot Composting Through the Cold Months

Idea 11 of 30

Hot composting is the fastest way to make compost in winter, because a hot heap keeps its own microbes working. Build the heap in one go, in layers of greens and browns, with plenty of fresh material and enough moisture, and it will heat up within days. Insulate it well and turn it once, on a mild day, to feed the hot core. A hot heap can finish in six to eight weeks, even in the cold months.

12

Cold Composting: Slow but Steady

Idea 12 of 30

Cold composting is the slow, simple way: add material to the heap as it comes, and let it rot down in its own time. It will not heat up much in winter, and it may take a year to finish, but it still works, and it needs almost no effort. Cold composting suits gardeners who have more leaves and prunings than time. The heap may freeze on the surface, but underneath it keeps breaking down, slowly and steadily.

13

The Right Browns-to-Greens Balance in Winter

Idea 13 of 30

A compost heap needs both browns and greens, and winter makes the balance harder, because greens are scarce. Aim for roughly two or three parts brown to one part green by volume: a bucket of kitchen scraps needs two or three buckets of leaves, cardboard or shredded paper. Too many greens make a cold, slimy heap, and too many browns make a dry heap that does nothing. Add the browns in layers, so every layer of greens is covered.

14

Stockpile Browns Before Winter

Idea 14 of 30

Browns are the fuel of a winter heap, and the time to stockpile them is autumn, when leaves are everywhere. Bag up dry autumn leaves in old compost bags or a wire cage, and keep them dry under cover, so you have browns to add all winter. Shredded cardboard, newspaper and straw are useful too. A heap with a good stockpile of browns beside it never goes slimy, because you can always balance the next bucket of kitchen scraps.

15

Shredded Prunings as Winter Browns

Idea 15 of 30

Winter pruning produces a pile of woody prunings, and shredded, they make excellent browns for the heap. Run them through a shredder, or chop them small with secateurs, and mix them in with the kitchen scraps. Woody material breaks down slowly, which is good in winter, because it keeps the heap open and airy. Do not add long, unshredded branches, which make air pockets and stop the heap heating.

16

Autumn Leaves: Feed the Heap, Not the Bin

Idea 16 of 30

Autumn leaves are the best free compost material there is, and they arrive just when the heap needs them. Rake them up, shred them with a mower if you can, and add them to the heap in layers with kitchen scraps. Whole leaves mat together and go slimy, but shredded leaves rot down fast. Do not put leaves in the green bin — they are browns, and your heap needs them all winter long.

17

Leaf Mould: A Separate Winter Project

Idea 17 of 30

Leaf mould is not compost, but it is just as useful, and winter is the time to start it. Fill a wire cage or a bin bag with holes with damp autumn leaves, and leave it in a shady corner for a year or two. Fungi break the leaves down slowly into dark, crumbly leaf mould, which is perfect for seed compost and mulching. Leaf mould needs no turning and no feeding — just time, damp leaves and patience.

18

Kitchen Scraps All Winter Long

Idea 18 of 30

Kitchen scraps keep coming all winter, and the heap needs them, because they are the greens that keep the microbes fed. Vegetable peelings, fruit cores, tea leaves and coffee grounds all go in, and they bring the nitrogen and moisture the heap needs. Collect them in a kitchen caddy and empty it every few days, so the scraps do not freeze in the caddy. A heap fed through winter is a heap that starts spring warm.

19

What Not to Add in Winter

Idea 19 of 30

Some things should never go in a winter heap, because the cold heap cannot deal with them. Keep out cooked food, meat, fish and dairy, which attract rats and smell when they rot slowly. Keep out diseased plants and weed roots, because a cold heap does not get hot enough to kill them. Keep out glossy paper and treated wood, which add chemicals. When in doubt, leave it out — a cold heap is slow, and mistakes last longer.

20

A Kitchen Caddy That Does Not Smell

Idea 20 of 30

A kitchen caddy is the bridge between the kitchen and the heap, and in winter it needs to work well. Use a caddy with a tight lid and compostable liner, and empty it every two or three days, before the scraps start to smell. Keep it by the back door for a short trip to the heap, and rinse it out after emptying. A caddy that smells gets ignored, and scraps in the bin are scraps the heap never gets.

21

Bokashi: The Winter Bridge for Scraps

Idea 21 of 30

Bokashi is a winter lifesaver, because it ferments kitchen scraps indoors, whatever the weather outside. Fill the sealed bucket with scraps, sprinkle on the bran, and press it down to push the air out. The microbes ferment the scraps in two weeks, and the pickled result can be buried in the garden or added to the heap, where it breaks down fast. Bokashi takes cooked food and meat too, which a cold heap cannot.

22

A Compost Thermometer in Winter

Idea 22 of 30

A compost thermometer is the cheapest way to know what is happening inside your heap. Push the long probe into the middle of the heap, and read the dial: above 40 degrees the heap is working well, between 10 and 40 it is working slowly, and below 10 it is resting. Check it once a week, and use what it tells you — a cold heap needs insulation or more material, and a hot heap needs to be left alone.

23

Check Moisture: Damp Sponge, Not Soggy

Idea 23 of 30

A compost heap should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp, but not dripping. In winter, rain can make a heap too wet, and a dry heap under cover can be too dry. Squeeze a handful: if water drips out, add dry browns and cover the heap; if it feels dry and dusty, add water or wet kitchen scraps. Check the moisture every time you add material, because a heap that is too wet or too dry stops working.

24

A Frozen Heap? Leave It Alone

Idea 24 of 30

A frozen heap looks dead, but it is not — the microbes are only resting, waiting for the thaw. Do not try to force it by turning it in a frost, because turning lets out what little warmth is left. Leave the heap covered and insulated, keep adding scraps to the top if you can, and wait. When the thaw comes, the microbes wake up and start work again, and the heap will be warm within days.

25

Wormeries in Winter: Insulate and Shelter

Idea 25 of 30

Worms are more delicate than microbes, and a wormery needs help in winter. Move it into a shed, a garage or a sheltered corner, and wrap it in old carpet, bubble wrap or straw, so the worms stay above freezing. Feed them less in winter, because they eat slowly when it is cold, and keep the bedding damp. Worms that survive winter will breed fast in spring, and your wormery will be full again by May.

26

A Compost Tumbler for Small Gardens

Idea 26 of 30

A compost tumbler is a sealed drum on a frame, and it suits a small garden in winter, because it is easy to insulate and turn. Fill it with a mix of greens and browns, give it a spin every few days, and the sealed drum holds the heat in. Wrap it in bubble wrap or old carpet for extra insulation. A tumbler will not freeze as fast as an open heap, and it keeps rats out too.

27

Composting on a Balcony in Winter

Idea 27 of 30

A balcony gardener can compost in winter with a small bin, a bokashi bucket or a wormery, all of which fit in a corner. Use a lidded bin with air holes, feed it with kitchen scraps and shredded cardboard, and keep it sheltered from the wind. The small volume means it will work slowly, but it will still make compost. Empty the finished compost onto pots and planters in spring, and start again.

28

Use Finished Compost as Winter Mulch

Idea 28 of 30

Finished compost is the best winter mulch there is, and spreading it now protects the soil and feeds it. Lay a thick layer, five to eight centimetres deep, around shrubs, roses and fruit bushes, keeping it clear of the stems. The mulch stops the soil freezing so hard, keeps the rain from washing nutrients away, and slowly feeds the soil as worms pull it down. It is the simplest use of a year's composting.

29

Top-Dress Bare Beds Before Spring

Idea 29 of 30

Bare vegetable beds are the best place for finished compost in late winter, because they will be planted soon. Spread a layer over the empty bed in February, and let the worms and the rain work it in before sowing. The compost feeds the soil, improves its structure, and gives seedlings a good start. Do not dig it in deeply — a top-dressing is enough, and digging disturbs the soil life that winter has protected.

30

The Spring Restart: Wake the Heap Up

Idea 30 of 30

When the last frost passes, the heap is ready for a restart, and a good turn is the way to do it. Fork the whole heap over, mixing the cold outer layers into the warm middle, and add a bucket of fresh greens and water if it is dry. Within days the heap should heat up again, and the thermometer will climb. The spring restart turns a winter heap into summer compost, ready for the growing season.

Key Takeaways

  • Compost slows in winter because microbes slow below 10 degrees — warmth, not turning, is what the heap needs most.
  • Insulate the heap and keep it covered: straw bales, old carpet and a lid hold the heat in.
  • Feed the heap with browns all winter — stockpile leaves and shredded prunings before the cold arrives.
  • Kitchen scraps keep coming in winter: use a caddy, bokashi or a wormery to bridge the gap.
  • A frozen heap is not a dead heap — leave it alone and restart it in spring with a good turn.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does composting work in winter?

Yes, composting works in winter, but it works more slowly. Microbes slow down below 10 degrees and stop in a hard frost, so a heap that finished in six weeks in summer may take six months in winter. Insulate the heap, keep feeding it, and cover it against rain, and it will keep working, even if slowly.

Will my compost heap freeze solid?

A small heap can freeze solid in a hard frost, but a heap of at least one cubic metre usually keeps a warm core. Insulation, a cover and a sunny, sheltered site all help. A frozen heap is not dead — the microbes rest and wake up when the thaw comes, so leave it alone and keep it covered.

Should I turn my compost heap in winter?

Turn it less in winter than in summer, because every turn lets out heat. Leave the heap alone for two or three months, then turn it once on a mild day, to mix the cold outer layers into the warm middle. Cover it again at once, so the heat stays in and the microbes can get back to work.

What can I add to the compost heap in winter?

Add kitchen scraps, vegetable peelings, fruit cores, tea leaves and coffee grounds, which are greens, and balance them with browns: dry leaves, shredded cardboard, straw and shredded prunings. Keep out cooked food, meat, dairy, diseased plants and weed roots, because a cold heap cannot break them down safely.

How do I keep a compost heap warm in winter?

Make the heap big, at least one cubic metre, and insulate it with straw bales, old carpet, cardboard or bubble wrap. Site it in winter sun and shelter it from the wind, and keep a lid on it. Feed it with a good mix of greens and browns, and check the temperature with a compost thermometer.

Can I compost on a balcony in winter?

Yes, a balcony gardener can compost in winter with a small lidded bin, a bokashi bucket or a wormery. Feed it with kitchen scraps and shredded cardboard, keep it sheltered from the wind, and accept that it will work slowly. The finished compost goes onto pots and planters in spring, and the bin starts again.

What is bokashi, and does it work in winter?

Bokashi is a way of fermenting kitchen scraps indoors in a sealed bucket, using special bran. It works in any weather, because it happens indoors, and it takes cooked food and meat that a cold heap cannot. After two weeks, bury the fermented scraps in the garden or add them to the heap, where they break down fast.

When will my winter compost be ready to use?

Winter compost is usually ready in spring or early summer, depending on how cold the winter was. A hot heap built in autumn can finish in six to eight weeks, while a cold heap may take a year. It is ready when it is dark, crumbly and smells like soil, with no recognisable scraps left in it.
