29 Spring Bulbs to Plant in Winter

Flower BedsUpdated November 2026By TerraVellia Editors

There is no better winter job than planting spring bulbs: a few hours in the cold garden now buys you months of colour from February to June. These 29 ideas cover every way of planting spring bulbs in winter — tulips, daffodils, crocus, hyacinths, alliums, snowdrops and more — in beds, lawns, pots and gravel, with the depth, drainage and timing rules that make them flower. Before you start, check your hardiness zone and your last frost date: the planting window closes by mid-December in cold zones, but stays open into January in zones 7 and 8, and the right bulbs depend on how cold and wet your garden gets.

1

Tulips in a Sunny Border

Idea 1 of 29

Tulips are the heart of the spring border, and winter planting is how you get them. Choose a sunny, sheltered spot with soil that drains freely, and plant the bulbs 15 centimetres deep, pointy end up, about 10 centimetres apart. Tulips actually need the winter cold to flower well, so planting in late November or December is ideal. In heavy clay, add grit to the planting hole so the bulbs never sit in water.

2

Daffodils Naturalised in the Lawn

Idea 2 of 29

Daffodils are the easiest bulbs to naturalise: plant them in drifts through the lawn in winter and they will come back year after year. Scatter the bulbs and plant them where they fall for a natural look, about 15 centimetres deep. The golden rule is to leave the foliage alone after flowering — let it die back naturally for six weeks so the bulb stores energy for next year. Never tie the leaves in knots.

3

Crocus Drifts Along the Path

Idea 3 of 29

Crocus are the first colour of spring, and they look best planted in drifts along a path or at the front of a border. Plant the small corms 8 centimetres deep in groups of ten or more, in sun or light shade, and they will spread a little each year. They flower in February and March, often while snow is still on the ground, and bees love them. Choose early varieties for the earliest flowers.

4

Hyacinths by the Front Door

Idea 4 of 29

Few bulbs smell as good as hyacinths, so plant them where you will walk past them every day — by the front door, beside a path or under a window. They need sun and soil that drains well, and they flower in March and April. Plant the bulbs 10 centimetres deep and 15 centimetres apart, and stake tall varieties if your garden is windy. After flowering, leave the foliage to feed the bulb.

5

Alliums for Early Summer Height

Idea 5 of 29

Alliums bridge spring and summer: their tall purple globes rise above the fading tulips and daffodils in May and June. Plant the big bulbs 15 centimetres deep in a sunny spot with sharp drainage, and they will come back for years. They are superb in gravel gardens and at the back of borders, and bees love them. Leave the seed heads standing — they look wonderful through winter.

6

Muscari Rivers Under Trees

Idea 6 of 29

Muscari, or grape hyacinths, are the best bulbs for planting in rivers of blue under trees and shrubs. They cope with the dry shade at the base of a tree better than most bulbs, and they multiply freely. Plant the small bulbs 8 centimetres deep in drifts in winter, and in March and April you will have a blue carpet. They look lovely with daffodils and tulips planted through them.

7

Snowdrops in the Green

Idea 7 of 29

Snowdrops flower in January and February, and the best way to plant them is in the green — bought as growing plants just after flowering, rather than as dry bulbs. Plant them in winter in humus-rich soil in shade or part shade, in drifts of twenty or more, and they will spread into a white carpet. They hate drying out, so plant them quickly and water them in well.

8

Bluebells for a Shady Corner

Idea 8 of 29

English bluebells are the loveliest bulbs for shade, and they naturalise beautifully in a shady corner under trees. Plant the bulbs 10 centimetres deep in winter, in leafy, humus-rich soil that stays moist in summer, and leave them completely alone. They take a few years to build into a drift, but once established they return every spring. Buy only nursery-grown bulbs, never wild-collected ones.

9

Iris Reticulata in a Rockery

Idea 9 of 29

Iris reticulata are tiny jewels for the rockery, flowering in February and March in shades of blue, purple and yellow. They need sun and very sharp drainage — plant them 8 centimetres deep in gritty soil or in a raised rockery pocket. They look best in groups of ten or more, tucked between stones where their colour shows. In wet winters they can rot, so drainage matters more than feeding.

10

Fritillaria in Damp Soil

Idea 10 of 29

Snake's head fritillary is the bulb for damp soil: its chequered purple bells love a moist border or the edge of a pond. Plant the bulbs 10 centimetres deep in winter, in soil that stays damp through summer, and they will naturalise if you leave them alone. They flower in April and May, and they are wonderful with primroses and cowslips. They are also one of the few bulbs that dislike dry soil.

11

Anemone Blanda Carpets

Idea 11 of 29

Anemone blanda makes a low carpet of blue, pink or white daisies in March and April, and it is one of the best bulbs for the front of a border. Soak the knobbly tubers overnight before planting, then set them 5 centimetres deep in sun or part shade. They spread slowly into a drift, and they look lovely under shrubs and roses. They are happiest in soil that does not dry out in summer.

12

Chionodoxa for Early Colour

Idea 12 of 29

Chionodoxa, glory of the snow, flowers in February and March, often before the crocus. Its starry pink, blue or white flowers look best planted in drifts at the front of a border or in a lawn, where they will spread a little each year. Plant the small bulbs 8 centimetres deep in sun or part shade, and leave the foliage to die back naturally. It is one of the most reliable early bulbs for cold gardens.

13

Scilla in the Lawn

Idea 13 of 29

Scilla, or squill, is a tough little bulb that naturalises in lawns and under trees, flowering in March with spikes of blue bells. Plant the bulbs 8 centimetres deep in drifts in winter, and mow around them until the foliage has died back — the leaves need six weeks to feed the bulb for next year. They multiply freely, so a small planting soon becomes a blue haze across the grass.

14

Camassia in a Damp Border

Idea 14 of 29

Camassia is a tall, elegant bulb for damp soil, with spikes of starry blue or white flowers in May and June. It needs soil that stays moist through summer, so plant it in a damp border or near a pond, 15 centimetres deep, in sun or part shade. It naturalises well and looks wonderful with irises and moisture-loving perennials. In dry gardens it sulks, so give it the wettest spot you have.

15

Winter Aconites for the First Flowers

Idea 15 of 29

Winter aconites are often the very first flowers of the year, opening their yellow cups in January and February. They need humus-rich soil in shade or part shade, and they hate drying out — plant the tubers in winter, soaked overnight, about 5 centimetres deep. They take a year or two to settle, then spread into a golden carpet under trees and shrubs. Buy them in the green for the most reliable results.

16

Lasagne Layering in Pots

Idea 16 of 29

Lasagne planting packs three layers of bulbs into one pot for months of flowers: late tulips at the bottom, daffodils in the middle, crocus or muscari on top. Use a deep pot with drainage holes, fill it with compost, and plant each layer about 5 centimetres apart, finishing with the smallest bulbs near the surface. Water once, then leave the pot outside — the bulbs need winter cold to flower.

17

Tulips in Pots for the Patio

Idea 17 of 29

Tulips in pots are the easiest way to bring spring colour to a patio, and they are simple to plant in winter. Fill a deep pot with free-draining compost, plant the bulbs 15 centimetres deep and close together — a bulb's width apart — and water once. Keep the pot outside in the cold, and move it into the sun when shoots appear. After flowering, plant the bulbs out in the garden.

18

Daffodils in Deep Containers

Idea 18 of 29

Daffodils grow well in deep containers if you give them room: use a pot at least 30 centimetres deep, with drainage holes and free-draining compost. Plant the bulbs 15 centimetres deep, water once, and leave the pot outside all winter — daffodils need the cold to flower. In spring, feed them after flowering and let the foliage die back before you empty the pot.

19

Hyacinths in a Window Box

Idea 19 of 29

A window box of hyacinths brings scent and colour right up to the house in March and April. Use a deep box with drainage holes, plant the bulbs 10 centimetres deep in free-draining compost, and keep the box outside in the cold all winter. Choose one colour per box for the boldest effect, or mix pink, blue and white. After flowering, plant the bulbs out in the garden to flower again next year.

20

Planting Depth: Three Times the Bulb

Idea 20 of 29

The one rule that governs every bulb is depth: plant each bulb three times as deep as the bulb is tall. A tulip bulb 5 centimetres tall goes 15 centimetres down; a crocus corm 3 centimetres tall goes 8 centimetres down. Planted too shallow, bulbs dry out, freeze, and topple over in flower; planted too deep, they may never appear. Measure with a trowel or a dibber marked with the depth.

21

Pointy End Up, Every Time

Idea 21 of 29

Every bulb has a right way up, and it matters: plant it pointy end up, flat base down. The point is where the shoot grows, and the flat base is where the roots form. A bulb planted upside down will still flower — it wastes energy turning itself around — but it flowers later and weaker. If you cannot tell which end is which, plant the bulb on its side and it will right itself.

22

Plant Before the Ground Freezes

Idea 22 of 29

The planting window for spring bulbs runs from late autumn until the ground freezes, and in mild winters that means well into December. The soil must be workable — if your spade bounces off frozen ground, it is too late for beds, though you can still plant in pots of fresh compost kept in a sheltered spot. Earlier is better for rooting, but a bulb planted in December still flowers in spring.

23

Mark Where You Planted

Idea 23 of 29

It is easy to forget where bulbs are buried, and a spade through a clump of tulips in January is a sad accident. Mark every planting with a wooden label, a short cane or a ring of stones, and note the variety on the label. Markers also stop you planting annuals on top of bulbs in spring, and they remind you where to leave the foliage alone after flowering.

24

Feed Bulbs at Planting Time

Idea 24 of 29

A spoonful of bone meal in the planting hole gives bulbs the phosphorus they need for strong roots and flowers, and it lasts all season. Sprinkle it under the bulb, not on it, and mix it into the soil so it does not burn the roots. Do not use fresh manure — it rots bulbs — and do not feed again until the shoots appear in spring. Good soil and good drainage matter more than heavy feeding.

25

Mulch Bulbs After Planting

Idea 25 of 29

A layer of mulch over newly planted bulbs does two jobs: it keeps the soil temperature steady through winter, and it keeps the soil moist in spring. Spread 5 centimetres of compost, leaf mould or bark over the bed after planting, keeping it off the stems of any plants already there. In cold zones, mulch also stops the ground freezing hard, which helps bulbs planted late.

26

Protect New Bulbs from Squirrels

Idea 26 of 29

Squirrels love freshly planted bulbs, especially tulips and crocus, and they will dig them up within days. The surest protection is a sheet of chicken wire laid over the bed and pegged down, or wire mesh buried a few centimetres under the surface. Chilli flakes and holly leaves help a little, but mesh is the only reliable answer. Remove the wire in spring when the shoots are safely through.

27

Bulbs in a Gravel Garden

Idea 27 of 29

Gravel gardens are ideal for bulbs: the gravel drains fast, warms up early in spring, and sets off the flowers beautifully. Plant tulips, alliums, crocus and iris in pockets of soil between the stones, 10 to 15 centimetres deep, and top up the gravel after planting. The bulbs love the dry summer conditions under gravel, and they come back reliably year after year with almost no work.

28

Bulbs Between Shrubs

Idea 28 of 29

The ground under shrubs is often bare in winter, and it is perfect for bulbs: plant daffodils, snowdrops, bluebells and anemones in drifts between the shrubs, where they get light before the leaves open in spring. Plant them in winter while the shrubs are dormant and you can see the ground, and leave them alone — they will naturalise and flower every year as the shrubs leaf up above them.

29

Succession Planting for Six Weeks of Colour

Idea 29 of 29

One planting of tulips flowers for two weeks; three plantings flower for six. Choose early, mid-season and late varieties of the same bulb, and plant them together in winter — early tulips in March, mid-season in April, late doubles in May. The same trick works with daffodils and hyacinths, and it turns a short burst of colour into a whole season of flowers from one afternoon's planting.

Key Takeaways

  • Plant in late autumn and winter, before the ground freezes solid.
  • Depth is three times the bulb's height — pointy end up, every time.
  • Drainage matters more than feeding: bulbs rot in wet soil.
  • Layer bulbs in pots and stagger varieties for six weeks of colour.
  • Mark your plantings and let the foliage die back naturally.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is it too late to plant spring bulbs?

It is too late when the ground is frozen solid and you cannot get a spade in — in cold zones that is mid-December, in mild zones 7 and 8 it can be January. Bulbs planted in December still flower, though they root less well. If the ground is frozen, plant in pots of compost in a sheltered spot instead.

What happens if I plant bulbs too shallow?

Shallow bulbs dry out, freeze, and topple over in flower, and squirrels find them easily. Plant every bulb three times as deep as it is tall — a tulip 15 centimetres down, a crocus 8 centimetres. If you have planted too shallow, add a mulch to insulate them, and next year plant deeper. Bulbs at the right depth flower stronger.

Do bulbs need watering after planting in winter?

Water bulbs once after planting, to settle the soil around them and start rooting, then leave them alone — winter rain is enough. Only water again if the winter is very dry, and never water frozen or waterlogged soil. Pots need more attention than beds: check them in dry spells, because compost in pots dries out faster than garden soil.

Can I plant spring bulbs in pots in winter?

Yes — pots are ideal for winter bulb planting, and they let you move the colour where you want it in spring. Use a deep pot with drainage holes, free-draining compost and bulbs planted three times their height deep. Keep the pot outside in the cold winter, because bulbs need chilling to flower, and water if the compost dries out.

How deep should I plant tulips and daffodils?

Plant both about 15 centimetres deep, which is three times the height of the bulb, with the pointy end up. Smaller bulbs go shallower: crocus and muscari 8 centimetres, snowdrops and anemones 5 centimetres. In light, sandy soil you can plant a little deeper; in heavy clay, plant a little shallower and add grit underneath for drainage.

Why did my bulbs come up blind with no flowers?

Blind bulbs mean the bulb was too small, planted too shallow, overcrowded, or the foliage was cut off too early the year before. Leaves need six weeks after flowering to feed the bulb for next year — cutting them off starves it. Lift crowded clumps after flowering, divide them, and replant the bulbs at the right depth in fresh soil.

Should I feed bulbs when I plant them?

Yes — a spoonful of bone meal in the planting hole gives bulbs the phosphorus they need for roots and flowers. Mix it into the soil under the bulb so it does not burn the roots. Never use fresh manure, which rots bulbs, and do not feed again until shoots appear in spring.

Can I plant bulbs in frozen ground?

No — you cannot plant in frozen ground, and forcing a bulb into a frozen hole damages it. Wait for a thaw, and plant then; bulbs planted in late December still flower in spring. If the ground stays frozen, plant in pots of fresh compost instead, kept in a cold shed or against a sheltered wall until the ground thaws.
